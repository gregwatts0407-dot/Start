## 1. The Seventh

I said that I would not offer you a note on the author of the last account. Here she is instead.

My name is Wren Garrow. I am thirty-nine. On the night of the twenty-second of October, 1994, I was nine years and nine months old, and I was in Compline House from a little after nine in the morning until ten minutes to four the next. Nobody was supposed to know that. For thirty years almost nobody did.

You have met me several times already. I was the voice in the dumb waiter that Mr Pargeter was so polite to. I was the white face at the corner of the stable. I left the drawing on the nursery floor, and the footprints with stars in them. Hester Quaile heard me sing, and cry out, and she put her hand in the place where I had been lying and found it warm. Leopold Vane saw me in the yard with the rain on me and took me for his daughter. He decided later that I was a kind of fixed point, put there so that somebody should see. He was nearer than he knew.

I was the child that Tom Garrow left at a cottage door with an old woman, and made promise not to go on the road. I am the one who set down his telling. He was my father.

And I was the small white light at the orchard gate, low down, that Imogen waved to from the back of a horse. She thought it was her brother holding a candle. It was a red plastic torch from a gas station in Lethbridge, and I was holding it. I saw her wave. I waved back. I thought she was telling me to come on.

So that is who has been talking to you between the parts. I am sorry to have kept it back. I did not do it to be clever. I wanted you to read the six as I had to read them, without knowing what the seventh person knew. If I had told you at the beginning that there was a child in the house, you would have gone through every page looking for her, and you would not have listened to them.

They deserved to be listened to. Each of them carried that night alone for the rest of their lives, and each of them was wrong about what it was.

* * *

I should tell you what I do, because it has a bearing, and because I promised the old man's ghost that I would explain my professional interest.

I work at the National Physical Laboratory, at Teddington, in the group that keeps the time. That is meant literally. There is a room there with caesium fountain clocks in it, and hydrogen masers, and between them they generate the United Kingdom's time scale. It is sent out by radio from a transmitter in Cumbria, and down fibre, and it is what your telephone agrees with. If you have ever heard the pips, you have heard a thing that my colleagues and I are responsible for.

The clocks I look after would lose or gain a second in something like a hundred and fifty million years. I mention the figure because Leopold Vane would have wanted to know it. I think he would have taken his glass out of his eye and been silent for some time.

I did not choose the work by accident. I knew that at nineteen, when I chose it, though I could not then have said why. I had been a child in a house with a hundred and eleven clocks in it, all an hour out, on a night when everything depended on what time it was and nobody could agree. I wanted to spend my life in a place where they could.

I will tell you one thing about my trade that the old man would have liked. He believed that an hour was taken from the country every March and given back every October, and that in between it was held somewhere on deposit. He was told that this was a manner of speaking.

It is a manner of speaking. The time scale that I help to keep does not change in March or October. It is called Coordinated Universal Time, and it has no summer. It runs straight on, second after second, through the night the clocks go back, and nothing whatever happens to it. What changes is a label. At one o'clock by my clocks on the last Sunday morning of October, a rule comes into force which says that the civil time of this country is no longer to be reckoned as my time plus one hour, but as my time exactly.

So Leopold Vane had it precisely right about his own house, and I should like that recorded by somebody who is qualified to say so. Compline kept Greenwich time the year round. It kept, to within the error of a turret clock, the same time as the caesium fountains. It was the rest of England that went away in the spring and came back. On that one night in October, at the instant he opened his door, the country's clocks rejoined his. He was the only man in Essex who was already there.

He was wrong that an hour came in at the door. Nothing came in. But I have stood in the room at Teddington at one in the morning on that night, watching the count go by, and I have thought of fifty-odd million people who are at that moment being handed sixty minutes they did not have. They are asleep. He was right about that as well.

* * *

Now I have to tell you why I said nothing for thirty years. It is the part of this that I am least able to forgive, and I have had to forgive six other people a good deal in the course of putting this book together.

I shall give it in the order that it happened to me.

I was nine. My mother had been dead two years. I had one parent, and I had watched what it was to lose the other, a day at a time, for eight months. I do not think that I can make anyone understand who has not been that child what my father was to me. He was not only the person I loved. He was the only thing between me and nothing.

On the Friday he had taken me by the shoulders, which he never did, and made me promise that I would not set foot on the Wade. *Not if the Queen of England was to call you from the other end.* I had promised. I had said the words back.

And on the Saturday night I heard his voice in the stable yard at Compline, forty feet below me, shouting a man's name. I heard what he asked. I heard the man laugh at him. Then I heard my father's gun.

I knew that gun. I had watched him clean it at the kitchen table that afternoon—no. I had not. I was in the nursery at Compline that afternoon. I knew it because it was my grandfather's, and had stood in the corner of the kitchen at Saltcote, and I had been told not to touch it. And I knew my father's voice as I knew my own heartbeat.

The man beside me in the clock room went backward into my arms with blood coming out of his neck.

That man was dead thirty-six hours later. He was found on a sandbank. I heard them talking about it in the kitchen at Saltcote, my father and the lady who had come on the horse, in low voices, when they thought I was asleep on the settle. I heard the word *pellets*. I heard my father say, *They'll come for me, Imogen.* I heard her say, *They will not. I've told them where you were.*

I was nine years old and I had been reading detective stories for a year. I knew what it meant when a man has been shot at and is afterward found dead. I knew what they did to the person who had fired. I had it, I am ashamed to say, from a book in which it was done with a rope.

I believed that if anyone found out my father had been on that island with a gun, they would take him away and hang him. And there was exactly one person in the world who could put him there. It was not the lady. She had only seen the empty cartridges. It was me. I had been in the tower, and seen the flash in the yard, and tied my handkerchief round the place where the shot went in.

That is the first reason. I kept my mouth shut to save my father's life. I would do it again.

The second reason was Mrs Pask.

She found me at seven that morning, in bed, at her cottage, with my hair wet. I will tell you about that in its place. She sat on the edge of the bed. She was grey in the face; she had been all night looking for me in a house with a sea round it. She took hold of my two wrists, and she said:

"You were never there. Do you hear me, Wren Garrow? You were never in that house. You were here, with Vi, all night, in this bed. If they find I took you over, I'll lose my place, at my age, and your father'll never speak to me again; and that's the least of it. There's been something done in that house last night that I don't know the half of. There's a policeman been. If you say one word about where you were, they'll have you in a room with a lady in a hat, asking you questions, and then they'll have your dad. And they'll take you off him. They do that. They'd say he wasn't fit, a man on his own, leaving a child. They'd put you in a home."

She did not mean to be cruel. She was a good woman, and frightened, and she loved me. She had no idea what I had seen. She thought that I had been wandering in the passages and had heard a quarrel.

"Swear," she said.

I swore. I swore on my mother.

So I had made two promises that week, and broken the first in the worst way that it could be broken. I was not going to break the second.

The third reason is the simplest and the worst.

The man had given me a message. He gave it to me at about ten minutes to five in the morning, in the dark, from the water, and he made me say it back to him twice. It was for three people. One of them was his sister. One was my father. The third he corrected himself about; he had already told that one.

I could not deliver it. To say what he had told me, I should have had to say where he told it. I should have had to say, *I was on the Wade. I went on the road. He came in after me.* And then there would have been the questions, and the lady in the hat, and my father.

I told myself that I would do it when I was older. I told myself that when I was twelve, and when I was sixteen. By the time I was old enough to understand that no one was going to hang anybody, I had been not telling for so long that I could not find the place to begin. My father had married Imogen. They were happy, in that low house, with the lamp in the window. I thought, they have put it behind them. I thought, if I speak now, I shall be the one who brings it back.

I did not know what they were each carrying. That is the thing I ask you to believe. I did not know that my father went over that night most evenings of his life, doing a sum about blood. I did not know that Imogen thought her brother had walked into the sea because of what she said to him in a scullery. They never spoke of it to me, or to each other. There were two rooms in that house that neither of them went into, she wrote. I lived in it for nine years and never knew the rooms were there.

Edmund Vane had lived for twenty years with a secret that would have set three people free, and had told no one, because he was ashamed of his own part in it. He told it to a child, on the last night of his life, and asked her to pass it on.

And the child grew up and did the same as he had. For thirty years.

I work with clocks that do not lose a second in the lifetime of a continent. I was half a lifetime late with nine words.

* * *

My father died in December of 2021, in a hospital in Chelmsford. He was seventy. It was his heart.

I have put in his own part what he said to me there. I had gone in every day for three weeks, and for most of that time he could not talk. On the last afternoon but one he was awake, and clear, and he began, without any warning, on the tower.

I had never heard that piece. He had told me the crossing a hundred times, and the ewe, and the Rill. He always stopped when the mare came out of the sea at Saltcote. He had never once said what he believed came after.

He said that he had put the shot in Edmund Vane that took him down to the water. He said that the light Imogen saw at the gate had been low down for a man, and he had thought ever since that Vane was on his knees. He said, *I got the whole of it, that night, everything a man could ask, and I got it with his blood on it.*

He said, *I never asked him the question.*

I was holding his hand. I had held it at nine years old, walking the boundary.

I said, "Dad. He didn't die of that. I know how he died. I was there."

He did not turn his head.

I said it again. I said, "The light at the gate was me. It was my torch. I went on the road. He came in after me and put me up the Perch. He told me what happened to her. She turned back for him, Dad. She'd left. She was coming to Grandpa. She turned back because Edmund had fallen in the Rill."

His eyes were shut. They had given him something an hour before, for the pain. His hand was slack in mine.

I do not know whether he heard any of it. I have told myself that the hearing goes last. A nurse said so. I sat there until it was dark, and said it over, the whole thing, from the nursery to the cottage bed, to a man who was breathing eight or nine times a minute.

He died the next evening without having woken.

So one of the three never had it. He was the one it was first meant for; it was his father who had asked. I shall not get past that. I have had to learn to carry it, as they all learned to carry theirs, and I have this advantage over them, that mine is at least the right weight.

* * *

I could not tell Imogen. Not then. She had just buried him. She was seventy-one, and thin as a rail, and she went about the farm that winter in his coat.

I told her in the March of this year.

She was dying by then, and knew it. It was her lungs. She had moved her bed down into the kitchen at Saltcote, by the range, where she could see the window. She would not go to a hospital. She said that she had seen what they did to Tom.

I had come down from Teddington for the week. On the Tuesday evening she asked me to fetch something from the bottom drawer of her desk. It was a parcel in brown paper. Inside were six paperback books with a rose on the spine, and a typescript.

"I wrote that," she said. "In 1998. Nobody knows. Not even your father knew. I want you to read it. There's a chapter in the typed one that isn't in the book; they made me take it out." She was looking at the lamp on the sill. "I want somebody to know what I said to my brother. I've never told a soul. I think that I killed him, Wren. I think that he went down to the water because of me."

I read it that night, by the range, while she slept. I read the sixth chapter three times. *I hope you never have another night's sleep as long as you live.*

At about four in the morning she woke and saw my face.

"You've read it," she said.

"Yes."

"Well? You needn't be kind. I've had thirty years of people being kind."

I got down on the bricks by her bed. I do not think that I have ever been so frightened, not even on the Wade.

"Immy," I said. "He didn't. It wasn't you. I've got to tell you something, and I should have told you when I was nine, and I'm so sorry."

I told her. I told her all of it. It took until it was light. She did not interrupt me once. She lay with her eyes on the ceiling and her hands on the blanket, and when I came to the Perch, and what he said there about their mother, I saw the tears begin to run back into her hair. She did not make a sound.

When I had finished there was a long silence. The clock on the wall ticked. It was the old painted one.

"She put him up the ladder," Imogen said.

"Yes."

"And he put you."

"Yes."

"And she gave him the ring from the water. With her hand up." She shut her eyes. "I held that ring. He put it in my palm and I said, *give it to him yourself.* He tried to tell me. Twice. *It's what she did, at the end.* And I said, *she drowned, I'll not hear another word.*" Her voice cracked. "Oh, Edmund. Oh, my poor boy."

"He said to tell you that she turned back for him. Not from Amos. Those were the words. He made me say them twice."

"When you were nine."

"When I was nine."

"On a post in the sea, in the dark."

"Yes."

She began to laugh. It was dreadful to hear, with her chest as it was. She laughed and coughed and held on to my arm.

"Thirty years," she said. "Oh, my Lord. We are a family of people who cannot deliver a message. Mother gave Edmund a ring to carry two hundred yards and it took him twenty years. He gave you nine words and it's taken thirty. And Papa waited twenty to tell her the time." She wiped her eyes with her wrist, as she always did. "And I sat behind a vine. And then behind a curtain. And then I wrote it all down in a book with a rose on it and changed the names."

"I thought they'd hang Dad."

"Of course you did. You were a child." She took my face between her hands. Hers were so thin. "I am not angry with you. Do you understand me? I should be the last. I looked back from that horse and saw your torch, and I gave it my brother's name, and waved. You were walking into the sea, nine years old, and I *waved* to you. If Edmund hadn't been standing at that gate—"

She could not finish.

After a while she said, "Does anyone else know?"

"Nobody."

"Tom?"

I told her about the hospital.

She was quiet for a long time. I thought that she had gone to sleep.

"Then you'll have to tell the rest of them," she said.

"The rest of whom?"

"All of them. Everyone who was there." She opened her eyes. They were her mother's eyes; I have seen the portrait. "You've read mine. I've thought for thirty years that I did it, with my tongue. Your father thought he did it with a gun; I always knew that he thought so, though I never let him say. And they're not the only ones. I've had letters."

"What letters?"

"The little nurse wrote to me, in 'ninety-five, from a hospital. She'd had a breakdown. It was a strange letter. She said she had done a thing in the hall that night which she would answer for. She wouldn't say what. Toby Pargeter sent me his ridiculous book, with a page turned down, and wrote on the flyleaf, *I think you should know that it was me.* I never answered him. I thought he meant the pudding." She was breathing with difficulty. "And there was a policeman. Fenn told me before he died. He said the man had gone to his grave believing that he'd helped Celia get away with murder. And Celia. God knows what Celia thinks. She's in Portugal. She sends a card at Christmas with a tram on it."

"Immy, rest."

"I'll rest presently. Listen." She had hold of my wrist. It was my father's grip, at the last, and my grandfather's. "They all think they did it. Don't you see? Every one of us. We each of us had our own piece of that night, and nobody ever laid them side by side. Edmund said it himself, to that policeman, across the dinner table; the man wrote it down. *Everybody in the house has their own idea of who did it, and they're all wrong.*"

"I know. I've read it now."

"Then you know what you've got to do. You're the only one who can. You saw all of it." Her eyes were very bright. "Have them to the house. On the night. In October. Mother's rule. You'll be thirty years late, but that's nothing, for a Vane. Get them round that table and tell them. Not kindly. Exactly. You're good at exactly. Tell each of them what they did, and what it came to. And then tell them what he did."

"I'm not a Vane."

"You're the nearest thing that's left," said Imogen. "And you've got her habit of standing in doorways."

* * *

She died on the eleventh of April. She left me Saltcote, which was hers, and Compline, which had come to her from Kit, who had never wanted it. She left me a horseshoe nail on a ribbon. And she left me a list, in pencil, of names.

I spent the summer finding them.

Some of it was easy. Mr Pargeter's book was on her shelf. Miss Quaile I wrote to, at an address on the Yorkshire coast. She took a week to answer. Then she wrote, in a small clear hand, that she had been waiting thirty years for somebody to ask. She sent me nine exercise books by registered post: three of her own, and six that she had taken down from an old man's dictation and kept, since no one else had wanted them. She put in with them a small red diary and a child's drawing in wax crayon. She said that she thought the drawing must be mine.

It was mine. I had not seen it since I dropped it.

The policeman took me longest. I had only a surname and the word *Leigh*. I found him in the end through Mr Pargeter, who had once bought him a whisky. He had been dead five years. The manager of the nursing home had kept his box. She gave it to me across her desk and said, "He used to type at night. We could hear him. He said he was writing up a case." I read it on the train home. When I got to the last line I had to get out at Shenfield and sit on a bench.

*Edmund Vane was dead at a quarter past one by my watch. I'd swear to it. I've sworn to worse.*

My father's I had already. I had had it since I was ten years old, a piece at a time, on the sea wall. I wrote it out in August, in the kitchen at Saltcote, in the evenings. I did not need notes.

And then I had six piles on a table.

I did to them what a person of my training does. I have told you. The lining paper, the ruler, the tide tables. I had a seventh pile as well, a very small one. It was a school notebook with a spiral binding, the cover gone soft, that had been in a biscuit tin under my bed since 1994. On the front, in capitals, in felt pen, it says THE SECRET OF COMPLINE HOUSE. And underneath: CLUES.

I had never been able to open it. I opened it that August.

It took me eleven weeks to lay the seven together. I had an advantage that nobody else could have had. I knew the answer. But I want to say that knowing the answer is not the same as seeing how it fits. I knew where Edmund Vane had been at five o'clock in the morning. I did not know, until I had Mr Cade's watch in one column and the kitchen clock in another, how he had got there through six people who each believed they had stopped him.

When it was done I looked at the sheet for a long time. It was nine feet of lining paper, pinned to the wall of my stepmother's kitchen. Every statement that any of them had made was on it, in its square.

There was not a lie anywhere. There was not one square that I had to cross out.

I wrote to them all in September. I said who I was. I said that I had something to tell them about the night of the twenty-second of October, 1994, and that I would rather tell them together, in the place, on the anniversary. I said that I knew it was a great deal to ask. I gave the times of the tide.

They came. I am going to tell you about that. But first I have to give you the seventh account, since it is the one they came for. It begins at nine in the morning, in the back of a van, under a sack.

## 5. Slack Water

I waited for her at the corner of the stables with my back to the brick. The wind had dropped. I noticed it then for the first time. It hadn't gone, but the weight had gone out of it, and in the gaps you could hear the sea, which you couldn't before. It was a long steady hiss from every side, like a tap left running in an empty house. The moon was out more than it was in.

I had my watch up to my face about every half minute. I'd got a job with a time on it and that was holding me together. There's nothing like it. You can have your head split and your judgment gone and a kiss on your mouth from a woman who's just told you she's killed her husband, and if you've got to be somewhere by a quarter to two you'll put one foot in front of the other.

She came at twenty to. She'd found a coat, a man's waxed jacket off a peg, far too big, and a pair of gumboots, and she had a torch, a rubber one, with her hand over the glass so that the light came through red between her fingers.

"It's not there," she said.

"What isn't?"

"The key. The ring. The whole ring's gone off the nail." She was out of breath. "And Mrs Pask's not in the kitchen. There's nobody in the kitchen at all. There's a goose carcass in a pan and the back door's standing open."

I thought about that. I couldn't get anywhere with it.

"Where is everybody?"

"In the hall, getting ready. Leo's down. That girl's got him in his chair by the door with a rug. Fenn. Octavia. I saw Imogen on the stairs—" She caught herself. "No. I don't know what I saw on the stairs. I'm seeing things. Somebody in grey."

"Did you put the bottle back?"

"You've got the bottle."

So I had. I didn't remember putting it in my pocket. I said I'd give it her after. I never did. That's one more loose end I can lay at my own door.

"We'll have to break it," I said. "The gate. Is there a crowbar in this place? An axe?"

"In there." She took her hand off the torch and pointed it at the door of the harness room, where I'd spent half an hour on the floor. "There's all sorts in there."

There was. I went in with her torch, stepping over my own blood. I found a thing behind the corn bin about four feet long, iron, with a flat end, which I suppose they'd used for getting the shoes off cart horses. I brought it out. And we went down the track to the spinney, the two of us, at seventeen minutes to two in the morning, to break in on a dead man.

I went in front. She kept the torch on the ground at my feet. The trees dripped. I had the bar over my shoulder like a rifle, and I was working out where to put the point of it, in between the gate and the brick, low, where the hinge was.

I never used it.

The torch came up off the ground and onto the mound, and the arch, and stopped. I heard her breath go in.

At a quarter to two the gate was open and the steps were empty.

* * *

I'm going to go through this bit slowly. I did it slowly at the time, and it's the part I'd have been cross-examined on, if anyone had ever asked.

The gate was standing wide. It had been pushed right back against the brick of the passage, the way you'd open it if you wanted to bring something through. It wasn't forced. I put the torch on the lock. The tongue was drawn back in, and there was a key in the keyhole, on the outside, with a ring hanging from it and half a dozen other keys on the ring. One of them had a twist of red wool round it. That was the one in the lock.

I didn't touch it. I gave Celia Vane the iron bar to hold, and took the torch, and went in.

The crate wasn't on the landing. I'd known that. I went to the edge and shone the light down the steps.

I can see it now. I'd only had the lighter before, and that had shown me a man and not much else. Now I had a proper beam. The steps went down round the curve of the wall, twelve of them, brick, worn hollow in the middle, with green on them. At the bottom there was the straw. There was a pile of sacking, and beyond that the ice, a long grey heap of it under more straw, with the wet running out from under and lying in a sheet across the floor. A meat hook hung from the middle of the dome on a chain. That was all there was. It was a round brick room with ice in it.

There wasn't any man.

I went down. I went down with one hand on the wall, sideways, like a crab, because the steps were slimy and I'd seen what they could do. I stood where he'd been. The straw was pressed flat in the shape of a body. It was as plain as a bed that's been slept in, the shoulders and the hollow for the head. Where the head had been, the straw was dark, and stuck together, and when I put my fingers on it they came away red. It was on the edge of the bottom step, too, a smear the size of my palm, with some hairs in it. Dark hairs going grey.

So I hadn't dreamed him. That was my first thought, and I was glad of it. After the business with the dress I'd begun to wonder.

I shone the torch round. I looked behind the sacking. I looked over the ice, in case, God knows why, in case he'd got up and climbed onto it. I went the whole way round that floor, which took about fifteen seconds. He wasn't there, and the crate wasn't there.

There were other things. I'll list them.

On the third step from the bottom, set down neatly out of the wet, was the stub of a candle in a saucer. It was a kitchen saucer, white, with a blue line round it. The wick was still soft.

In the wet straw by the ice was a spoon. It was a big one, silver, the kind you serve with. I picked it up. It had a crest on the handle.

There was a mark on the straw and across the wet floor, from where he'd lain to the foot of the steps. Something heavy had been pulled along. There were two furrows side by side, about the width apart of a man's heels.

And there were footmarks on the steps themselves, in the green slime, going up. They'd been made by more than one person. One was a big smooth shoe with a leather sole, a man's evening shoe. The other was a flat broad print with a pattern on it, something like a carpet slipper or a galosh.

"Harry." Her voice came down from the top, very small. "Harry, where is he?"

"He's not here."

"What do you mean, he's not *there*?"

"I mean somebody's had him away."

I heard the iron bar go down on the bricks. Then she was on the steps. She came down them faster than I had, in those gumboots, holding the wall. She stood at the bottom beside me and looked at the flattened place in the straw, and at my fingers, which I hadn't wiped.

Then she said, "He's alive."

She turned on me. Her whole face had opened up. "He got up. He's alive. Harry, he got up and walked out of here, he's only—he hit his head, and he was out, and it wore off, or the cold brought him round. He's gone up to the house." She had hold of my lapels. "He's alive!"

I've thought a lot about that minute.

It would be easy now to say she was right and I was a fool. I can only tell you what I was looking at. I was looking at two furrows in wet straw. A man who gets up and walks leaves footprints. He doesn't leave heel-marks, side by side, in a line, like a plough. Those are what you get when a man is taken under the arms from behind and dragged, with his legs trailing. I've seen them on a carpet in Bethnal Green and across a car park in Ilford. And he hadn't unlocked that gate himself, from the inside, with a key that turned only from the outside, and then left the cook's ring hanging in it. And there'd been two people on those steps besides, one in slippers.

"Look at the floor," I said. "Mrs Vane. Look at it."

She looked. I showed her with the torch. I watched her read it. She was a quick woman.

"Somebody carried him," she said slowly.

"Dragged him to the steps, and then carried him up. Two of them. And took the box."

"But then he might—he might still have been—if they came and found him, and he was breathing, they'd have got him out, they'd have—"

"Who would? Who's they?" I wasn't gentle. I hadn't the time. "If somebody in that house had found Edmund Vane at the bottom of these steps with his head broken and breath in him, what would they do? They'd shout the place down. They'd have every lamp on the island out here, and the old man's nurse, and blankets. You've just come from the house. Was anyone shouting? You said they were in the hall getting ready for the party. You don't quietly lift an injured man out of a hole and go back in for a drink." I gave her a second with that. "People who move a body at night without telling anybody, and take a box worth a million with it, aren't doing first aid."

Her hands came off my coat.

"I saw him," I said. "I had a light on his face for a minute by the clock. I'm sorry. I've seen a lot of them. He wasn't breathing and his eyes were fixed. He's been dead since before one, and somebody else knows it besides you and me. And they've had the best part of half an hour's start."

She stood there. The hope went out of her. I've done that to people before. It never gets any nicer to watch.

"Who?" she said.

"I don't know. Whose is this?"

I held up the spoon. She took it and turned it to the torch.

"It's ours. It's one of the Vane spoons, there's a dozen of them, they live in the pantry." She frowned at it. "It's what Mrs Pask uses for—" Then she shook her head. "I don't understand. I don't understand any of it."

Nor did I. I'd got a candle in a kitchen saucer, and the cook's keys, and a serving spoon, and a slipper print. If I'd had my head, I'd have stood still for five minutes and let those four things talk to one another. Every one of them said kitchen. Every one of them said somebody had come down here from the house on an errand, with a spoon in his hand, for God's sake, and found a man on the floor. But I'd settled what kind of night it was hours ago. Somebody was coming for a clock, and a man had died of it. So I saw a clean-up. I saw two people who'd come and got rid of what they couldn't afford to have found. I looked at a pudding spoon and I thought of Ronnie Sears.

It was eleven minutes to two.

"The boat," I said. "It'll be at the Hythe now. If they're Sears's, the ones who did this, that's where they've taken him. And the box. It's the only way off."

"Then let them go. Harry. Let them take him, if he's—let them have all of it. You said yourself, if the box goes to the boat—"

"And they put your husband over the side halfway to Harwich with a length of chain on him, and he's never found, and you spend seven years waiting to be a widow, with every copper in Essex wondering where he went and why. Or they don't bother with the chain. And he comes ashore at Clacton with half a bottle of chloral in him." I started up the steps. "I want to see who they are. That's all. I want to see a face."

"You can't even walk straight."

"I walk better than he does."

It was a rotten thing to say. She took it like a slap. I stopped halfway up and looked back down at her, standing in that brick well in a dead man's jacket with the torchlight coming up under her chin.

"Go back to the house," I said. "Go in by the garden and get that dress dry, and be in the hall when the old man opens his door. Stand where everybody can see you. Whatever happens tonight, you were with twelve people at one o'clock by the house. Do you hear me? You never came out here. You don't know anything about any of it."

"And you?"

"I'll come and tell you first," I said. "Like I promised."

I left her the torch.

* * *

Outside the arch, in the mud, where the torch hadn't been, I nearly went over something. I got the Ronson out and looked. It was a rut. It was a single line cut into the soft ground, a couple of inches deep and as wide as my hand, going away from the ice house and up the track toward the stable yard. Beside it on either side were the same two sets of feet, the evening shoes and the flat slippers.

One wheel. I thought about that for the time it took the flame to burn down. I didn't know of any vehicle with one wheel.

I know now. So do you, probably. You're sitting in a chair with a light on, and you haven't been hit.

I let it go. It went up toward the yard and I was going the other way, along the wall, to the water.

* * *

That walk to the Hythe was about six hundred yards and I'd put it among the worst of my life. The sea wall's a bank fifteen feet high with a path on top a yard wide, and nothing to hold. I'd walked along it at midnight with a gale trying to pick me off and thought that was bad. Now the gale had dropped and it was worse, because I could see. The moon was out, and on my left hand there was the whole of the tide, right up, brimming, as high as it would go. It was not a foot below the top of the stonework in places, and dead flat, and it wasn't moving. I've never seen anything like it. Water's always doing something. That water was just standing there, miles of it, as if it had come as far as it was going and was thinking what to do next.

That's the slack. I hadn't known what the word meant till I saw it. For a few minutes at the top of a big tide the sea stops. It holds its breath. Then it turns round and goes.

And ahead of me, where the wall bent, there was a light.

It was a small white one, low down, steady. As I got nearer I could see what it was fixed to. There was a wooden staging that ran out from the wall on piles, a jetty, maybe thirty feet of it, and it should have stood well clear. That night the sea was up between the planks. Lying against the end of it was a boat. I'd expected something flash, something with chrome. It was an open launch, twenty-odd feet, heavy, the kind they use for oysters, with a stump of a mast and an engine in a box. The light was a lantern on the mast. The engine was turning over, slow, with a noise like a man clearing his throat.

There was one man in her. He was standing in the stern with a line in his hand, holding her in against a post. He wore a long oilskin and a woollen hat.

I'd thought I might find three or four of them there. I'd thought I might find a box being lowered down, and a long shape in a blanket. I had nothing in my hands. I'd left the iron bar in the ice house.

There was one man and an empty boat. I stood on the wall above the jetty and tried to take it in.

He'd seen me. A torch came on in his fist and came up the bank and found my face.

"You're late," he said. "I'd have give you two more minutes." It was a slow thick voice, local, with the vowels all sat on. The torch went down me and up. "Where's the box?"

I walked down onto the jetty. The planks were under an inch of water and it came in over my shoes. I kept walking till I was close enough to see him. He was sixty, or near it. He had a wide flat face that the weather had been at, with white bristle on it and little pale eyes. His hands on the rope were like a pair of gloves stuffed with gravel. He'd handled a lot of boats in a lot of weather, and I didn't think he'd have known Southend if you'd pushed him off the pier.

"You're not Vane," he said.

"No."

"He said a big feller with a gammy leg. He said he'd have a box, heavy, and I was to help him down with it, and take the two of them round to Parkeston by five. Where is he?"

"Who's *he*? Who told you all that?"

"I don't know as that's yours to ask."

"A man called Sears?"

The pale eyes didn't flicker. "I was give a telephone number," he said. "I rung it Thursday, the way I was told. A feller said what I've said. And he give me two hundred pound in the Plough car park yesterday dinnertime in an envelope, and two hundred more to come. I never see who it was from. I don't arst. I been running a boat off this coast for forty-four year, and you live longer not arsting." He spat over the side, neatly, into the still water. "I was told a quarter to one. It's gone that. I'll not lay here after the ebb starts making. She'll be running through that gut like a train in a quarter of an hour, and I'm not having my boat stove on this old staging for four hundred pound, nor yet for four thousand. Is he coming or isn't he?"

"He isn't coming."

"Then where's the box?"

"You haven't got it?"

He stared at me.

"Nobody's been down here? In the last half hour? Two men, maybe. Carrying something."

"There's been nobody down here," the boatman said, "since I come in round the point. Only you. And a heron." He shifted the rope to his other hand. "What d'you mean, have I got it? I come for it. If I'd got it, I'd not be stood here talking to you."

I believed him. He had no reason to lie, for one thing. For another, I could see the whole of his boat. There was a coil of rope in her and a fuel can and a thermos flask.

So it wasn't Sears. It hadn't gone to the boat. Somebody had taken a dead man and a million-pound clock out of that ice house between a quarter past one and a quarter to two, and they hadn't brought either of them here. There was nowhere else on that island to take them. There were ninety acres of it, and the sea all round, standing still.

"What's your name?" I said.

"What's yours?"

"Cade."

That did something. He turned the name over. I watched it going round behind his eyes.

"Cade," he said. "There was a Cade the feller spoke of. In the car park. He said, if you see a London copper name of Cade hanging about over there, you're to tell him Ronnie says he owes him a drink." He looked me up and down again, by the torch, as if I'd changed. "He said you was the one grassed up your own guv'nor."

Nobody knew I was at Compline. Fenn knew, and a man at Pemberton's. That was Wednesday I'd been hired. By Friday dinnertime a man was handing out envelopes in a pub car park at Thorney Staithe and mentioning my name.

I'd like to say I worked out then and there who had told Ronnie Sears. I didn't, not then. I only felt the ground go, a bit, under my wet shoes, the way it does when you learn you've been in somebody else's plans for longer than you've been in your own.

"You tell Ronnie," I said, and I didn't get any further, because the boatman had stopped listening.

He'd put his head up, like a dog. He was looking past me, over my shoulder, back along the wall toward the house.

"What's that, then?" he said.

* * *

I turned round.

From the Hythe you could see the stable block end-on, black against the sky, across the dark hump of the spinney, three or four hundred yards off. The tower stood up out of it. I could make out the pale round of the clock face, and above that the little roof where those two stood.

There was a light up there.

It was right at the top, in among them. A yellow light, a lantern, moving about. I could see it going from one side to the other behind the uprights. It swung. Somebody was up on that tower, at the level of the bell, with a lamp in his hand, and he was swinging it in a slow arc, side to side, out toward the sea. Toward us.

"That's him," said the boatman. "That's the signal. He was to show a light if it was off. Three swings and cover it. That's a hell of a place to show it from. What's he want to go up there for?"

"That's not him."

"Who is it, then?"

I didn't know. I stood there with the water over my shoes and looked at that light going to and fro on top of the tower. I knew Edmund Vane had been dead for an hour. I'd seen him. Somebody else knew his signal. Somebody was standing up there with the wooden man and the wooden woman, in the place where the lamp was to be shown, showing it. I've said I'm not the type to feel things. I felt that. It went right down my back.

The clock began to strike.

You heard the works first, even at that distance, across the water. There was a grinding, and a run of the little bell, the quarters, *ting-tang ting-tang ting-tang ting-tang*. I thought of the wooden man in his dinner jacket, banging away. Then there was a gap. Then the big bell struck once.

One o'clock, by the house. It was two by me. I looked at my watch to be sure, and it was two exactly, the long hand straight up over the crack in the glass. I thought, the old man's opening his door.

The bell hadn't died away when the shots came.

There were two of them. They weren't together. The first one was flat and heavy, a slam, and you felt it in your chest as much as heard it. After it, quick, before the echo was back off the house, came the second. I know a twelve-bore when I hear one. You don't forget it. I'd stood behind a van in Walthamstow once while a man emptied one at us through a letter box.

Up on the tower the lantern jumped. It went up, and sideways, and then it dropped. It fell straight down the front of the tower, turning over, a little yellow spark. It went out before it hit anything.

And something else came down after it.

I only saw it for a second, against the sky, where the cloud was thin. Something long and dark came away from the top of that tower, from under the little roof. It leaned out and it went. It didn't fall the way the lamp had. It turned as it came down, slowly, with its arms out. It dropped behind the roof of the stables, on the far side, where the ground fell away to the creek.

I didn't hear it land. It was too far.

"Christ alive," said the boatman.

I couldn't speak. Above the stables, on the tower, there was one figure left under the roof, on the right-hand side of the bell. It was the big one, the woman. She had her arm up.

* * *

"I'm away," the boatman said. He was already bending to the post. "I don't know what you lot have got going over here and I don't want to know. I never see you. I never come."

"Wait."

"I'll wait for nothing. That's guns." He'd thrown off the line. The launch began to come away from the staging by itself, not fast, and I saw that the water was moving. It had been dead still five minutes before. Now there were little creases in it round the piles. The slack was over. It was going out. "You tell Vane, if you see him, I come and I waited, and that's four hundred pound I'm owed whether or no. And you can tell him the other feller says he'll want a word. Now stand off my rope."

I had a hand on his gunwale. I don't know what I thought I was going to do. I think I wanted to go with him. I think for about one second I wanted more than anything on earth to step down into that boat and be taken round to Harwich with the thermos flask, and never find out what was lying behind the stables.

He didn't argue. He picked something up off the bottom boards and swung it. It was a boathook, six feet of ash with a brass end. It got me across the forearm and then, coming back, on the hip, and I sat down on the jetty in three inches of sea. He wasn't angry about it. He did it the way you'd clear a fender. By the time I'd got up on one knee the launch was ten yards off and turning, with the engine opened up and the lantern coming down off the mast, and he was a shape hunched over the tiller.

I've read a lot of books, since, where the private detective has a fight on a wharf. He takes a couple and gives a couple and somebody goes in the drink. I'd like to have given you one of those. I got hit twice with a pole by an oysterman of sixty who then went home. That's the only fight I had that night. I'd been knocked cold by a pianist before that. I told you at the start that I used to be good.

I got up. My arm was dead to the shoulder. I went back along the wall.

* * *

I ran some of it. I'd not have said I could. I ran along the top of that bank with the tide beginning to slide away on my right hand, and the black gap of the spinney coming up. All the way I was watching the tower. There wasn't any light on it now. The woman stood up there alone against the cloud.

I didn't go through the trees. I kept to the wall, round the outside. The wall bent in behind the stable block, and I remembered from the afternoon that the creek came in there, the fleet they called it, through a brick sluice under the bank, and ran along the back of the buildings.

I came round the bend and I was above it.

It was a ditch in the daytime, I suppose, twenty feet of mud with a trickle down the middle. It was full now. It was a strip of black water as wide as a road, lying between the back wall of the stables and the bank where I stood. The tower went straight up from the far edge of it. And it had begun to run. The whole length of it was sliding along, smooth and quiet, toward the sluice and the sea, with bits of straw and foam on it turning round as they went.

There was something in it.

It was sixty yards up from me, near the foot of the tower, when I first saw it, and I took it for a log. It was long and dark and low in the water, coming down slowly, a little sideways. The moon was behind a cloud. I stood on the bank and waited for it to come on.

The cloud went by. The light came down on the fleet like someone drawing a curtain.

It was a man. He was lying on his face. His arms were out from his sides and his legs were together, straight, and he rode high, with his back out of the water. He had a black jacket on. It had come up round his shoulders with the wet, and I could see the white of a shirt cuff at the end of one sleeve, and the back of a dark head. He came down that creek on the first of the ebb like a man asleep on a lilo. As he passed under where I stood he turned a little, the way the straw was turning, and I saw the white of a collar.

I went down the bank. I went down it on my backside in the mud, and got to the edge with one foot in. I reached. He was twelve feet out. I hadn't a stick or a rope. I put my other foot in and the bottom wasn't there. It just went. I was in to the thigh, and it was like standing in cold porridge with a hand round your ankle. I grabbed the grass and hauled myself back. I've never learned to swim. They don't teach you in Poplar.

So I knelt on the bank and watched him go. I'll put that down plainly, the same as the other.

At two o'clock somebody fired a twelve-bore twice. At a quarter past, a man in a dinner jacket went down the fleet on the ebb, face down, in no hurry.

He went under the bank through the sluice, into the dark, and I heard the water take him on the other side. That was the sea, out there. It was going out, and it wouldn't be back till the afternoon.

I knelt there. I thought, well, there he goes. That's Edmund Vane. They brought him up from the ice house and took him up the tower and put him over, into the one piece of water on the island that'd carry him clean away. I'd seen them do it. I'd seen him fall, from the Hythe, with his arms out. I couldn't think why they'd carried a dead man up forty feet of ladder to do it. I couldn't think who'd shown a lamp, or who'd fired a gun, or what at. None of it would come straight. But I'd seen what I'd seen. An hour ago I'd looked at a dead man in a black jacket at the bottom of some steps, and now I'd looked at a dead man in a black jacket going out to sea. It didn't cross my mind that there could be two of them.

I'd worked it out, you see. A detective is a man who can't leave two things lying next to each other without joining them up.

Behind me, over the stable roof, from the direction of the house, I could hear music.

I thought it was my head. I got up and climbed the bank and listened. It was real. It was thin and scratchy and it came and went on what was left of the wind. It was a waltz.

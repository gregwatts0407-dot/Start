## 1. In Which I Arrive by Sea

Let me say at once, for the benefit of the nervous reader, that nobody died. I am in a position to know, having carried the body.

I put that first because the story I am about to tell has a house on an island in it, and a storm, and a family dinner at which the host rose to accuse his nearest and dearest of a crime. Any reader who has been properly brought up will be feeling for the light switch at this point, and counting the guests. I know the kind of book you mean. My Aunt Hilary reads nothing else. I can only say that I was there, from five in the afternoon until a quarter to six the next morning, and that the worst thing that happened in my sight to any person under that roof was that a clergyman fell asleep. There was also an incident with a wheelbarrow, which I shall come to. Nobody could call it fatal.

It was my mother's idea. Most of the really bad ones were.

I should explain how I stood in the autumn of 1994. I was twenty-nine, and the wine trade had got hold of me in the way that the sea gets hold of some families. I had no gift for it, but all my friends were in it. I had recently been let go by a firm in St James's, quite amicably, after a misunderstanding about a pallet of Sancerre. I was living in two rooms in Fulham on what remained of a legacy from my grandmother. I had, however, an Opportunity. A man I had met at a tasting, a Bulgarian with a beautiful overcoat, was in a position to supply four hundred cases of a red wine from the Thracian valley at a price that would have made a Frenchman weep. All that was needed to secure it was five thousand pounds by the end of the month. I had three hundred and twelve.

I laid this before my mother over lunch. She heard me out. Then she said, "You must go and see your Uncle Leo."

"I haven't got an Uncle Leo."

"Of course you have. Granny's brother. The clock one. He's rolling in it, and he's never done a thing for any of us. He must be nearly dead. He has a party every October for the family and we're always asked and none of us ever goes, because it's in a swamp. I shall write and say you're coming."

"But I don't know him."

"All the better," said my mother. "He'll have nothing against you."

So I went. I think it was Napoleon who said he would rather have a lucky general than a clever one. My mother has always had the same view of sons, and I have been a steady disappointment to her on both counts.

* * *

I had at that time a motor car, an MG Midget in a shade the makers called Flame. It was nineteen years old. I had bought it from a man in the trade who was emigrating, and I have often wondered since whether that was why. It had a number of ways of expressing itself. There was a knock that meant the weather was going to change. There was a smell of hot toffee that came on at about sixty, and one learned to respect it. And there was a habit, in the wet, of simply ceasing. It didn't cough or struggle about it. It stopped, the way a horse stops at a ditch it has thought about, and no amount of language would shift it till it had dried out.

I mention this because it rained all the way from Chelmsford.

My mother's letter had produced a reply, on thick paper, from a Mrs Edmund Vane. It said that they would be delighted, that the road was tidal and I must on no account try to cross before a quarter past five, and that I should bring a dinner jacket and not expect to sleep. I thought that last a hospitable touch at the time.

I will not describe the country. Mr Cade, who was also of the party, and of whom more later, once told me it reminded him of the inside of a filing cabinet, and I can't improve on that. The Midget, for once, had gone like a bird. I reached a village at twenty to five and a sea wall at a quarter to, and from the top of the sea wall I looked down upon my uncle's drive.

It was just coming up out of the sea. There was a road, of a sort, lying on the mud like something dropped, with sticks either side of it, and it went away into the murk toward a lump with trees on. A good deal of water was still lying about on it. Beside the road at the top of the slope was a notice board, which I read with close attention. It said that one should on no account cross within three hours of high water, and that the sea came in faster than one could walk.

There was a card with the times. Road closed, it said, from 8.40 a.m. until 4.12 p.m.

I looked at my watch, which was the Mickey Mouse one, of which also more later. It was ten to five. The road had therefore been open, by the estate's own figures, for thirty-eight minutes. Mrs Vane's quarter past five I put down to a hostess's natural caution. One expects a certain amount of water on a road that has recently been the sea. I let in the clutch with a light heart. It did not occur to me to read the small print at the bottom of the card. It never has. I am told this is how they get you with timeshares.

For those who have not driven on a causeway, I can report that it is very like driving on a road, but that one's opinion of the road keeps changing. For the first two hundred yards I thought it rather jolly. There were birds. For the second two hundred I noticed that the surface was not so much wet as provisional. Then I came to a place about halfway over where the whole thing went down in a dip, to cross a sort of creek by a bridge made of planks, and the dip was not there. Where it should have been there was a sheet of water about the size of a tennis court, with the two rows of sticks marching down into it and out the other side.

I took it at speed. I had been told that one should. The Midget went into that water like a labrador, with a great fan of spray on either side. It found the bridge, more by its luck than my judgment, came out on the far side, went up the slope for about its own length, and ceased.

I knew that silence. I had heard it on the Hammersmith flyover and outside a registry office in Reigate. I sat for a moment with my hands on the wheel. Then I got out, into about eight inches of the North Sea, put up the bonnet, and looked at the engine, which is what one does. I have never known what I was looking for. There was steam coming off it and the smell of toffee.

It was now, by Mickey, three minutes to five. I lit a cigarette and leaned on the wing and looked about me, and it was then that I noticed the creek.

It was going under the plank bridge like an express train. There was foam on it. Bits of weed were going by at the pace of a man running for a bus. I am a Londoner, and the only tide I had ever had to do with was the one at Putney, which is a gentlemanly affair. I looked at that water, and I looked at the sea, which appeared to be on every side of me, and I asked myself a simple question. Was it coming in, or going out?

I had no idea. I don't know how one tells. I stood in it, in a yellow pullover, with the dusk coming down, and all at once the notice board came back to me with terrible clearness. *Faster than you can walk.* I looked at my watch again. I looked at the thirty-eight minutes I had been so pleased with, and wondered for the first time whose minutes they were.

I should like to say that I acted with decision. What I did was walk round the car. I walked round it several times, briskly, as though the trouble might be on the other side. I tried the starter. I spoke to the car quite frankly about its past. I thought of pushing, and discovered what every Midget owner discovers in the end, that it is a very small car until you try.

Beside the bridge there was a wooden hut on four posts, about fifteen feet up, with a ladder. I had taken it for something to do with birds. It now came to me, with the force of revelation, what it was actually for. I splashed across to it and got a foot on the bottom rung.

The bottom rung came off.

It did not break, exactly. It gave a sort of sigh and came away at one end, and hung there by a nail. I looked at it. I looked at the one above, which was the colour of a mushroom and about as firm. I am six foot three and at that period I weighed thirteen stone. It was plain that whoever had built that ladder had had a smaller refugee in mind.

I pushed the rung back into its socket. I don't know why. One does. It seemed rude to leave it. It stayed there, more or less, with the nail sticking out, looking perfectly sound to anybody who didn't put a foot on it. I have had that rung on my conscience since. I once woke in the night thinking of it. But I am getting ahead of myself.

I was standing at the foot of that hut, with the sea over my shoes, composing in my head the paragraph that would appear in the *Essex County Standard*, when I heard an engine.

* * *

It was a tractor. It was coming toward me from the island end, very slowly, pushing a bow wave. A young man was driving it. He was thin and dark and he had a cigarette in the exact centre of his mouth. Behind him, standing on the drawbar with her hands on his shoulders, was a girl with no hair.

I say no hair. She had the kind one sees on a tennis ball. She was wearing army boots and a man's dress shirt and she was laughing so much she could hardly stand. As they came up she pointed at me and shouted something, and the young man took the cigarette out of his mouth.

"Are you the wine one?" he called.

I said I was Toby Pargeter, and that I thought we ought to hurry, because of the tide.

"Kit. This is Saskia. We saw you from the tower." He stopped the tractor and regarded me. "What about the tide?"

"Well. It's coming in. Isn't it?"

"It's going out."

"Oh," I said.

"It's been going out since half past one. You could sit there till nine o'clock tonight and not wet your knees. You've just come twenty minutes before it had finished, that's all. Didn't you read the bottom of the card?" He jumped down, up to his shins, with a rope. "Compline time. An hour behind yours. Grandfather won't have the other sort on the place. Nobody reads it. In the spring they turn up an hour late and we go out for them in the punt. In the autumn they turn up early and sit in the Rill with their bonnets up. He says it teaches people to finish a sentence."

"I thought I was going to be drowned."

"Sass had a bet you'd try the Perch," said Kit. "I owe her a fiver. Did the ladder hold?"

"Not altogether."

"No. Father's been going to see to that since I was at school." He was already under my front bumper. "You'd not have drowned. But another ten minutes sitting in that and you'd have had salt in everything, and then you'd have a garden ornament. Sass, get the jacket off the hook, I want the hook."

The girl reached under the back of the tractor and unhooked a black dinner jacket, of all things, which had been hanging there like a dead rook. She put it on over the shirt. It came down to her knees.

"We've been dressing the Jacks," she said to me, as if that explained it. "This one's spare. Your car's a really horrible colour, did you know?"

"It's called Flame."

"It's called a mistake." She had a wide, gappy grin. I liked her at once, in the way one likes a dog that has just knocked one over.

Kit Vane had got the rope round something. He did it with about two movements, without looking. He had extraordinary hands, like a surgeon's, and he kept the cigarette going throughout. I have never been able to tie anything, including my own tie, without a mirror and a run at it.

"Get in and steer," he said. "Don't brake. Don't do anything clever."

"I never do anything clever."

"No," he said, looking at the Midget. "I can see that."

So I arrived at Compline House backwards. That is to say I was towed, at the end of twenty feet of rope, through the retreating sea, behind a tractor, steering a dead car with the hood down. A girl in a dinner jacket rode in front, facing me, and laughed all the way. The water came in under the doors. I sat in it. By the time we came up off the causeway onto the island my shoes were full and I had a small crab on the passenger seat.

I have told this story to girls I wished to impress. I do not recommend it for the purpose.

* * *

They left me in a stable yard, in the rain, with the car. Kit said somebody would come. He and the girl went off across the gravel toward a tall tower with a clock on it, hand in hand, arguing about the fiver.

I looked about me. It was a long, low, handsome old place, the stables, built round three sides of the yard, and in the middle of the cobbles stood a block of stone with steps cut in it, about the height of a kitchen table, for getting onto horses from. I took careful note of that block. I did not know why at the time. I think some part of one does know. I sat down on it and emptied my shoes.

"That's not a car," said a voice. "That's a roller-skate."

I leapt up. There was a Land Rover in the yard, which had not been there a moment before, and getting out of it backwards, rather as one gets out of a bath, was the largest old lady I had ever seen. She wore a mackintosh that had seen service and a hat with a salmon fly in it. She had a face like a kind horse.

"Octavia Prowse," she said. "You'll be Dorothea's grandson. You've got her legs. She was a great gawk of a gel too; we came out the same year. Why are you wet?"

I told her.

"Nobody reads the bottom of the card," said Dame Octavia Prowse. "You were lucky. You made your mistake on the right side of it. I've said to Leo for twenty years, put it at the top, in red, a foot high. He says a man who can't read a notice to the end deserves what he gets. And then he did lose one that way, and you'd think that would have settled it. But no. More stubborn than ever." She looked at the Midget with real interest. "Does it go?"

"Not at present."

"I had a hunter like that. Shot him." She seized my hand and wrung it. "Well, come along in. You're to have the Blue Room, Celia tells me, which is the one with the damp. Have you come to ask him for money?"

I went the colour of the car.

"Thought so. They all do, the ones who turn up. The ones who don't want money stay at home, very sensibly, and send a card." She set off toward the house at a rolling stride, and I trotted beside her. "I'll give you a tip. Don't ask him tonight till the door's been opened. Before one o'clock he's as tight as a tick; he'll look at you through that glass of his and tell you what your watch cost. After one, it's the Odd Hour, and he'd give you the roof if you put it to him nicely. That's the rule. Nothing counts. I've seen him forgive a man in that hour for running off with—well. Never mind what with. But you'll want to get it in writing, and cash it Monday."

"That's awfully good of you."

"It isn't good of me at all. I like to see somebody get something out of this family. God knows I never did."

She stumped up the steps and in at the door, and I followed, and so entered my uncle's house, and heard the clocks.

I am not going to describe the clocks. Abler pens than mine have had a go. I will say only that there were a great many of them, in the hall alone, and that they were all ticking, and that none of them was ticking at quite the same moment as any other. It was like being inside a typing pool. I stood on the mat and dripped. After a moment I found that I was breathing in time with the nearest one, and trying to stop.

"You get used to it," said Dame Octavia. "Or you go mad. I've never been sure which Leo did."

* * *

The Blue Room was at the top of the main stairs and along a passage. I found it by myself, the household being occupied, as far as I could judge from the smell, with a very large bird.

It was on my way there that the thing happened which, if this were a different kind of book, would have a chapter to itself with a quotation at the top.

I had got to the turn of the stair. I was carrying my suitcase in one hand and my shoes in the other, and I was thinking about a bath. Someone was coming down. I stood aside. And round the corner of the landing, carrying a small tray, came a girl.

I want to be accurate about this. She was not, by the standard of the magazines, a beauty. I have had it pointed out to me. She was small, to begin with; she'd have come up to about my tie-pin. She had sandy sort of hair, pulled back. She was very pale, with those almost colourless eyebrows that go with it. She wore a dark blue frock, absolutely plain, and flat shoes, and no paint of any kind. On the tray she had a brown medicine bottle with a cork in it, a spoon, and a glass of water.

She stopped, because I was in the way. She looked up at me.

She had eyes the colour of the sea off Cornwall on a doubtful day. It was a very thorough look. It started at my wet hair, went down the yellow pullover to my socks, where it paused, and came back up to my face. It took in the shoes in my hand and the crab, which I found afterwards was sitting on my shoulder. I can't say what her opinion was. But I had the distinct sensation of being entered in a book.

"You're dripping on the Jacobean," she said.

It was a low, clear voice, with the north in it. Yorkshire, I learned. From the coast.

I looked down. I was standing on a strip of carpet about four hundred years old, and there was a pool.

"I'm most awfully sorry. I came by sea. I mean, I didn't mean to. The car—"

"There's a towel in the Blue Room. And there's something on your shoulder."

"Is there?"

"It's alive," she said. She said it quite gravely, as one might point out that a man's shoelace was undone. Then she went on down past me with her tray. At the bottom she turned along the hall toward the back of the house without looking round. I stood on the turn of the stair with a crab on me and my mouth open, and I was a changed man.

I have been in love, by a conservative estimate, eleven times. I have married two of them, and am engaged to a third. I say this so that the reader may weigh my words. I had never before, and I have never since, been looked at by a woman in a way that made me want so badly to be a better kind of thing. I didn't want to be more amusing, or richer. I wanted to be the sort of man who was not, at that moment, holding his shoes.

I put the crab out of the landing window. It was all I could think of to do for her.

* * *

The Blue Room was blue. It was also, as advertised, damp. There was a bed with posts, a washstand, a towel, and a small round table by the bed. On the table stood a little brass clock with a handle on top, about the size of a tin of cocoa, ticking away to itself in a fussy sort of manner.

I have gone over what happened next many times. Counsel for the defence would say that the table was unstable, that the light was poor, that the accused was a tall man in a low room in a state of emotional disturbance, trying to take off a wet pullover over his head. All this is true. The fact remains that my elbow came round, inside the pullover, like the boom of a yacht, and I heard something go.

When I got my head out the table was empty.

I looked under the bed. It was a high bed with a valance. The clock had rolled, or bounced, a surprising distance. I could see it right at the back, against the wall, on its side, still ticking. It looked hurt.

I went in after it. It was a tight fit, and dusty, and I had to go in on my stomach. I had just got my fingers to it when I sneezed, and banged my head on a slat. In backing out I put my knee on something that was not the floor.

I shall not forget the noise. I have described it elsewhere as like stepping on a budgerigar, and people have thought that I was straining for effect. I was not. There was a crunch, and then a sort of soft tearing sound, and then a single clear *ping*, and something small and bright shot out from under the valance, crossed the room at about the height of the skirting board, and went behind the washstand.

The ticking had stopped.

I sat on the floor of the Blue Room with what remained in my two hands. I am not mechanical. But it did not take a mechanic to see that this was no longer a clock in any useful sense. The front had come away from the back. There was glass, and a number of little wheels, some of which were still going round, slower and slower. Out of the middle of it a spring had got loose and was uncurling across my knee like a fern in one of those nature films.

I had been in my uncle's house for nineteen minutes. I had come to ask him for five thousand pounds. And I had broken one of his clocks. He was, I had been given to understand, perhaps the greatest living authority on English clockwork, and a man who cared about nothing else on earth.

How good a clock was it? That was the question. I held the bits to the window. It had a name on the dial, in very small letters, which I could not read without my glasses, and my glasses were in the car, under the sea. It might be a nothing. A bedroom clock, for guests, bought by the dozen. Equally it might be the only surviving work of some Huguenot genius, a thing mentioned in books. In that house, how was one to know? They'd all looked ruinous to me.

I will be honest about what I did. I've had nine years to think of a better thing I might have done, and I have thought of several. I gathered up the remains: the case, the dial, the wheels, the fern. I fetched the bit from behind the washstand. And I put the whole lot in the right-hand pocket of my dinner jacket, which was hanging on the door.

I reasoned thus. If I left the pieces in the room, they would be found by whoever turned down the bed, and the hue and cry would go up before I had so much as shaken my uncle's hand. If I put them in my suitcase, I was a thief. But a dinner jacket pocket was neutral ground. I should have the thing about me. And at a suitable moment, I should take my uncle aside and produce it, with a manly word. *I'm afraid, sir, I've had a bit of an accident with your Tompion.* He would respect that. Some time after one o'clock, I thought, when nothing counted.

I had a bath, in rusty water. I dressed. The dinner jacket, which I'd had since I was seventeen, had a tendency to ride up at the wrists. With a third of a clock in the pocket it also hung down on one side and clanked a little when I walked.

At five minutes to six by the house, a number I was already learning to say, I went down the stairs to meet my relations. I had a crunching pocket and Cornish eyes on the brain, and the idea that the evening could hardly get worse.

There was a basin on the hall table. A tall fair woman in a green dress was standing beside it, with her hand out, and she was looking at my wrist.

## 7. In Which I Leave, and What I Left Out

I slept in the hall. I had meant to go up to the Blue Room, but there was a second settle on the far side of the fire from the vicar's, and I sat down on it to take my shoes off and that was the last I knew. I dreamed that I was pushing a clergyman up a flight of steps in a wheelbarrow and that at the top there was a girl with a tray, who said, "On his left side."

I was woken by somebody shaking my foot. It was still dark.

"Mr Toby. Mr Toby. It's gone half past five."

It was Mrs Pask. She had a candle. I sat up, and every clock in the hall told me at once that she was quite right.

I looked at her, and I was shocked. I had left her at ten to one, a strong square woman going up the back stairs with a candle to see to something. She looked now as though she had been ill. Her face had fallen in. Her cap was gone and her grey hair was coming down, and there were cobwebs on her shoulders and on the front of her apron. The hem of her skirt was wet, and there was mud on her galoshes that had not been there before.

"Mrs Pask! Where have you been? I waited. We had to serve the trifle."

"I know. Gail said." She didn't seem to care about the trifle. "I've been looking for something."

"Your keys? I'm most awfully sorry about the keys. I'll go out now and—"

"They're on the nail. Somebody's put them back. I don't know who, and I'm past asking." She sat down, heavily, on the end of my settle. I'd never seen her sit. "I've been in every room in this house, Mr Toby. Every cupboard. I've been in the roof. I've been round that sea wall with a lamp, twice, and down to the boathouse, and I've looked in places I've not set foot in since Miss Imogen was in her cot."

"For what?"

She opened her mouth to tell me. I'm sure of that. I watched her decide against it.

"Something I'd the minding of," she said. "That I'd no business to bring. It'll have gone home. That's what I keep saying. It's a sensible little thing, and it'll have gone home when the water dropped, and I'll find it sat on my step." She twisted her hands in her apron. "You never saw nothing? In the night? Nothing that'd no call to be there?"

I thought of the dumb waiter. I thought of a small white face at the corner of the stable wall. I said, "Only the lift."

"Ah," said Mrs Pask. "Well. That's that, then."

I thought she was talking about a cat. I had heard her calling one. I said, with the idea of comforting her, that cats always came back. She looked at me as if I'd spoken Chinese.

Then she asked the question I had been dreading.

"Did you look in on Mr Edmund? I never got out to him. I'd the basin ready, and then I went up, and I never came down."

"There was no need," I said. "He's up. He was in your kitchen at a quarter past two, sitting on the table, eating your goose. He'd been up the tower. He was as right as rain. He was laughing."

She shut her eyes. She let out a long breath.

"Thank the Lord for that," she said. "That's one, anyhow. I told you he bounced." She got up, with a hand on my shoulder. She was Mrs Pask again, or nearly. "Now. You're wanted. It's the vicar. He's Communion at eight, at Thorney, and he can't be woke, and somebody's to get him there. The road'll be dry in five minutes. Your motor's no use; Kit's had the bonnet up and says the salt's been in it. So you're to take the vicar in his own, and leave it at the vicarage, and get the bus."

"Me? But oughtn't I to say goodbye? I haven't seen my uncle. I haven't seen—" I stopped.

"If it's that nurse you're thinking of, she's sat with Mr Vane in his room with the door locked, and she'll not open it. I tried. She said through the door that he was asleep and she'd thank me to go away." Mrs Pask gave me a look in which there was a good deal of knowledge. "You'll keep. Write to her. She'll have had a night of it, the same as the rest."

* * *

Gail and I loaded the vicar. I shan't describe it. The reader knows the method by now. We put him in the passenger seat of his Morris Minor with a rug round him, and his teeth in his pocket, and he said, "Is it Easter?" and went back to sleep.

It was beginning to get light. There was a yellowish streak in the east, behind the stables. I drove round into the yard. I'd never driven a Morris Minor, and one sits very upright in them, like a child in a high chair.

There was a big Ford standing in the yard with its engine running. The man Cade was at the wheel.

I've said that I intended to make a clean breast of things to somebody. My uncle had refused to hear me, and Edmund had been interrupted. Hester had other things on her mind. And here, by himself, at six in the morning, was the very capable man in person. I felt that Providence had sent him. I stopped the Morris and got out.

He wound his window down. In the grey light he looked dreadful. There was a dressing of sorts on the back of his head, made out of a handkerchief, and one of his arms hung as if it didn't belong to him.

"I say, Mr Cade. Might I have a word? Only I gather you're by way of being a sort of detective. Kit says. And I think I'd better own up to somebody, and I'd rather it was a professional."

He just looked at me. He had the most tired eyes I've ever seen in a human head.

"It was me," I said.

"What was?"

So I told him. The Blue Room, the pullover, the bed. I told him about the noise like a budgerigar. I took the whole sad handful out of my pocket and held it out to him, on my palm, in the dawn. The fern had got tangled with my cuff link.

"I've had it on me all night," I said. "I kept thinking he was looking at me. Do you suppose it was one of the good ones?"

He looked at it. He didn't even use a glass.

"It's a ten-pound carriage clock," said Mr Cade. "They sell them in Woolworth's."

I could have kissed him. He wasn't a man one would kiss, but I could have. I had gone through fourteen hours of torment. I'd nearly confessed at dinner in front of a solicitor. And it was Woolworth's.

"Oh, thank God," I said. "You've no idea."

I put the clock away. I ought then to have gone. But I was in a confessing mood, the way one gets into a swimming mood once one is wet. I had got the small thing off my chest, and there was still the large one.

"There's another thing, actually," I said. "Rather worse. I don't know whether I ought to mention it."

"Then don't," said Mr Cade.

"No. Only I can't find him to apologise, and we're off now, and I shouldn't like him to think I'd just—" I looked at the mounting block. It was ten yards away. It was just a block of stone, with the light coming up on it. There was a dark place on one corner about the size of a penny. "It was the wheel, you see. On the cobbles. They aren't built for it."

The vicar had woken up. He leaned across and sounded the horn. A Morris Minor has a horn like a sheep.

"Write him a letter," said Mr Cade.

I thought that excellent advice. It was a great weight off. One can say things in a letter. I said that I would write to Edmund from town, when he had cooled down. I said cheerio.

I did write. I wrote it on the Sunday night, in Fulham, four pages. I told him about the handle. I posted it on the Monday morning on my way to the bank. It came back to me in December from Mr Fenn's office, unopened, with a compliments slip.

As I got back into the Morris I looked up at the tower. I don't know why. One did look at it. And there was something different, which took me a moment to place. There had been two wooden figures at the top. The woman was there, with a wet shawl on her. The man was gone, the one in the dinner jacket, who'd hit the little bell.

I thought the gale had had him. I remember hoping that it wasn't the good dinner jacket.

* * *

I drove off the island at a quarter to six by Mickey, whom I had fished out of the Font on my way through the hall.

The causeway was dry, or what passes for dry on the bottom of the sea. I took it at the pace of a funeral. I had a clergyman aboard, and I'd lost one car on that road already. I kept my eyes on the surface immediately in front of the bonnet. When we came to the dip, and the plank bridge, I looked at nothing in the world but the planks.

So I didn't look at the hut on legs beside the bridge. I drove past it at eight miles an hour, with my nose on the windscreen, and I didn't turn my head.

I have been told what I should have seen. Mr Cade came over the same road a quarter of an hour behind me, and he did look. I met him again, years later; it was at a funeral, as it happens, and afterwards in a public house in Leigh-on-Sea. He told me that the door of the hut was open and banging. And he said that the ladder had no bottom to it. From about the height of a man's chest down to the ground the rungs were gone, snapped off short, and the breaks were white.

He told me as a thing he had noticed and never been able to place. He had no idea that it would mean anything to me. I didn't tell him. I bought him another whisky and talked about something else.

I had stood at the foot of that ladder at five o'clock on the Saturday afternoon. I'd put my foot on the bottom rung, and it came away in my hand. Then I pushed it back into its socket, with the nail sticking out, because it seemed rude to leave it. I left it looking sound, for anybody who might come along after me and need it.

I don't know who came along. I don't know who was climbing that ladder in the night, or why, or what happened when the rung went. Nobody has ever been able to tell me. The road was under six feet of water from ten till half past five. I have asked myself how anyone could have been out there at all.

But I think about that rung. I said I'd come back to it.

* * *

The rest is quickly told. For nine years it was all there was.

I got the vicar to his church. There were four old ladies waiting in the porch, and a churchwarden, a Mrs Dace, with a face like a hatchet. She came out to the car. She looked at the Reverend Aubrey Stiles, who was asleep with his mouth open, and she said, "Compline?" I said yes. She said, "It was the weathercock last year," and went back and pinned a notice on the door. I read it as I walked to the bus stop. *No service. The Vicar is indisposed.* Somebody had added underneath, in pencil, *(Vanes)*.

On the Monday morning at half past nine I presented myself at my bank in the Fulham Road with a cheque for five thousand pounds. The cashier was a girl of about nineteen. She looked at it for a long time.

"There's no date," she said.

"There's a time."

"We can't take a time."

"It's a very exact one."

She fetched the manager. The manager telephoned to a bank in Colchester, and came back, and was sorry. He said that a Mr Fenn had been on the line to them at one minute past nine. The cheque had been stopped.

I walked home. I wasn't even angry. I had been warned, by an expert. I told the Bulgarian that afternoon that the Thracian valley would have to manage without me, and he took it very well and sold me a watch.

That would be the end of that, in the telling. People like it. It rounds things off. But it isn't quite the end. About three weeks later I had an envelope from Fenn & Ruddock. There was a cheque in it, drawn on the firm's client account, for five thousand pounds. It was correctly dated. There was a letter, of two lines. It said that Mr Vane had asked every morning since the twenty-third whether the young man had got his plums, and had become so distressed at being put off that Mr Fenn had felt obliged to reconsider his position.

I bought the wine. It was just as I'd told my uncle. It tasted of plums and it didn't give you a headache. I sold four hundred cases by Christmas at two pounds ninety-nine, and I ordered a thousand more. It's the only real money I have ever made, and I made it by telling an old man the truth at half past one in the morning. The firm has been called Pargeter & Plum ever since. People assume there was a Mr Plum.

And Hester.

I wrote to her, as Mrs Pask had advised. I wrote four times. She answered the fourth. She said that she had an afternoon free on the following Thursday and would be in Colchester, and that there was a tea shop near the castle.

I took my grandmother's ring. I had had it cleaned. I got there twenty minutes early and sat at a table in the window with a pot of tea, and she came in out of the rain in a grey mackintosh. She looked smaller than I remembered, and thinner. She sat down opposite me and took her gloves off and folded them.

I'm not going to give that conversation. It was the kindest thing anybody has ever done to me, and she did it without smiling once. She let me say all of it. She let me get the box out. And then she told me, very carefully, what she had thought I was asking her, by my uncle's chair, at half past one in the morning.

She had thought that I was asking for her silence. She'd thought I was offering mine. I had told her about Edmund and the stable, and that she mustn't tell; and I had said that whatever had happened that night, the glass and the tray and all of it, she need never say a word to anyone. And then I had said, "Will you?"

"But you said yes," I said.

"I'd have said yes to anything that night that let me stop thinking," said Hester Quaile. "I'm sorry. It was wrong of me. I ought to have listened to what you were asking. I wasn't in a fit state to hear it."

I looked at the ring. I'd been engaged for sixteen days. They were among the happiest of my life, and it turned out she had not been present for any of them.

"I suppose there's no chance," I said. "If I were to ask again. Now that you know what the question is."

She didn't answer at once. She turned her teacup round in the saucer. I shall always be grateful that she thought about it.

"There's a thing I did," she said. "That night. After you spoke to me. I'm not going to tell you what it was. I've thought about it every day since, and I shall go on thinking about it. I can't marry anyone while I'm doing that. It wouldn't be fair. They'd think they had all of me." She looked up. "And I'm not leaving Mr Vane. He's only got me now. I'll stay till he doesn't need anybody."

"And after?"

"I don't know about after."

She put her gloves on. At the door of the tea shop she turned and held out her hand, and I took it. It was quite warm this time.

"You were kind to the vicar," she said. "And you fetched me. I haven't forgotten that. Most people don't fetch anyone."

I did not see her again for seven years. I have since married two other people, and am about to try a third. I was fond of all of them, and I think I'd have been a better husband to each if I hadn't once stood on a staircase with a crab on my shoulder.

* * *

That is the Compline business, as I've told it at a great many dinners.

I end it in the tea shop. I say, "And so I was engaged for sixteen days to a woman who didn't know it, on the strength of a cheque that had no date, having served my great-uncle a clock for his pudding. And the only casualty of the whole night was a ten-pound carriage clock from Woolworth's." People laugh, and somebody passes the port. I am rather good at it by now. I've got the timing of the "voilà."

I said at the beginning that nobody died. I said I was in a position to know.

I'm going to write down the rest. I've never told it. I have decided that a man shouldn't publish a book called *Mostly My Fault* and leave out the one thing that was.

On the Tuesday after I came back from Compline, my mother telephoned. She'd had it from a cousin, who'd had it from Imogen. Edmund was dead. He had not been seen since the small hours of Sunday. He had been found on the Monday afternoon, on a sandbank seven miles down the coast, by a man digging for worms.

I said that it was impossible. I said I had seen him at a quarter past two, in the kitchen, eating goose. He had been laughing.

"Well, he's not laughing now," said my mother. She is not a sentimental woman. "They say he drowned. They say he must have walked into the sea."

There was an inquest in December. I wasn't called. Nobody knew that I had anything to tell. Mrs Pask had sworn me to silence in a loose box, and she kept her own side of it; she told the police that Mr Edmund had been in liquor and that she had last seen him at dinner. I read the report in a cutting that my mother sent me.

I read it standing up, in my hall in Fulham, with my coat on.

The doctor said that Edmund had drowned. There was no doubt of that. But he had found a number of other things, and he went through them in order, and I followed him down the column with my finger. There was a fracture on the left side of the forehead. I knew about that. I had seen it by candlelight at the bottom of twelve steps. There was the drug. I knew about that too. There were some pellets of shot in the neck. He had told me about those himself, on the kitchen table, and laughed.

And then there was a sentence that I can repeat from memory. I've had nine years to learn it.

*A second injury, a large contusion over the occiput, separate from the first and sustained somewhat later.*

I had to look up "occiput." It is the back of the head.

I've told this as a comedy, and so it was. It was a funny night. A man does not carry a pudding into a room and find that it's a clock every day of his life. I have told it for laughs so many times that there are mornings when I very nearly believe that is all it was. I think of him still sitting on that table with a goose bone, saying, "Hallo, it's the pudding."

But I know what a large contusion over the occiput is. I was there. I heard it. It sounded like a melon dropped on a kitchen floor.

He was awake afterwards. I hold on to that. He spoke to me in the yard, and he was sitting up an hour and a half later, eating and talking, in the best spirits I ever saw in him. I've asked two doctors, without saying why. I asked them at dinner, as a general question. They both said the same. A man can take a blow like that and walk about for hours. He can feel perfectly well. He can feel unusually well, they said; rather light, as if a weight had been lifted. And then, quite suddenly, his balance may go, or he gets sleepy, or he doesn't quite know where he is.

*I feel as if somebody had taken a hundredweight off my back,* he said. *I suppose it's concussion.*

Some time after a quarter past two, my cousin Edmund went down to the water. Nobody knows why. Nobody saw him go. I think he went to look at the tide. I think he was dizzy, and that he was dizzy because of me.

Mrs Pask had the barrow. She knew the ground and was taking it round the block, slowly, picking her way. I was twenty-nine, and I felt a fool walking alongside with a box. I said, "Do let me," and took hold of a handle.

I only wanted to help. That's what I said to her at the time, on the cobbles. It's what I have said to myself ever since, and it's true, and it doesn't alter the outcome in the least. I caught the pudding, and I dropped the man. If I'd let the box go and held on to the barrow, he'd have slid out onto his feet, or onto his backside, and sworn at me, and I'd have a broken clock on my conscience and nothing else.

I never told anyone. I never told the man Cade, who would have known what to do with it. I never told Hester in the tea shop, though I had it ready. She said there was a thing she had done that night which she thought about every day, and I very nearly said, so have I. I should like to know what hers was. It cannot have been as bad.

I notice that I began this chapter by saying that I had carried the body. I wrote that sentence a long time ago, for a different purpose. It was the first line of the after-dinner version, and it always got a laugh, because the audience knew that the body was going to sit up and ask for goose. I've left it where it was. It is still true. I didn't know how true when I wrote it.

Nobody died that night in my sight. I can swear to that. When I shut the baize door he was alive.

But it was mostly my fault. I've thought so for nine years. I called the whole book that so that I could put this chapter in it.

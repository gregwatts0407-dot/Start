## IV. The Cry

I shall be quick about the dinner. Others were there who will have noticed more than I did. I sat through it with my hand going to my pocket, and saw very little beyond the two people on either side of me.

One of them was Mr Edmund. The other was no one.

They had put me next to the empty place. I had dined in the kitchen until that night, and had only seen it from across the room. I had not known what it would be to sit beside it. The chair was drawn back a hand's breadth from the table. There was a napkin, folded, and three glasses, and the candlelight stood in them. Mr Vane sat on the further side of it, at the head. Whenever he turned to speak to it he looked through the space where a face would be, and his eyes focused there. I mean that they did not look at the wall behind. They stopped short, at a distance of about two feet, and rested.

It was cold on that side of me. I am aware of what that sounds like. There was a window behind us, and the curtains moved all through dinner in the draught, and I had a bare forearm on the cloth. I do not say that it was anything but the window. I say that my right arm, which was toward Mr Edmund, was warm, and my left was cold to the elbow, and that when the girl leaned between us to set down a plate of soup at that place, I saw her shiver.

On my right he ate little and drank a great deal. He did not speak to me. He had said what he had to say in the library, and I understood that we were to be strangers until the morning. Once his sister, on his other side, said something to him in a low voice, and I heard a name, and I felt him go rigid from the shoulder to the wrist. Once he reached across me for the salt, and the cloth of his sleeve touched my arm, and I felt it in every part of my body. I looked at my plate. I thought that every person at that table must have seen. I think now that only one did, a heavy grey-faced man opposite who had been introduced as from the insurers. He watched us all as a cat watches a wainscot.

The old gentleman's speech I need not give. I knew a part of it before he made it. He had told me on the stairs, a week earlier, that someone was taking his clocks. He said that he had written to his solicitor, and forgotten, and remembered. I had not known that he would say it at his own table. He stood up to do it. I half rose, behind him, to be near if he should fall. His hands were pressed on the cloth, and I could see them from where I was. They did not shake until the very end.

"One of you has been robbing me," he said.

I felt the blood leave my face. I knew that he did not mean me. But I had in my pocket at that moment a thing of his wife's, that I had taken out of his house's one silent clock and kept from him for three days. And I had made a bargain over it that afternoon with his son. Everyone at that table went still when he said it. I think they were each looking into their own pocket. I was looking into mine.

He said that he would know by one o'clock. He said that whoever it was might come to him in the Hour and tell him why. And then his voice changed, and he said that he had not so many hours left that he could afford to waste the odd one, and I saw his hands begin to tremble on the cloth, and I had my arm under his before he was fully down.

"What is this?" he asked me, of his plate. It was a syllabub. I told him so. He turned to the cold place on his right. "Margot, did we order a syllabub?"

He waited for the answer. I watched his face while he listened. And he nodded, and took up his spoon.

I looked at Edmund then. I could not help it. He was not looking at his father, or at the chair. He was looking down the table at the grey-faced man, with a small hard smile. Then, as though he had felt my eyes, he turned his head, and the smile went.

He looked at me for one second. He looked like a man who had been told that the train he is waiting for has been taken off.

* * *

We went into the drawing room. I put Mr Vane in the wing chair by the fire, with the rug, and took the low stool beside him. It was a little before ten by the house. Outside the wind had risen; I could hear it in the chimney, and I listened to it, I confess, with attention. It did not sing. It made the noise that wind makes.

Young Mr Kit Vane played the piano. It was a piece of Schubert, slow, with a tune in it that came round and round. Mr Vane sat with his eyes shut and his hand moving on the rug, very slightly, beating a time that was not quite the music's. His daughter had gone to the window seat and drawn the curtain back. She was looking out toward the Wade, though there can have been nothing to see.

I had met her only the day before. She was a quiet, dark, guarded woman, with a white lock in her hair, and I had liked her, and been afraid of her. The first time she looked at me I had started. She has her mother's eyes. She has them exactly, the same grey and set the same. When she turned them on me in the hall and said, "You're the one who tells my father the truth," I had felt that the portrait had come down off the wall to say it.

At twenty past ten, in the middle of a bar, the lights failed.

The music stopped, and began again. Nobody cried out. They were used to it in that house. Someone said that it was the transformer. I sat in the dark with my hand on the old man's wrist, and felt his pulse go on, slow and regular. All through the house I could hear the clocks. They had not noticed.

The boy finished the piece. In the firelight I saw Mr Vane open his eyes.

"Thank you, Kit," he said. And then, in the same voice: "She has gone up. She always goes up after the Schubert, and takes her shoes off, and comes down for the Hour. Imogen, did you see her go?"

There was a pause. I had my mouth open. I had told him the truth on twenty nights and I was ready to tell it again, before them all.

"Yes, Papa," said Miss Vane from the window, without turning round.

"Was she tired?"

"A little."

I shut my mouth. I do not know why I let it pass. I think it was the way she said it. She did not say it as the others said such things, to keep him quiet. She said it as if she had seen.

Mrs Pask came in with lamps. People moved about. The gentleman from the insurers went out, and I did not see him again that night until much later. Young Mr Vane went out too, abruptly, with a white face. I put more coal on and sat down again.

At a quarter to eleven Edmund came in.

He had been out of doors. His hair was wet, though he had on a dry coat. He went to the tray by the piano and stood with his back to the room for some time. Then he went out again, and I heard his voice in the dining room, and other voices, and a laugh. When he came back, a little after eleven, his wife was with him. She sat down on a sofa at a distance from everyone, very upright. He took a chair on the far side of the hearth from his father and stretched his legs out.

I watched him. I had no right to, and I did. He was not as he had been at dinner. The hardness had gone out of his face. He looked easy, and almost sleepy. He yawned, and apologised to nobody, and yawned again. His eyelids were heavy. He rubbed at his eyes with his thumb and finger like a child kept up too late.

I thought it was the drink. He had been drinking since noon, and I had seen men go that way at the end of it, suddenly loose and mild. I am a nurse. I had sat by that tray in the Admiral's room every night for three weeks and measured out one spoonful. I had seen what the stuff does, to the eyes, and the jaw, and the hands. And I sat ten feet from Edmund Vane for twenty-five minutes, by firelight, and watched the same thing come over him, and did not know it.

At half past eleven he got up. He stood a moment with his hand on the chair-back, as if the floor had moved. He said, "I'm going to have a look at the generator." He said it to the room. His wife's head came up. She looked at him as a person looks who has dropped something down a well and is waiting to hear it land.

He went past me to the door. He did not look at me. He put his hand, in passing, on his father's shoulder. It rested there only an instant, and the old man did not wake.

That was the last time I saw him.

I must be exact about that. I have been told a number of things since by other people, as to where he was after that and what he did. Some of them I believe. But I am writing down only what I saw with my own eyes. I never saw Edmund Vane again after half past eleven that night, when he walked out of the drawing room with his hand trailing off his father's shoulder. I did not see him living, and I was not shown him dead. I saw something at one o'clock. I shall come to it. I do not know what it was.

* * *

It was only a minute or two afterwards that I heard the steps.

They were overhead. The drawing room is under the long gallery. I had sat in that room on other evenings and had heard, now and then, a board give as someone crossed above. That was a slow sound, one person walking. This was light and very quick. It began at one end and came the whole length of the ceiling, a patter, without a pause. I thought it was the sound that a child makes, running in stockinged feet down a long bare floor because it is there.

It stopped at the far end. It stopped just above my head, where the portrait hangs.

I looked round the room. Dame Octavia was asleep. Mr Fenn was nodding over a paper. Mrs Edmund Vane had got up and was standing by the door with her back to us, quite motionless, listening for something of her own. The clergyman had wandered away. None of them had raised their eyes.

But Miss Vane had. She had turned from the window at last. She was looking at the ceiling, and then she looked at me. I saw that she had heard it.

I got up. I went across to her.

"Miss Vane. I must go upstairs for five minutes. Would you sit by your father? He's asleep. He oughtn't to be left."

She did not answer at once. She had the look of a person who has been called back from a great way off. Her face was very white, and her eyes were wide and bright. I thought that she had been crying, or was about to.

"Yes," she said. "Of course. Go."

I ought to have seen that she was not attending. I ought to have waited until she had crossed the room and sat down in my place. I have been over it and over it. But the steps had stopped above the portrait, and I could not have stayed in that room for any consideration.

I took a candle from the mantel and went. As I shut the door I looked back. He was asleep in the wing chair, with the rug to his chin. His daughter was standing where I had left her, with one hand at her throat.

It was a little after half past eleven. I had promised him at six o'clock that I would not leave him. I had kept that for five hours and a half.

* * *

I did not reach the gallery. I had got to the first landing when I heard someone come pounding up behind me, and my name called. It was the tall young man who had had the crab.

"I think I've killed the vicar," he said.

I shall not write out what followed in the library; it was soon over. The old clergyman was in a chair by the fire, deeply unconscious. I took his pulse. I looked at his pupils. They were small, and they did not answer to the candle as they should. I put my face to his mouth, and I knew.

It has a smell. It is sweetish, and sharp, a little like pears that have gone over. One does not mistake it who has uncorked that bottle every night for three weeks. The young man gave me the glass that had been on the floor. I smelled it, and I put a drop on my tongue to be sure. It was brandy, and it was the Mixture.

I asked him where the glass had come from. I heard my own voice ask it, quite low and level.

He said the dining-room sideboard. He said that it had been Mr Edmund's. Mr Edmund had drunk about half of it and made a face and said that it was corked. Then he had left it, and the vicar had not liked to see it wasted.

I want to set down exactly what went through me. It did not come as a thought. It came as it comes on a ward when a bell rings in the night from the wrong bed, and one is running before one has understood.

The bottle was mine. I mean that it was in my charge. It was kept on a tray in the Admiral's room, and nobody else was to touch it. That had been said to me on my first day. I had carried it down myself at twenty past five, to the pantry, and carried it up again, and set it by the bed. I had not looked at it since. I had been at dinner for two hours, and in the drawing room for an hour and a half, and the Admiral's room had stood empty with its door unlocked. Anyone might have gone in.

Someone had gone in. They had taken the Mixture and put it in a glass of brandy, and given it to Edmund.

I thought of his eyes, by the fire. I thought of his hand on the chair. I had sat and watched him go under and had called it drink.

And he had drunk half. The vicar had had the other half and was insensible. Edmund was half as heavy again, and hardened to spirits, and it would be slower in him. But it would come. He had walked out of the drawing room ten minutes ago, to go out of doors, in a gale, on an island with a fifteen-foot drop all round it. He had business to see to on the water.

I asked the young man where Mr Edmund was now. He said that he had gone to see a man about a boat.

I told him what to do for the vicar. I told him twice. He was a good creature; he listened as if his life depended on it. I said that I must look at a tray. Then I went up those stairs faster than I have ever gone up any stairs, with my hand round the candle flame.

The door of the Admiral's room was shut. I opened it.

The tray was on the table by the bed, where I had left it. The spoon was there, lying across the saucer. The clean glass was there, upside down on its cloth.

There was no bottle.

I looked on the floor. I looked under the bed, and on the mantel, and the washstand, among the seven clocks. I knew that I should not find it. Whoever had taken it had not poured out a dose and put it back. They had taken it away with them. A bottle that had been new on Thursday, with enough in it to put an old man to sleep for sixty nights.

I stood with the candle in the middle of that room. I thought, quite clearly: this is the second time.

I had sat at a desk and put my head on my arm, and a woman had died reaching for a bell. I had sworn that I would never again fail to watch. I had taken night work so that I might sit by one patient with my eyes open and be sure. And I had gone down to a dinner, in a blue dress, to sit beside a man that I had no business to think of. I had left behind me, on a tray, in an unlocked room, the one thing in that house that could do harm.

I do not know who took it. I have never known. I have my own belief, and I have kept it to myself, since I had no proof and it could have done nothing but injury. But whoever's hand it was, the bottle was mine. I had left it there.

I went back down to the drawing room. I ran. I had it in my mind that I must find Edmund, and that first I must see that the old man was safe, since his daughter was with him and could be told, and could send her nephew out with a lamp.

The wing chair was empty.

The rug lay on the floor in front of it. The fire had sunk. Dame Octavia Prowse slept on her sofa. Mr Fenn sat with his paper on his knee and his spectacles pushed up. There was no one at the window. The curtain hung as she had left it, drawn back on the dark.

"Mr Fenn. Where is Mr Vane?"

He started awake. He looked at the chair.

"He was here a moment ago. He got up. He said—" He passed a hand over his face. "He said that he had a clock to stop. I supposed that Miss Vane was with him. Is she not?"

"When did Miss Vane go?"

"I couldn't say. Directly after you, I think. She went out rather quickly. I assumed that she was following you." He was getting to his feet, an old tired man in a dress shirt. "Miss Quaile, is anything wrong?"

I did not answer him. I could not have said it in fewer than a hundred words, and I had not the time for ten.

* * *

I looked for him as I had been trained to look for a patient who has got out of bed. One does it by rooms, in order, and one does not let oneself think.

I looked in the hall. There was no one there. The fire burned and the tall clocks stood round the walls. I noticed, as I went through, that one of them had stopped. It was the plain dark one to the left of the hearth, which he called the regulator and set all the others by. Its pendulum hung straight down behind the glass. In three weeks I had never seen it still. I marked it, as one marks a thing that is out of its place, and went on.

I went down to the Shop. A lamp was burning on the bench, low. His stool was pushed back. The tools lay in their rows. He was not there, and the air was cold, as if a door had been opened.

I went to the side door by the gunroom. It was unbolted. There was wet on the flagstones inside it, the marks of a man's feet, coming in or going out, I could not tell which. I opened the door and held my candle up, and the wind blew it out.

I stood in the doorway in the dark. The rain was driving past. Across the yard I could just see the stable block, and the tower, black. I called his name. The wind took it.

I thought: he has gone to the tower. He has gone to his machine. He said that he had a clock to stop.

I thought: I cannot cross that yard without a light. And I thought of the sea wall, which I was to keep him off.

I went back for a lamp. I am trying to give the order of it truthfully. I went back through the hall, to fetch the lamp that stood at the foot of the stairs. And as I took it up I thought that he might after all have gone to his room. He might have gone up by the back stair while I came down by the front. It would take me one minute to be certain, and if he were there I need not go out at all. I knew it was the cowardly choice. I went up the stairs.

His room was empty. I had left the door open, and the candle I had dropped was still lying on the carpet by the tray.

I came out onto the landing. I stood at the head of the main stair with the lamp in my hand. To my left, the long gallery ran away into the dark. Behind me was the narrow stair that goes to the top floor. Somewhere beneath me a clock that ran a little fast was gathering itself to strike. I looked at the dial of the one on the landing. It was five minutes to twelve.

And the child cried out.

* * *

I have been asked to describe it. I have tried, for Dr Imrie, a number of times.

It was above me. It was on the top floor, at the end, where the old nursery is. It came down the narrow stair as clearly as if a door stood open.

It was a child's voice. I am as sure of that as I am of my own name. It was not a woman's, and it was not an animal, or the wind, or a hinge. I have heard children cry in hospital at night, for their mothers, or from pain. This was neither of those. It was one cry, high and short, and it broke off. It was the sound a child makes that has seen something, and I thought that it had not been frightened for itself. It had seen a thing done.

Then there was nothing. I could hear the clocks.

I do not know how I got up those stairs. I remember the lamp swinging and the shadows of the banisters going round the wall like the spokes of a wheel. I remember that I was saying something aloud, and that it was, "I'm coming, I'm coming," as I used to say it down the length of a ward.

The nursery door was open.

I had tried it at four o'clock that afternoon, and it had been locked. Mrs Pask had told me that it had been shut since 1955. It stood open now, about a foot, and it was dark inside.

I pushed it wide and held up the lamp.

It was a long low room under the roof, with a sloping ceiling and the one barred window. There was a fireguard, and a cupboard painted with faded animals, and a table. There was the rocking horse in the middle of the floor. It was not moving. Its glass eye took the light.

Against the far wall there was a small iron bed.

It had been slept in. I should say that it had been lain on. There was an old eiderdown, pushed back in a heap toward the foot, and a pillow with no case. In the middle of the mattress was the hollow that a body leaves. It was a small one. I have made up a great many beds. I knew the size of the person who had made that hollow as well as if I had weighed it. It was a child of nine or ten.

I crossed the room and put my hand flat in it.

It was warm.

I cannot make that any less than it is. I held my palm there and counted ten, to be sure that it was not the heat of my own hand, and I moved it to the edge of the mattress, which was as cold as the room, and back. The hollow was warm, as a bed is from which someone has risen within the last few minutes.

"Where are you?" I said. I said it gently. "I'm not angry. I'm a nurse. Where have you gone?"

Nothing answered. I looked under the bed. I opened the painted cupboard, and there were a few broken toys, and a smell of mice. I looked behind the door. There was nowhere else in that room that a cat could have hidden.

I went round it then with the lamp, slowly, and I looked at everything, as I had been taught to look at a bedside.

There was a plate on the table. It had crusts on it, and the rind of a piece of cheese. Beside the plate there was a jam jar with an inch of water in it. And there was orange peel. It lay in a neat heap, in the small curled pieces that a child makes who has not yet learned to take it off in one. It was fresh. When I bent to it I smelled what I had smelled on the landing.

On the floor by the bed there was a sheet of paper. I picked it up.

It was a drawing, done in wax crayon. It showed a tall thin building with a pointed top, and near the top a circle with two lines in it for the hands of a clock. Above the circle, under the pointed roof, there were two figures. One was small and black. The other was larger, and had been coloured grey, heavily, with the crayon pressed hard, and it had one arm sticking straight up. Underneath, in pencil, in a child's square capitals, somebody had written:

THE MAN HITS THE LITTLE BELL.
THE LADY WAITS.

And lower down, smaller, as if it had been added afterward:

SHE IS LOOKING AT THE HOUSE.

I held that paper under the lamp. My hand shook so that I could hardly read it. I have it still. I kept it, with the red book. I have shown it to Dr Imrie, and he has looked at it for a long time and given it back to me without a word.

There was one thing more. By the door, on the bare boards, when I turned to go, the lamp showed me a mark. It was wet. It was the print of a shoe, a very small one, and it had come in from the landing. There was another beyond it, and a third, fainter, going toward the bed. Whoever had made them had been out in the rain, and had come back up the stairs running, and had not been in the room long.

I knelt and looked at the nearest. The sole had a pattern. It was a pattern of small stars.

I am a nurse and I do not make up evidence. I have put down what was in that room. There was a warm bed, and a plate, and peel, and a drawing, and three wet footprints with stars in them. A sensible woman, reading that list, will say that there was a child in the house. She will say that somebody had hidden a living child in the old nursery, for some reason of their own, and fed it. The child had got out, and seen something in the dark to make it cry, and run back, and run away again when it heard me on the stairs.

I have said all that to myself. I said it to myself then, on my knees on those boards. I should very much like to believe it.

But I had been in that house three weeks. I knew every person who slept in it and every person who had come to it that day, and there was no child among them. I had asked a truthful woman, who had been there for forty years, and she had looked me in the face and told me that there was no child at Compline. And I ask the sensible reader, in her turn, to tell me this. If there was a living child in that nursery, where did it go? I was on the stair within a quarter of a minute of the cry. No one passed me. There is no other way down from that landing.

There is no child at Compline. I heard a child cry out in the old nursery at five minutes to twelve. I found the bed warm, and nobody in it.

And it was Edmund's nursery. He had slept in that iron bed until he was thirteen. I thought of the photograph of the small boy on the pony, scowling because he was frightened. I thought, kneeling there, that I knew whose voice it had been. He had seen a thing done, a long time ago, and he was still up here at night crying about it, in the room where it had first been safe to.

I got to my feet. As I did so, I heard a sound on the landing. It was a soft rumble, in the wall, like ropes running. There is a hatch there, by the head of the stair, with a wooden door, where the nursery dinners used to be sent up from the kitchen. I pulled it open. The shaft was black, and a cold air came up it. The two ropes that hang there were moving. They were trembling, a very little, as a bell-rope trembles after the ringer has let go.

I let the hatch fall shut.

I had lost the old man. I had lost the bottle. Somewhere out in that gale a man was walking about with a sleeping draught in him that I had been set to guard. And I was standing on the top floor of the house, at midnight, in front of a hole in the wall, with a child's drawing in my hand.

Below me the first of the clocks began to strike twelve.

I took up the lamp and went down.

## 6. The Odd Hour

The front door of Compline House was standing wide open.

I came round the end of the stables onto the gravel and that was what I saw. Eighty yards of dark with the wet shining on it, and at the far end a tall doorway full of yellow light, both halves of the door folded right back against the stone, and nobody on the step. They'd opened it and left it. You could have driven a hearse in.

I crossed toward it slowly. I wasn't being careful. I'd gone past careful. I was wet to the thigh on one side and to the knee on the other, and I had a head like a cracked bell and an arm I couldn't lift. I'd just watched a man go out to sea. And somewhere on that gravel as I walked, or in the trees, or in one of those black outhouses at my back, was somebody with a twelve-bore who'd already used it twice. I ought to have felt that between my shoulders. I didn't much care.

The music got louder as I came. It was a gramophone, an old one, the kind with a horn; you could hear the needle. A big sugary waltz with a lot of fiddles in it.

I stopped at the edge of the light, short of the step, and looked in.

I've said the hall was a stone room with a fireplace and a dozen tall clocks. They'd filled it with candles. There must have been fifty; on the table, on the stairs, along the top of the panelling, on the hoods of the clocks themselves. The fire was built up. The Font stood where it had stood, with the light sliding about on the watches in it. All the long brass pendulums were going behind their little windows, out of step, and winking as they went.

In the middle of the floor two people were dancing.

One of them was the old man. He had his dinner jacket on and his white hair standing up and the eyeglass swinging on its cord. He was holding himself as straight as a guardsman, and he was waltzing. He wasn't shuffling. He was doing it properly, the turn and the reverse and the long step, the way they were taught it before the first war. His left hand was out, holding hers. His right was flat in the small of her back. His face was lifted, and his eyes were shut.

The woman he was dancing with wore a long grey silk dress.

I'll say what I saw and then I'll say what I think. She was a woman of middle height, slim, with dark hair done up on her head. The dress was an evening dress, old-fashioned, cut low at the back, and it was the grey of a pigeon's breast. When they turned and the skirt swung out, the candles ran up and down the folds of it like water. She had her head a little on one side. I knew that dress and I knew that tilt of the head. I'd stood in front of both of them for ten minutes that afternoon, at the end of the long gallery, in a gold frame.

They came round. I saw her face over his shoulder. Grey eyes.

I told myself it was the daughter. I'm telling you now it was the daughter. It couldn't have been anybody else. She was the right height, and her hair was dark, and if there was a white streak in it, well, the candles were on the other side. She'd put on her mother's frock. Why she'd do that on that night, in front of that old man, I couldn't have told you. But families do worse.

I'd like it noted, though, that I stood outside that door for a full minute and wasn't sure. I'd seen one woman in two places at five to one. I'd watched a man with a lamp on a tower an hour after I'd looked at his corpse. By that time, if she'd walked out of the hall straight through the wall, I'd have written it down with the time beside it and gone on.

Nobody was looking at them. That was the queer part. Or they were, but sideways, the way they'd looked at the empty chair at dinner. Dame Octavia Prowse was planted in a big chair by the fire with a glass, with her chin sunk on her chest, staring into the coals. Fenn was at the hall table, bent over, writing something on a long sheet of paper with a fountain pen, and stopping to read it through. The vicar was on a settle with his head back, asleep, and somebody had put a cushion under it. The cropped girl was sitting halfway up the stairs. She had on a man's overcoat, buttoned up to her chin, with her boots sticking out of the bottom of it, and her face was in her hands. No boy with her.

On the hall table, next to the Font, next to Fenn's elbow, where no one seemed to be taking any notice of it, there was a thing I couldn't make out at first. It was a square grey heap, with something on top that shone. Then I got it. It was an army blanket, folded back. And standing in the middle of the blanket, with all the candles in its glass, was the clock. The big dial, the five little ones, and the moon.

I shut my eyes and opened them. It was still there.

I'd tied that clock up in a tea chest at twenty to twelve. I'd watched its owner dead at a quarter past one and missed it from the landing. At a quarter to two I'd gone down twelve steps and found the place where it and he had been. I'd walked six hundred yards along a wall to ask a boatman if he had it. And it was on the hall table, with a party going on round it.

Then I saw her. She was standing by herself at the back of the hall, under the biggest of the clocks, where the light was good. She'd done what I told her. She had the green dress on, dry or near enough, and her hair combed, and her shoes, both of them. She stood with her hands together in front of her and her back straight, and she was watching the old man dance with the woman in grey. Her face was white and it was perfectly composed. You'd have said she was a woman at a party, a little tired.

I'd told her to stand where everybody could see her. She must have felt me there, outside, in the dark. She turned her head, slowly, and looked straight out through the open door at where I was. I don't know if she could see me. She looked for a second or two. Then she looked back at the dancers. She'd been on a stage, and she knew better than to look at the wings.

The record came to the end. The needle went round and round in the groove with a noise like someone breathing. The old man stood still in the middle of his hall, holding the woman, with his eyes shut.

"There," he said. I heard him from the step. "There, my dear. You see. It's quite all right. We had the time after all."

She kissed him and went, quickly, toward the back of the hall. I didn't see where. The nurse got the old man to a chair. And the nephew, who'd been sitting on the stairs with a plate, got up and crossed the floor to her. He stood bent right over her, tall as he was, with his big pink hands going, saying something very fast and low, and stopping, and starting again. She was looking up at him without any expression at all. As I watched she nodded, once. He straightened up as if someone had given him a present.

* * *

I didn't go in by that door. I've never felt less like walking into a room.

I went round the side, past the dark dining-room windows where I'd stood at midnight in a flowerbed, and in at the kitchen yard. The back door was open, as she'd said. There was a lamp burning on a long scrubbed table, and a range with the fire door ajar and a red glow behind it, and the carcass of the goose in a pan. There was nobody about. I stood on the mat and dripped. The kitchen clock on the wall said twenty-two minutes to two. That would have been a lie half an hour ago. It was now, by Act of Parliament, more or less the truth. I'd got to stop thinking like that.

I looked for the nail by the range. It was there, a big one, driven into the bricks. Nothing on it. Her keys were still hanging in the ice-house gate.

I was looking for a drink, mostly. But a room tells you things when it's empty, and I went round that one. There was a stone sink with a wooden plate rack. One plate had been taken off the rack and laid on the draining board, a big oval dish, a good one, with nothing on it. Beside it stood a round thing under a cloth. I lifted the cloth. It was a copper mould, a tall one, shaped like a dome, with a lid. A pudding mould. It was empty and clean, and it was warm from the room.

On the table somebody had dumped a tray of little glass plates, dirty, with cream and bits of sponge on them. I counted. Thirteen. Twelve had been scraped clean and one hadn't been touched. They'd even given her a helping of that.

So somebody had fetched out a dish to turn a moulded pudding onto, and then served a trifle instead. The mould had never been filled, or it had been filled and emptied and washed. I thought about a silver serving spoon in wet straw, with a crest on it. I had one wheel, a spoon, a pair of slippers, the cook's keys and an empty pudding mould. I've done a few crosswords since, in the home, of an afternoon, and I know now what you do with a set of clues like that: you stop looking for something clever. I picked up the cloth and put it back over the mould. I felt the answer brush past me in that kitchen. I heard it go out of the door behind me, and I let it.

There was a brandy bottle on the dresser, the cooking stuff. I had some from the neck.

Off the kitchen was a stone passage, and off that a door with a slatted panel in it. I opened it because it was there. It was the pantry: shelves, crocks, a ham in a cloth on a hook, a marble slab. In the wall at the back was a square hatch with a rope hanging in it, a lift for sending dishes up. On the floor underneath the slab, shoved in anyhow, with its lid beside it and a length of sash cord trailing, was a plywood tea chest with tin edges.

It was empty. There was nothing in it but a pillowcase.

So they'd unpacked it here. They'd carried the box in from the ice house to the kitchen, into the pantry, and opened it, and taken the thing out and carried it through to the hall and stood it on a table on its blanket, for anyone to see. *Who* had? It wasn't a way to steal a clock. It wasn't a way to hide one. I leaned on the marble and tried to think like Edmund Vane's murderer, and like Ronnie Sears, and like a wife, and I couldn't make any of them do it.

It didn't occur to me to think like a man who'd been sent to fetch the pudding.

* * *

When I went up the back passage and through the baize door into the hall, the dancing was over and the company had thinned out. The gramophone was shut. The woman in grey had gone, and so had Celia. The girl had gone from the stairs. Dame Octavia hadn't moved, and the vicar slept on. The nephew was at the fire, alone, pouring himself something, and humming. The front door was still open on the night, and the candles nearest to it were streaming sideways.

Leo Vane was standing by himself in front of one of the tall clocks.

It was on the left of the fireplace, plainer than the rest, a long narrow case of dark wood with a glass door the whole length of the front. He had the door open. Inside, where the others had a pendulum going to and fro, this one had a pendulum hanging dead. It wasn't going. I'd come past it, I suppose, a dozen times that day, and couldn't have told you whether it was or not. He was looking at something in the bottom of the case. As I came up he put his hand in, and touched it with the tips of his fingers.

It was the weight. A cylinder of dull grey metal a bit bigger than a tin of beans, hanging on a line off a pulley, low down, where it had run to the end of its drop.

I'd come to ask him about the clock on the table. I'd come, I suppose, because he was the one who'd hired me, and it was gone two, and he'd said he would know by one. I stopped a yard behind him. I had no idea what I was going to say.

He turned his head. The glass was in his eye.

"You've been in the water," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"That's unwise. It was a spring tide and a surge on it. High water was at eight minutes to one. I have that from the Thorne, which is never out, not by a minute in the year, whatever they print on their cards." He took his fingers away from the lead and rubbed them together, slowly, looking at them. "Are you the man?"

"I'm the man Mr Fenn sent."

"Then you are late. You were to tell me by one."

"I know who took your clocks, Mr Vane."

"So do I," said Leo Vane. "I've seen to it."

It's a small thing, how a man says four words. I'd have passed over it from most people. But he said it the way he'd have told me he had put a new spring in something. A job done, and done properly. Then he looked at his fingers again.

I looked too. I couldn't help it. There was candlelight all down that side of him. His hand was clean. But the cuff of his shirt, the right one, where it came out of the black sleeve, wasn't. The edge of it was brown. There was a stain on the linen the size of a half-crown and a few more, smaller, going up toward the link, like drops off a brush. I've seen that colour a good many times, on a good many shirts, when it's had an hour or two to dry.

"You've hurt yourself," I said.

"I?" He turned the wrist over and considered it. "No. No, I don't believe so. That will be Edmund."

There are times when everything in a room gets very clear and small, as if you were looking down the wrong end of a telescope. I remember the noise of the fire. I remember the nephew humming.

"What will be Edmund, sir?"

"He was in the ice house, you know. He had her clock in a box, like groceries. I asked him why. I asked him quite civilly. I've been waiting all the year to ask him why." His voice was thin and reasonable. "He laughed. He said, 'It's a copy, you old fool. They're all copies. There's nothing in this house that isn't.' And I had this in my hand. I don't recollect why I had it in my hand. I must have taken it off to stop the clock. One stops the clocks when there is a death, you know, it was always done. So I must have known beforehand. That's the part I can't get straight."

He put his hand into the case again and lifted the weight an inch on its line and let it down. It was heavy. You could see the cord go taut.

"Fourteen pounds," he said. "Lead, in a brass jacket. Mr Vulliamy's, for the regulator. I struck him here." He touched his own left temple, above the eye, with one long forefinger. "He went down the steps. I heard him go. I killed him, you know. At ten minutes to twelve. I looked at the dial as I came in; every clock in the hall said it."

He'd got the right temple. I mean the left, the correct one. I'd looked at it by a lighter for a minute at a quarter past one, and it had gone in where it should have gone out.

"Mr Vane—"

"And now I haven't." He smiled at me. It was the smile of a clever child. "That's what's so very interesting. You should write that down; you had a notebook. At one o'clock the hour came back. I had calculated that it would, and it did, and I've been through it a second time, and it has all gone differently. Margot came down. She didn't go out on the Wade at all. I gave her the tide, properly this time, to the minute, and she's gone to put her shoes on." He glanced round the hall as if to be sure nobody else could hear, and lowered his voice. "I haven't been out to see about Edmund yet. I am going directly. But you may take it from me that he will be perfectly well. It doesn't happen the second time. That's the whole principle."

I stood there. I've interviewed some mad people. There was a woman in Hackney who'd been getting messages from her gas meter. But I've also interviewed a good many men who told me a thing that was sixty per cent raving, and in the middle of it was a fact, with a time on it and a place, and the fact checked. You learn to hear the difference. The raving comes loose and wanders. The fact stays where it's put.

Ten minutes to twelve by the hall clocks. That was ten to one by my watch. He'd named the ice house and the box, and the left temple, and the steps. And there was that cuff.

And I had a woman's kiss on my mouth, a woman who'd sat on a hearthrug and told me, *I did that. Nobody pushed him. You needn't look for anybody.*

They couldn't both have. Could they? She gives him enough dope to drop a horse. He walks out of the house on his own legs half an hour later; she'd said so herself, and been surprised at it. He goes down to the spinney. And somebody follows him there. It's not a boatman or a moneylender's man. It's his own father, eighty-one years old, in his evening pumps, with a lump of lead off a clock swinging in his hand.

Then the gate. I'd wondered about the gate. *A man doesn't shut himself in a hole he means to come out of.* No. But a man who has just done a thing like that, and walks away from it, pulls the door to behind him. Anybody would. He wouldn't even know he had done it.

It fitted. God help me, it fitted better than hers.

I was going to ask him. I had my mouth open. I was going to say, *Mr Vane, who else was there? Who moved him?* But I saw that I'd lost him. His eyes had gone past me to the open front door and the dark. He was listening to something.

"It's stopped," he said. "The Jack's stopped. He should have done the quarter." He frowned. "I must go across. I've the Reconciler to see to, and it's all coupled through the going train, and if he has jammed—" He began to walk. He went by me as if I were a hatstand. At the door he checked, and put a hand on the stone, and looked back into his hall, at the candles and the clocks.

"You might tell my wife where I've gone," he said. "If she asks."

Then he went out, down the step, onto the gravel, in his thin shoes. I watched him as far as the light went. A long white-haired old man going off slowly toward the stables.

I let him go. I'd like that known, as well. An old man of eighty-one, off his head, was going out alone at half past two in the morning, toward a place where somebody had been letting off a shotgun. I stood and watched. I've got no excuse. I was thinking about his cuff.

"Excuse me." It was the nurse. She had come up beside me without my hearing. She was a small pale thing, and that close I could see she was shaking, very slightly, all over, like a wire with a current in it. "Have you seen Mr Vane? I only turned my back. I was asked something and I only turned my back for a minute."

"He was here," I said. "He was looking at this clock."

She looked at the clock. She looked at the open case, and the still pendulum, and the grey weight at the bottom of its line. I saw her look at that weight and I saw her stop shaking.

She didn't say anything. She put out her hand and shut the long glass door, gently, till the catch clicked.

"Thank you," said Miss Quaile, and went away toward the stairs. She hadn't asked me where he'd gone, and I hadn't told her. I'd file that, I thought. If I ever opened the cabinet again.

* * *

Fenn found me in the library. I'd gone there because it was dark and had a chair in it. The fire was nearly out. The glass case stood on its table in the window with the baize cloth over it, covering nothing.

He came in with a candle and shut the door and set the candle down on the mantelpiece. He stood with his back to the grate and his hands behind him. He had a long envelope sticking out of his breast pocket.

"You look as though you had been in a road accident, Mr Cade."

"I was. Somebody's son ran into me."

"I shan't ask." He meant it. "Mr Vane wished to be told the name of a person before one o'clock. He was not told. May I ask whether you are now in a position to tell it to me?"

"Edmund."

"Yes." There wasn't a flicker.

"He lifted the Thorne out of that case at about twenty past eleven, when the lights went. He put it in a tea chest and carried it to the ice house. I saw him. I saw the clock in the chest. There was a boat due at the Hythe at a quarter to two, sent by a moneylender in Southend called Sears, to whom he owes a hundred and eighty thousand pounds. He's had thirty clocks out of this house in four years, not eleven. That's what you paid for." I had the back of my head against the leather of the chair. It was the only way I could keep the room level. "The boat came. I talked to the man in it. He's gone, without the clock. The clock is on the table in the hall."

"I am aware of that," Fenn said. "I was present when it arrived."

I sat up. It cost me something.

"You were what?"

"I was in the hall, with the rest of the company, at one o'clock. Mr Vane had opened the door. There was a clap of thunder, or so I took it to be, two claps, and some little confusion; Miss Quaile cried out. And into the midst of this came Mr Pargeter, from the kitchen passage, carrying a wooden box. He appeared to be under the impression that it contained a pudding." Fenn said it with no more expression than he'd used for the family tree. "He set it on the table and removed the lid with something of a flourish. I believe he had prepared a few words. It contained what you have seen."

"The *nephew* had it?"

"Mr Pargeter."

"Where the hell did the nephew get it?"

"I did not enquire. I formed the view, Mr Cade, some hours ago, that this was not a night upon which to enquire into anything. I am sixty-seven years of age, and I have a wife."

I got my head back onto the leather. A pudding. I thought about the empty mould under its cloth. The spoon. The saucer with the candle on the third step. One wheel.

It's hard to describe how I felt. Something was trying to come up, from a long way down, and it was nearly there. It felt like a laugh. I was afraid of it. I thought, if I once let that come up, I'll never be able to take anything seriously again, and there's a man dead.

"What did the old man say? When he saw it?"

"He looked at it for some moments through his glass. He said, 'Ah. The Copy. I wondered where I had put it.' He then observed that it had stopped. And he thanked Mr Pargeter, and said that it was very thoughtful, but that he believed we had been promised an ice."

"The copy."

"That was the word he used."

"He told me a few minutes ago that Edmund said the same thing to him. That it was a copy. That everything in the house was."

"Did he." Fenn looked at the covered case in the window. "I should not place too much reliance, Mr Cade, on anything Mr Vane has said since midnight. Nor, I regret to say, since about 1991. He is capable of telling you with great exactness the day on which he acquired that clock and what he paid for it. He is equally capable of telling you that his wife has gone upstairs to change her shoes."

"There's a mark," I said. "On the real one. A sprig of thorn. Scratched under the left foot of the dial. I looked for it this afternoon."

"And?"

I didn't answer him straight off.

"The light was bad," I said.

Fenn inclined his head. He didn't say anything. He reached into his breast pocket, beside the long envelope, and brought out a smaller one, and held it out to me between two fingers.

"What's that?"

"A cheque. Three days at two hundred pounds, and fifty for expenses. You will find it in order."

"I've done one day."

"You have done all that will be required." He waited until I'd taken it. "I shall tell you how matters stand, and then, if you'll forgive me, I shall go to bed. In the course of the last hour Mr Vane addressed his family. He was, for perhaps ten minutes together, entirely himself; more so than I have seen him in three years. He said that he had asked a question at dinner, and that he no longer wished to have it answered. He said the matter of the clocks was closed. He said that whoever had taken them had no doubt had need of them, and that he had been a poor father to anyone who had been driven to it, and that it was all to go on the Hour's account and not be spoken of again. He then desired me to take down a codicil to his will, which I did, and which has been properly witnessed, and which does not concern you." He touched the long envelope. "My instructions from my client, therefore, are that you found nothing. There was no enquiry. A gentleman from Pemberton's came to count the clocks and was detained by the tide. That is what I shall say if I am asked, and I should be obliged if you would say the same."

"There's more to it than clocks, Fenn."

"I daresay."

"A man's dead."

He didn't jump. I'll give him that. He stood on the hearthrug with the candle behind him and his face in shadow. It was quite a time before he spoke.

"Which man?"

"Edmund Vane."

"You have seen him?"

"At a quarter past one, in the ice house, at the bottom of the steps, with his head stove in. Through the gate. And at a quarter past two in the creek behind the stables, on his face, going out with the tide."

"Through the gate," Fenn said. "And in the creek. I see. You did not, on either occasion, lay a hand upon him?"

"I couldn't get to him."

"No. Then you have not, in the sense in which a coroner would understand the words, got a body."

"He's not in the house, is he? Go and look for him. Go and knock on his door."

"I shall do nothing of the kind. Edmund has not slept a night through in his own bed on this date in twenty years. He walks about. He has been found in the morning in the stable, and in the boathouse, and on one occasion in the vicar's car." Fenn took his hands from behind his back and looked at them. "Mr Cade. I engaged you because I was told that you had once, at considerable cost to yourself, declined to go along with a falsehood. I thought the better of you for it. I think the better of you now. But I will ask you to consider your position. By your own account you have been struck on the head. You've been drinking; so have we all. It is dark, and you don't know the place. You saw something through a gate by, I assume, a match, and something else in the water by moonlight. And upon that you propose to tell me, at the age of eighty-one, that Leo Vane's son is dead? Tonight, of all nights? When he has just forgiven him?"

"I propose to tell the police. At twenty-five to six, when the road dries."

"That is your right. I should not dream of preventing you." He picked up his candle. "I would only observe that if a man has gone into the sea off the Ness on the ebb of a spring tide, the sea will bring him back in its own time, without the least assistance from either of us. It always does. And that when it does, everyone in this house will be asked where they were, and what they saw. I shall say that I was in the hall, taking down a codicil. I wonder what you'll say. I wonder who you would be obliged to mention."

He knew. He didn't know what, exactly. He'd stood in that hall for an hour and watched Celia Vane come in at the garden door at ten to one with her hair wet, and take up a place under a clock, and not move. He'd watched an old man come in from the dark with a clock-weight. He'd been the family's solicitor for forty years. He knew there was something, and where it was, and that was as much as he wanted. I'd met a hundred like him. They'd tell you they never asked a client whether he did it, and they'd tell you it was a principle.

"You'd rather not know," I said. "You told me that on Wednesday."

"I told you that I act for the family, Mr Cade. All of the family." He opened the door. "Good night. There's a decanter on the table behind you. I should have the whisky; the brandy in this house has been rather unreliable this evening."

* * *

I had the whisky. I sat in the dark with it and Fenn's cheque. Six hundred and fifty pounds, to have found nothing.

I'd been in that house eleven hours. I counted up. I'd had two clients. One of them had just paid me off and told me the job had never existed. The other was a widow, and I'd stood in a brick well and told her so, twice. I had two confessions. The wife said the drug had done it, and showed me the bottle. The father said the weight, and showed me his cuff. They couldn't both be the whole of it, and each of them, I'd have sworn, was telling me what they believed. There'd been a body, and somebody had moved it. I'd got a clock worth a million pounds that might be worth nothing. There was a shotgun out there I couldn't account for at all, and a boy with a cricket bat, and somebody in slippers. And somebody had shown a dead man's signal from the top of a tower.

I've known cases with nobody in the frame. I never had one before where everybody was trying to get into it.

At some point the clock in the stable tower struck. I heard it through the open front door, down the length of the hall. The big bell, twice. Two o'clock by the house, three by the watch on my wrist. No. Two by both. I'd forgotten to put it back. I never did put it back, not that night. I went on being an hour ahead of the lot of them till the morning.

There was something wrong with the striking. I sat and worked out what it was. There'd been no little bell first. All night it had gone *ting-tang* four times over before the hour, and that time it hadn't. Only the big one, twice, slow, out of nowhere. The old man had said it: *the Jack's stopped*. I thought of the little roof up there, and the one figure left under it with her arm up.

The door opened. I knew who it was before I saw her. That scent came in ahead of her.

She shut the door and stood against it, exactly as she had at eleven in the other room. She had no candle. There was only what was left of the fire.

"You said you'd tell me first," Celia Vane said.

So I told her. I told her about the boat, and one man in it, and him gone. I told her about the light on the tower and the shots, and what came down. I told her what I'd watched go under the wall at a quarter past two. I didn't make it gentle, because there wasn't a gentle way. She listened to all of it without a sound. When I got to the creek she slid down the door until she was sitting on the floor with her knees up. She put her forehead on them.

I didn't tell her about the old man's cuff.

I've asked myself why. I had it ready. It would have been a kindness, of a sort, to say to her: it may not have been your bottle. It may have been a piece of lead, and you needn't carry it. I didn't say it. I think it was because I wasn't sure, and you don't hand a woman a thing like that unless you're sure. I'd like to think so. But I know what I am. If she hadn't done it, she wouldn't need me, and I think that was in it too.

"Then he's gone," she said, into her knees. "There isn't even anything to find in the morning."

"Not here. Somewhere down the coast, in a day or two."

"And they'll say he drowned."

"He went out drunk and he went off the wall. Twelve people saw how he was at dinner. It's a better story than the ice house, as it happens. Nobody has to explain a locked gate." I heard how that sounded. I went on anyway. "There's blood on the straw down there. I'll see to that before it's light. And your cook's keys."

She lifted her head.

"Why did they bring the clock into the house?"

"I don't know. The nephew had it. He thought it was a pudding."

"Toby?" She stared at me, and then she made a sound that was half a laugh. "Oh, God. Toby. Of course he did." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "And Leo says it's a copy."

"Leo says his wife's upstairs changing her shoes."

"Yes."

"Is it a copy?"

"I don't know," said Celia Vane. "I never could tell one from another. I hated every one of them." She was quiet. Then she said, in a different voice, slower: "Does it matter? If the man who's paying for it thinks it's the real one?"

I looked at her across the dark room. I think I'd known since the fourth stair that it would come to this.

"Sears has still got to be paid," she said. "He doesn't stop because Edmund's dead. You know him; I don't. Does he stop?"

"No."

"He'll come to the estate. He'll come to Leo. He'll come to Kit." She got up off the floor. She came across to the chair and knelt down in front of it and put her two hands on my knees, and I could feel the cold of them through the wet cloth. "Harry. That clock's sitting on the hall table with a blanket round it, and nobody in this house wants it. Leo's just told everyone he doesn't care who took what. There's a man in Southend who'll wipe out a hundred and eighty thousand pounds for it and give something over. Edmund fixed the price. It's all arranged; it's only the boat that's gone. You've got a car."

"You want me to drive a stolen clock to Ronnie Sears."

"I want you to drive me. I'll do the rest. I'll telephone him from a box. I know the number, I've heard it often enough. You needn't see him. You needn't see anyone." Her hands tightened. "I can't stay here. I can't be in this house when they find he's gone. I can't sit at that table at breakfast and watch them start to wonder, and Imogen looking at me, and the boy. I'll go mad. I'll say something. Take me with you when the road opens. Please."

"And after?"

"I don't know about after. I told you, I do a day at a time."

I've got to be fair to myself, just once. I knew what it was. I knew a woman who's on her knees to you at three in the morning isn't there because of your eyes. I knew I'd be driving off that island at dawn with a dead man's wife and a dead man's clock in the boot, a few hours after he'd gone out on the tide. And I'd be doing it under the nose of a solicitor who had just told me, in so many words, that he'd remember it.

But I could feel her hands. And I thought of that card from Sears, in the drawer at Leigh, six years old. I thought, he'll take it. He'll take a copy for a million and never know, and I'll have sold it to him. It was the only way I was ever going to get to laugh at him.

"Six o'clock," I said. "By my watch. Be in the yard. Bring a coat of your own."

She put her head down on my knees. I put my hand on her hair. It was still damp.

We stayed like that. Out in the hall the clocks went on, all hundred and eleven of them. The Odd Hour was over. They'd told me nothing in it would count.

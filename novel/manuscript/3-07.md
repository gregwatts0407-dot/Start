## VII. The Hurdle

The windows of the Admiral's room look east. I drew back one curtain at about five and watched it get light.

There is not much colour in a dawn on that coast. The sky went from black to the grey of a slate, and then a yellow line came along the bottom of it, behind the stables. The tower stood against that. I could see the clock face, and the hands, and the roof over the bell.

She was there, on the right. Her shawl hung straight and wet. On the left there was an iron stump, and the sky.

I looked at the gap for a long time. I thought of what Dame Octavia had said in the hall. The Jack had gone, then. That much was not in doubt. Something of oak in a dinner jacket had been on that tower at midnight and was not there at dawn.

I ought to have been comforted. I told myself to be.

A little before six, by the clocks behind me, two cars went out of the yard. The first was the clergyman's, driven very slowly. Some minutes afterward a large one followed. I saw the heavy man from the insurers at the wheel. Beside him, in a pale coat, with a scarf over her head, sat Mrs Edmund Vane.

She did not look back at the house. I watched the car go down the track toward the Wade, and I knew that she would not return. I knew it as surely as one knows that a patient has decided not to get well. I said nothing about it to anyone that day. I had begun to count the things that I was saying nothing about, and that was the fifth.

At seven Mr Vane woke. He lay for a while looking at the canopy. Then he turned his head on the pillow and found me.

"Has she gone?" he said.

I did not ask whom he meant.

"I believe so, Mr Vane. I've not been down."

"She will have waited for the horse. I told her twenty minutes to three." He was quite clear, and very tired, and peaceful. "Did she get across?"

I had lied to him three times in the night. I had cleaned a weight. I found that I could not add to it.

"I don't know," I said. "I didn't see."

"No. You were with me. You're a good girl." He closed his eyes. "She got across. I gave it to her to the minute. A horse can do it at that state of the tide, if he knows the Rill, and the Garrows' horses always did." He smiled. "I should like my tea. And then I must go down to the Shop. Edmund is coming to me there after breakfast. He said so, in the tower. He has something more to tell me, and he wants the girl by, he says, to hold his coat."

I was folding the rug. I went on folding it.

"Did he say that, Mr Vane?"

"He did. He said, 'I've told you the worst of it, Father, and I'll tell you the rest in the morning, in the Shop, with Miss Quaile there to keep me to it. She's got a book of Mother's that you're to see.'" He opened his eyes. "Have you a book of my wife's?"

"Yes."

"Then bring it."

I had heard a voice in the clock chamber, and had not dared to climb four rungs to see whose it was. I had told myself afterward that the old man had been talking to the dark. And here he lay, in the morning, with his wits about him, repeating to me an arrangement that I had made with his son in the library the afternoon before. No one else alive knew of it.

I shall leave that as it stands. I have not been able to do anything else with it.

* * *

I took him down to the Shop at nine. I had the red book in my pocket.

He sat at his bench, under the window. He was happier than I had ever seen him. He took up a small brass plate that he had been engraving and looked at it through his glass, and hummed. It was the waltz.

I stood by the steps, where I could see the door. I had my hands clasped to stop them shaking. I listened for an uneven step on the stone.

It was a quarter past nine by the Shop clocks, of which there were nineteen. It was half past. I heard the hall clocks tell ten, overhead, one after another. The old man worked on. From time to time he looked up at the door, mildly, and then at me, and went back to his plate.

At a quarter to eleven he laid down his graver.

"He is not coming," he said.

"He may have overslept, Mr Vane."

"No. He would have come. He gave me his word, and he has never given it before, so that I know he meant it." He turned on his stool. "Something has prevented him. It doesn't matter. He told me the chief thing. What was the rest, do you know?"

I came forward. I took out the book.

"He said that if he was not here, I was to give you this anyway. And I was to tell you that Edmund said he was sorry, and that it wasn't the clock."

"It was not the clock," Mr Vane repeated. "No. He told me so. She knew I'd put it back; she had her watch. She went in good time. And she turned round, on the Wade, and came back." His voice was steady. "He told me why she turned. I shan't tell you, my dear. It's his to tell, and he was only a boy. But she was coming back to this house when the water took her. That is what I have. I thought for twenty years that she drowned going away from me."

He held out his hand. I put the diary into it.

He did not open it. He held it on his palm, and looked at the red cover, and the gilt year. He ran his thumb along the edge of the pages, as he would test the edge of a wheel.

"That's her hand," he said. "On the tide tables. I'd know it. Where was this?"

"In the little gilt clock on her desk. The one that doesn't go. The works had been taken out."

A slow smile came over his face. It was the first time I had seen him look like the man that the woman in the portrait was laughing at.

"In the one that was allowed to be wrong," he said. "Oh, Margot. Of course it was. She knew I should never open that case. I had given her my word in 1948." He was silent for some time. "What is in it?"

"The year 1974, Mr Vane. Up to the day."

"Does she speak of me?"

"Yes."

"Kindly?"

I thought of the cathedral, and the cold feet. I thought of *I do love him.*

"Very kindly," I said. "And truthfully."

"And of someone else."

I did not answer. He nodded.

"Yes. Amos Garrow. I knew it then and I've not let myself know it since. He had a way with a horse that I never had with anything living." He looked at the book. "If I read this I shall hear her say it. I shall hear exactly how she said it; she had a way of putting things. And I shall know what she thought of me in the last year, to the word."

He closed his hand on it. Then he held it out to me.

"No. I'll not read it. She hid it from me. If she had wished me to see it she would have left it on my bench. She was not a careless woman." His hand was quite firm. "She turned back. That is enough for an old man. I'll not go behind it and ask what she turned back from. Put it where you found it."

"In the clock?"

"In the clock that's allowed to be wrong. It may stay there until I am dead, and then you may do as you think right. You're a truthful girl. But you have learned something about truth in this house, I think, that you did not know when you came."

I took the book. I could not see him clearly.

"I've lied to you, Mr Vane," I said. "Three times, last night. I said I never would."

"I daresay you had reason. People do." He picked up his graver. "My wife lied to me for ten years, it appears, and Edmund for twenty. And I find that I can forgive the pair of them between breakfast and luncheon. It is extraordinary how little it weighs, afterward. I wish I had known it sooner."

I put the red book back that afternoon, in the gilt drum between the cupids, and shut the little door. It stayed there till the spring.

* * *

By noon the house knew that something was wrong.

Miss Vane's bed had not been slept in. There was a line on a sheet of paper on her dressing table: *Gone across to Saltcote. Don't wait luncheon. I.* Gail brought it to me, since there was no one else to bring it to. Mrs Edmund Vane had gone to the chemist's at Maldon at six in the morning, on a Sunday, and had not come home. Mr Fenn and the old lady had driven off at eight. The young man and the vicar were gone.

And Mr Edmund was not to be found.

That in itself troubled nobody at first. Mrs Pask told me so, in the kitchen. She was back at her range. She had been to her cottage on the mainland at first light, she said, to see to something, and had come back across on her bicycle. I had expected to find her as I had left her the day before, and I was startled. She looked as though she had not slept for a week, and yet her face was lighter than I had ever seen it, and while she worked she sang under her breath. I had not known that she could.

"He'll be laid up somewhere," she said. "He was sat on my table at a quarter past two eating goose; Mr Toby saw him. He'd a head on him. He'll have gone to ground, like a dog. I've known him not come in till the Monday."

"Mrs Pask. You were in the stable with him. Mr Pargeter told me."

She gave me a look.

"Mr Pargeter's a tongue like a bell-clapper. Yes. I was. And I'll thank you to keep it from the old gentleman."

"Was he badly hurt?"

"He'd a lump on his brow like an egg. And a cut at the back, that he got in the yard." She stopped singing. "He spoke to us. He was sensible. I'd not have left him else." She turned a pan. "You've not said nothing upstairs? About where he was found?"

"No."

"Nor I shan't. It's the old man I'm thinking of. He's had the best night he's had in twenty years; Gail says he was dancing. I'll not have it spoiled with tales of Mr Edmund lying drunk in the ice house."

I said nothing. I thought of what else she did not know about that ice house, and who had been there before her. I held my hand over the place on my dress, though I had changed it.

"Mrs Pask," I said. "I went up to the nursery last night. The door was open."

She did not turn round.

"Was it."

"The bed was warm. There was a plate, and orange peel. There were wet footmarks."

"You'll have been dreaming. You were up all night."

"I have a drawing that I found on the floor. It is in my pocket now."

She put down her spoon. She stood with her back to me and her two hands on the rail of the range. I saw her shoulders rise and fall.

"Miss Quaile," she said. "I've been in this house forty-two years. I've kept more than you'll ever know, and none of it my own. I'm going to ask you to leave that be. There's no harm come of it. I swear that on my mother. There's nobody been hurt by what was in that room, and nobody will be, so long as it's let alone. You'd oblige me by putting that paper on the fire."

"Whose was the voice?"

"There wasn't any voice," said Mrs Pask.

She took up the spoon again. In a moment she had begun to sing.

I did not put the paper on the fire. I have said that I kept it. But I did not ask her again. She had sworn that there was no harm, and I had believed her about the wind in the flue, and been wrong. I believed her about this. I do not know that I was right.

* * *

It was Mr Kit who raised the alarm.

He had gone out at nine to look for his father, with the cropped girl. He was in a queer state. He would not look at anyone, and he started at every sound, and twice that morning I saw him go to the door of the harness room and stand there, staring at the floor, as though he expected to see something lying on it. He had asked Gail three times whether the gentleman from the insurers had been at breakfast. When she told him that Mr Cade had driven off at six with Mrs Vane, he sat down on the stairs and laughed until he cried.

I did not understand it. I have learned since that he had his own reasons for watching that floor.

They searched the stables. They went up the tower. He came down and said that the Jack was gone off his pin, sheared clean through, and that the post behind was full of shot. He said the trap had been open and there was blood on the boards of the clock chamber, a little, and a handkerchief. He held it out. It was a small one, a child's, with a pattern on it of red maple leaves.

"That's not Dad's," he said. "Whose is that?"

Nobody knew. I looked at it in his hand. I thought of footprints with stars.

They went along the sea wall, north and south. The tide had gone out in the night and come half in again. At about ten the girl, who had run ahead, began to shout from the outfall, where the fleet goes through the wall into the saltings.

I was at the Admiral's window. I had gone up to fetch the old man's spectacles. I saw the boy begin to run.

I must tell this exactly as it came to me.

For an hour and a half there was nothing. I could not leave Mr Vane. I got him his luncheon on a tray. I stood at one window and then another. From the gallery, on the west side, one can see along the wall as far as the outfall, and I saw figures there, small and dark, bending. There were four of them. The boy and the girl had fetched two men from the oyster sheds on the point. They had spades.

They were digging something out of the mud.

I watched until I could not watch. I went and sat with the old man and read to him, I do not know what. The clocks told half past eleven. They told the quarter.

At noon I heard the front door, and feet in the hall, a number of them, heavy, and going carefully, as people walk who are carrying something between them. I heard Gail scream.

I went down the stairs. I did not run. I held on to the rail.

They had set it down in the middle of the hall, on the flagstones, where the old man had danced. It was a sheep hurdle, such as they use on the marsh. Something long lay on it, under wet sacking. The four of them were standing round, with mud to their thighs. The cropped girl had her knuckles in her mouth.

From under the edge of the sacking, on my side, an arm hung down. It was in a black sleeve. There was a white cuff at the end of it, soaked and grey, and it trailed on the stone.

I came to the bottom of the stairs. I stopped. I thought: I shall see his face. I must be ready to see it. I have seen the faces of the drowned before, at home; they bring them into the lifeboat house.

Mr Kit stooped. He took hold of the sacking at the head end and turned it back.

They brought him in at noon on a hurdle, and he was made of wood.

It was the Jack. He lay on his back with his painted face to the ceiling. I had only ever seen him from far below. He was smaller than a man and thicker. His features had been cut with a chisel two hundred years ago, by someone who made figureheads, and they were broad and simple: a nose, a ledge of brow, a mouth like a slot. There was mud in the mouth. One arm was raised from the shoulder, stiff, with the fist closed round a peg where the hammer had been. Someone had put a dinner jacket on him and buttoned it, and a starched front, and a black bow.

The front was torn open. The wood beneath was pale and fresh, and splintered, in a patch as wide as two hands, and there were small dark holes in it, a great many, close together.

"He was in the outfall," the boy said. His voice cracked. "Jammed in the grating. Face down. We saw the jacket. We saw the back of his head and the collar. I thought—Sass thought—" He stopped. He put the back of his muddy hand against his eyes. "For about a minute down there I thought it was Dad."

The girl began to laugh. It was not a good sound. One of the oystermen patted her on the back, as one would a horse.

I stood and looked down at that wooden face. I had thought that I should feel relief. The old lady had been right, and my eyes had been wrong. A dummy had been shot off a clock by someone after duck, and here it was. The sensible woman had won her case.

It was not what I felt. I had seen a man in evening dress go backward off that tower. I had been told since, by two people, that a living man had been lying in the stable beneath it a quarter of an hour before. I had heard a voice in the clock chamber that I knew. I had stood on the bank of that creek and held a lamp over the water.

And now they had gone down to the place where the creek runs out, and dug, and brought back what the water had kept. It lay on the stone at my feet, and it had never been alive.

I thought: then where is the other?

I looked up. The front door had been left open behind the bearers. Through it, across the gravel, I could see the tower. She stood alone, with her arm lifted. She was looking at the house.

I am going to write down what came into my mind. I have said that I despise this kind of thing. But I have been told to leave nothing out.

I thought: she has changed them. A man went up to her on the stroke of one. He had done her a wrong, a long time ago, on the Wade, and had kept it twenty years, and that day I had set a term to his keeping. He stood beside her at the hour. She brought down her arm. And what the house gave back to us, in the morning, on a hurdle, was the thing that had stood there before him. She had kept the man.

I know what that is. I have known all along. I do not need to be told.

Mr Vane had come to the head of the stairs. He came down them slowly, with his hand on the rail. He stood by the hurdle, and screwed the glass into his eye, and bent.

"Number five shot," he said. "Both barrels, I should think, at thirty yards. It has gone through the lime and into the heart-oak. He can be mended. I have some seasoned oak in the Shop that was his brother's." He straightened. He looked round at our faces. "Why are you all so white? It's only the Jack. He has been loose on his pin since 1941."

Nobody answered him.

"Where is Edmund?" he said.

* * *

They found Edmund on the Monday afternoon.

A man who was digging for bait came upon him at low water on a bank they call the Ray Sand, seven miles to the south. He was in his shirt and trousers. He had no coat.

A policeman came to the house in the evening. I was in the hall when Mr Fenn, who had driven over, went into the drawing room to tell the old man. He was in there four minutes by the clock at the foot of the stairs. When he came out he was grey.

"He says that it is a mistake," Mr Fenn said to me. "He says that he saw to it. He says that he went back." He took off his spectacles. "I have told him twice. I can't do it a third time. Miss Quaile, I must leave him to you."

I went in. He was in the wing chair. He looked up.

"Fenn has been talking nonsense," he said. "He says that Edmund is drowned."

I knelt down by the chair. I had made a rule on the first of October, and I had broken it on the twenty-second, three times, and done a worse thing than break it. I thought that I would mend it now, if it could be mended.

"It's true, Mr Vane. They have found him. He was drowned on Saturday night, or Sunday morning. I am very sorry."

He looked at me for a long moment with his pale eyes.

"No," he said gently. "You're mistaken, my dear. I do not blame you; you were not in the tower. I killed him at ten minutes to twelve. That is quite true. And then the hour was given back, and I went through it again, and I did not. I found him alive at ten minutes to two. I touched his face. He talked to me for a quarter of an hour." He patted my hand. "A man cannot be dead who has been made not to have died. You must see that. It is a matter of simple sequence."

"He was alive when you saw him. I believe that. He died afterward."

"Afterward? Of what?"

"He was drowned."

"But why should he be drowned? He had no reason to go near the water." He frowned. He was working at it, honestly, as he would have worked at a train of wheels that would not mesh. "Unless there was a second debit. I had not thought of that. There was the Jack. I supposed that the Jack had paid for the hour." His face cleared a little. "No. No, I've done the sum too often. It comes out. You will find that he is somewhere about. He'll be in to dinner."

He was not in to dinner. On the Tuesday morning the old man asked me where his son was. I told him. He heard it as though for the first time, and wept, and then, by degrees, remembered that he had put it right, and comforted me.

It went on so all the winter. He asked, perhaps one morning in three. I told him each time that Edmund was dead. He was grieved, and then he corrected me. We were both of us telling the truth as we had it. I have nursed a great many old people who had lost the thread of their lives, and I never had another who held on to two ends so firmly that neither would give.

I came to think, toward the end, that his was the better thread. I had seen a body identified. He had put his hand on a warm face in the dark, after he had struck it, and had been spoken to. I do not know which of those is the stronger evidence.

* * *

There was an inquest, in December. I was called, as the person in charge of the drug.

I said that the bottle had been in my keeping. I said that I had left it on a tray in an unlocked room for three and a half hours, and that when I returned it was gone, and that I did not know who had taken it. The coroner asked whether that had been in accordance with my instructions. I said that it had not. He wrote something down. He was not unkind.

I listened to the doctor. He read from a paper. He said that Mr Edmund Vane had died by drowning and had been alive when he entered the water. There was a fracture on the left side of the brow, made some hours before death by a heavy blunt object of small surface. I looked at my hands. There was a later injury to the back of the head. There were shot wounds in the left shoulder and neck. There was chloral in the blood.

The palms of his hands were torn, the doctor said, and the insides of his arms, and there were splinters of tarred wood in them. It was as though he had held on to something for a long time.

I thought of the black box on its legs in the middle of the Wade. I had passed it on the first of October and had known that someone had once sat in it through a night. I did not know why I thought of it then. I do not know now.

The coroner said that he had never known a man come to his death by so many roads. He said that he could not find how the deceased had got into the sea, and that he hoped somebody's conscience would one day supply what the evidence did not. He looked along the benches.

I looked down. I was not the only one. I have wondered since about the others. I knew what I had to look down for. I had wiped a piece of brass with a handkerchief. I did not know what was on the consciences of the rest, and I have never asked.

* * *

I stayed at Compline through the winter. There was no one else. Mrs Edmund Vane did not return. Mr Kit went back to London, with the girl. Miss Vane came on Sundays from the farm across the water, where she was living by then. She had a look on her face that I could not interpret, happiness and something under it that was not.

In January she went up to the Grey Room by herself. When she had gone I walked along the gallery. The door was locked. Through the keyhole I could see the wardrobe, shut. I do not know whether the gown was back on its hanger. I should think that it was.

I did not look at the portrait any more. I passed it every night, with the old man on my arm, and he would stop, and speak to it. I kept my eyes on the floor. I had no fear of what I should see there. It was only a picture now. She had gone out of it at midnight on the twenty-second, and she did not come back. I think that she had done what she had waited for. Or else I had only then begun to see it as it was.

The tower clock struck the hours all that winter, and never the quarters. One grew used to it. The old man had the Jack down in the Shop, on trestles, and worked on him a little each day. He cut away the shattered wood and let in a new piece, and carved it to match, so that one could hardly find the join. He meant to have him back on his pin by the spring. "She'll be glad of him," he said. "It is dull work, counting alone."

In the evenings, from December, he dictated to me. He said that he wished to leave a true relation of the experiment, for the benefit of whoever should come after. I took it down in exercise books, as he spoke it, and I did not alter a word. I learned from it a number of things about the night that I had not known. I learned what he had meant to do, and what he believed that he had done. It is in his own voice and I shall not repeat it.

I will say that when he came to dictate the part about the ice house, he gave it as he had given it to me in the hall, word for word. He remembered the weight. He remembered that he had hung it up again, and that the nurse had helped him.

"There was blood on it," he said. "I recollect that. I must have wiped it, afterward; I looked at it the next week and it was clean. I have no memory of wiping it. One does these things."

I wrote it down as he said it. My hand did not shake. It ought to have.

The child I never heard again. I went up to the top landing in November, by daylight. The nursery door was locked, as it had been at four o'clock on that afternoon. I looked through the keyhole. The rocking horse stood in the middle of the floor. The bed had been stripped. There was no plate. I stood there for some time. I put my face to the crack of the door, and I thought that there was still, very faintly, the smell of an orange.

* * *

He died in the spring. It was the last Saturday of March.

He had failed all that month. He did not walk any more at night. He lay in the big bed, and I sat by him, and the seven clocks went on. He asked me that afternoon what day it was. When I told him, he smiled.

"Then it is tonight," he said. "They take it back. At one o'clock by this house the rest of England will put its clocks on to two, and be an hour wrong again until October. I shall be sorry to see them go. We have agreed very well this winter, they and I."

"Don't talk, Mr Vane. Rest."

"I've rested for eighty-one years. I was resting while I worked; it is the only rest I ever understood." His voice was a thread. "I should like the window open. I should like to hear the tower."

I opened it. It was a still night, and not cold. One could hear the sea, a long way off.

He slept. He woke at midnight, when the house told it, and listened to the hour go through the rooms. He woke again at the half.

"Is Margot come in?" he said.

I had my answer ready. I had had it ready for six months.

"She's dead, Mr Vane. She was drowned. But she had turned back. She was coming home to you."

"Yes," he said. "Yes. That is right. Thank you. I had it, and it went round the corner." He lay quiet. "And Edmund?"

I took his hand. I did not know what I should say until I had said it.

"You found him in the tower. He was alive. You touched his face."

"I did. He was warm." He sighed. "You see, you do believe it. You've come round. I knew that you would. You are too truthful to hold out for ever against a fact."

He did not speak again. At five minutes to one, by the seven clocks, his breathing changed. I knew the change. I held his hand and counted. It was very easy. There was a long breath, and no other.

I looked at the dial of the nearest clock. It was three minutes to one. In three minutes the world would move its hands and leave that house behind for another seven months. He had gone while they still agreed. He had always hated to be made late.

I closed his eyes. I folded his hands. Then I did what he would have wished, since there was no one else in the house who knew the custom.

I stopped the clocks.

I began with the seven in his room. I opened each case and laid my finger against the pendulum until it was still. I went out into the passage, and along the gallery, and down the stairs. I took a lamp. I went into every room. I did not know how to stop some of them; I had to search for the way. There were clocks that I had never noticed, in cupboards, and on the backs of doors. It took me until it was light.

I counted as I went. I had been told not to. There were a hundred and ten.

The last was the regulator in the hall. I opened the long door. The weight hung at the end of its line, bright and clean. I put my hand on the pendulum and held it.

Then I stood in the hall of Compline House and listened.

I had not heard silence since the first of October. I had forgotten what it was. It came down through the house from the top, room by room, as the hours used to. It was like being let out of something. And I thought that I could hear, under it, how large the place was, and how empty, and how the sea lay all round.

The tower clock struck. I had not stopped that one; I had not the strength to wind it down. The great bell sounded once across the yard. One o'clock. By the time the note had died it was two in every other house in England.

She was still counting, out there. I do not know to what.

* * *

That is all. I left Compline a week after the funeral. Before I went I took the red book from the gilt clock, since he had said that I might do as I thought right. I have it here. I have not yet decided what is right.

I went home to my mother. I found that I could not sleep at night, or by day either. In June they brought me to this place.

Dr Imrie has read the three books as I finished them. He has asked me a question, and I have promised to answer it in writing, at the end, as honestly as I am able.

He asked what I believe that I saw.

I believe that there was a child in the house. I heard her, or him, and I touched the bed. A woman I respect has told me there was not. I cannot make those agree, and I have stopped trying.

I believe that a man fell from the tower at one o'clock. I have seen what they brought in. I know what a jury would say. I say that someone held the lantern.

I believe that I heard Edmund Vane's voice in the clock chamber forty minutes after I saw him fall. When I called his name, he did not answer me.

And the gallery. I know whose dress lay on the bed. I know whose hair was in the brush. I have had a letter. I have set all that down, and hidden nothing, and I would say every word of it on oath.

I do not say that there are ghosts. I have never said so. I am a nurse, and I was taught to report what I observe and to leave the diagnosis to those who are qualified.

I say that I saw her.

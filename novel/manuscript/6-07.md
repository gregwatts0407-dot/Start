## VII. Results, and the Decay of the Record

§ 68. I have set down the experiment and its result. An honest paper does not end there. It must give what was observed afterward, and in particular whatever tells against the conclusion. I shall do so in the order in which the facts came to me.

Upon the morning of the twenty-third I went down to the Shop at nine. I had an appointment there with my son.

He did not keep it.

I waited until a quarter to eleven. I worked at a small plate. Miss Quaile stood by the steps. At a quarter to eleven I said that he was not coming, and she gave me his message, which she had from him the day before: that he was sorry, and that it was not the clock. She had the book of which he had spoken. It was a diary of my wife's for the year of her death, which she had hidden in the French clock upon her desk, the one that she would never let me mend.

I did not read it. I should like that noted. I held it in my hand. I knew the hand upon the cover. I had been told three times that it was not the clock, and I had been told that she turned back. I found that I did not require to know anything more. If she had wished me to read what she thought of me in her last year she would have left it upon my bench, and not inside the one case in the house that I was upon my honour not to open.

I told the girl to put it back.

I was not then uneasy as to Edmund. He had said that he had things to do before morning. He has never been punctual.

§ 69. At noon they brought the Jack into the hall upon a hurdle.

He had been found in the outfall of the stable fleet. He had upon him my old dinner jacket, which the children had put on him for the night. He had been shot. I examined the wound. It was a charge of number five, both barrels or near it, at about thirty yards, from below. It had carried away the front of his chest to the heart-oak and sheared him from his pin.

I must here revise an entry.

I recorded, at § 46, that at the instant of reversal there were two reports from the foot of the tower, which I took for the take-up bursting under load; and that a figure was thrown from the leads, which I took for the price of the hour.

It now appeared that the reports were those of a shotgun, and that the figure was the Jack. Some person had been in my stable yard at one o'clock in the morning and had fired at my tower.

I do not know who. I have asked. I am told that it was a man after duck. There are no duck upon my stable roof.

I ask the reader to observe that this does not affect the result. It affects only my account of the mechanism. I had supposed that the engine burst of itself. It seems that it was broken from without; some of the shot had entered the cupboard, and cut the rod, and I have since taken eleven pellets out of the door. But it was broken at the stroke. I have Jenny's arm for that; she had not finished her blow. Whatever hand fired that gun, it fired it at the single instant in twenty years at which there was something in that chamber to be let go.

I have turned this over. I am inclined to think that an hour, like a tide, will find its own way out. If I had not provided a means of release it would have made one.

I have the Jack upon trestles in the Shop. He can be mended. I had a baulk of the same oak, that was his brother's.

§ 70. Upon the Sunday evening my daughter Imogen came across from the farm.

She told me that she was to marry young Garrow. I was glad of it. I had given them the ground the night before, and it is as well that someone should have it who knows what to do with sheep.

She brought me a ring.

It was my wife's wedding ring. I had it made in Hatton Garden in the spring of 1948, by a man called Abrahams, of twenty-two carat gold, which is too soft, and wears. It had worn to a wire. I knew it by the mark inside.

I had believed that she was buried in it. I did not look, at the time. I could not.

Imogen said that Edmund had given it to her, in the scullery passage, after two o'clock that morning. He had told her that their mother took it from her finger, upon the Wade, on the night of her death, and put it into his hand, and said, 'Give that to your father.' He had carried it, Imogen said, for twenty years.

That was the thing that he had not given me. He had said so in the tower. *There's a thing I've not given you.*

I put it on. It goes upon the smallest finger of my left hand. I have it there as I dictate.

I enter it under the results, and I shall say why.

My wife took off that ring upon the Wade, in the Odd Hour of 1974, and sent it to me. It did not arrive. It was kept back, by the messenger, for reasons that I understand, in a waistcoat pocket, for twenty years. And upon the night of the experiment, within one hour of the reversal, it was let go. It came to me by way of a woman who had crossed the same water, in the same gown, at an hour that I had given her.

The reader may call that a coincidence. I am a mechanic and I do not believe in them. When I find two wheels turning at the same rate I look for the arbor that joins them.

It took her twenty years to come back across that hall. It took her ring a day longer. But both came.

§ 71. I come to the entry that I have been putting off.

Upon the Monday afternoon Fenn came into the drawing room and shut the door. He told me that my son was dead. He said that he had been found that day upon the Ray Sand, by a man digging bait, and that he had been drowned.

I told him that he was mistaken.

I am aware of how that will read. I ask to be heard. I did not say it from weakness. I said it as I should say it to an apprentice who brought me a movement and told me that it had stopped, when I could see the scape wheel turning.

I had killed my son. I recorded it, with the time. I then passed through the hour again, and found him living. I had my hand upon his face. I counted his pulse. He spoke to me for a quarter of an hour, in sentences, and wept, and made a joke about rats. He put a key into the pocket of his coat. All this was after the stroke. It was upon the second passage, which is the one that stands.

A man cannot be dead who has been made not to have died. It is a matter of sequence.

Fenn told me again. He is an old friend. He was very much distressed. Then he went out, and Miss Quaile came and knelt by my chair and told me the same thing, in her own way, which is without ornament. She said that he had been alive when I saw him, and had died afterward.

I asked of what.

She said that he was drowned.

I asked why he should be drowned. He had no reason to go near the water. He had told me what he meant to do: to see his sister, and to watch a boat. He was to come to me in the Shop.

She could not tell me.

§ 72. I have had that conversation, I am informed, a great many times since. Miss Quaile says that I ask her where my son is upon about one morning in three. She tells me. I am grieved; and then I recollect the tower, and I correct her. She does not give way and nor do I.

I should like to set down, while I have it clear, what I think is the true account of the matter. I have reached it slowly.

I said at the beginning that nothing is had for nothing. I believed, upon the night, that the price of the hour had been the Jack. It was a comfortable belief. He is of oak.

But I see now that I was careless in my reckoning. I did not ask for one thing of that hour. I asked for two.

I had set the engine to give my wife the tide. That was the purpose for which it was built, and that was granted. A woman crossed.

And then, at ten minutes past twelve, with my son lying dead and the girl's wrists in my hands, I asked for a second thing. I asked that the blow should be taken back. I had not designed for it. I put it upon the engine as an afterthought, as a man throws another parcel upon a cart that is already loaded.

That was granted also. I found him living.

Two things were given. A crossing upon the Wade, safe. And a life.

I do not think that a wooden figure pays for that.

I have gone over it as I should go over a train that will not run true, wheel by wheel. And I find that the account balances in only one way. A woman was sent across that water who, in the hour as it first stood, was not to have crossed. And a man went into that water, before morning, who was not to have gone.

It was the same water. It was the same night of the year. He was her son.

I do not say that I understand it. I say that it is what the figures show. I asked the hour to give me back a wife from the Wade, and it did; and the Wade was owed one. I asked it to give me back a son, and it did; and I had him for a quarter of an hour, which was the first quarter of an hour that I ever had him. And then the difference was taken up.

I told him that a man is not hanged for what is done in a passage that has been gone through again. I was wrong. Nothing is forgiven. It is only moved.

§ 73. I shall be asked whether, knowing this, I would do it again.

I have thought about that upon a number of nights. I have the ring upon my finger while I think.

Before the experiment I had a wife who had drowned leaving me, by my own clock, and a son who had not spoken to me in twenty years.

After it I have a wife who turned back; and a son who told me why, and wept, and let me put my hand upon his head. I have a daughter across the water with a man who can tell the tide by the smell. I have paid Garrow. I have said that the train was better than Thorne's.

And my son is drowned. They tell me so.

I do not know how to weigh those. I am not a man who has ever known how to weigh such things. I can tell you the mass of a pendulum bob to the grain.

I think that if he were asked he would say that it was a fair exchange. He said in the tower that he had been waiting twenty years for somebody to hit him. I think that he had been waiting for more than that.

But I should not do it again. I record that. I should let the hour go by my door.

§ 76. I must put on record that the gallery clock has ceased to gain. I timed it against the regulator through the whole of November. It keeps within two seconds in the week.

It gained four seconds a week for thirty years. I never found the cause. Now it does not.

I asked Mrs Pask whether anyone had touched it. She said that nobody had, and then she sat down upon the stairs and put her apron over her face.

§ 77. I am not certain of the date. Miss Quaile says that it is the nineteenth of January. I have been in bed some days.

I wish to enter a correction to § 9. I stated there that I told my wife I had put the clocks back, and that she said, 'Oh, Leo. You would.' I have recollected the rest.

She was at her glass. She had the grey silk on, and was putting up her hair. She looked at me in the mirror. She said, 'Oh, Leo. You would.' And then she turned round upon the stool, which she did not often do, and looked at me directly. She said:

'Is there anything that you want to ask me? Before we go down?'

I said that I did not think so. I said that I had the Dent to finish.

She said, 'No. Well.' She turned back to the glass.

I have had that for twenty years and did not know that I had it. It was under the other. I think that the engine shook it loose.

That was the Hour, do you see. She had brought it upstairs. She was giving it to me early, by myself, at six o'clock, in her own room, where nobody should hear. She had her hand out.

I had the Dent to finish.

§ 78. Is Margot come in?

Miss Quaile says no. She says that she is dead, and was drowned, and that she had turned back.

Yes. I had that. I thank her.

§ 79. I wish to say something about the child.

I have recorded her twice as an anomaly. I have been thinking of her. I do not sleep a great deal now, and I go over the observations.

She was seen by me in the yard at eight minutes to twelve, upon the first passage. She was seen by my son in the stable at about a quarter to one, and gave him a handkerchief. She was heard, I believe, in the weight closet at five minutes to two. She was of nine or ten years, dark, in a coat too large. She did not speak to me. She looked at what I had in my hand.

There is no such child. I have enquired of everyone.

I have a hypothesis. I put it forward with diffidence.

When a movement is running, there is at each instant one tooth of the scape wheel that is neither locked nor free. It is upon the edge of the pallet, in the act of dropping. We call it the tooth in action. Everything else in the clock is either before or after. That one is now.

I think that an hour which is gone through twice must have, somewhere in it, something that is gone through only once. There must be a fixed point for the rest to turn about, or there could be no saying that it was the same hour. Something must stand in the yard and see both passages, and not be changed by either.

I think that she was that. I do not know what she was otherwise. But I think that when I came out of the ice house upon the first passage, with what I had done upon my hand, there was required to be someone who saw it; who would go on having seen it, whatever the engine did afterward. Else I should have got off too lightly.

She was not afraid of me. I remarked that at the time. She was afraid for him.

If there was such a person, I should like her to know that I am sorry she was made to look.

§ 80. Edmund has not been to see me.

I asked Miss Quaile whether he was away. She told me.

I said that it could not be so. I said that I had been through it twice.

She said, 'Yes, Mr Vane.' She did not say it as the others say it. She has stopped arguing with me upon that head. She says that I found him in the tower, alive, and touched his face. She says that she believes it.

I asked her when she had begun to.

She said that she did not know.

§ 81. I am going to dictate this section slowly. I have asked Miss Quaile to write it large. It is the twenty-third of February and I am clear. I have been clear since six this morning, and I think that I shall not be so again for very long together. I can feel the pivots.

I said at the head of this paper that I had three reasons for making it and that I should give the third at the end.

I shall first say what I know. I have not said it before, and I do not wish it thought that I did not know it.

I know what the Act is. It is a rule about the numbers upon a dial. I know that at two o'clock in the morning of the twenty-third of October every clock in England was put back by its owner's hand, or would be when he woke, and that nothing whatever was reversed but some millions of pieces of brass. I have said so myself for fifty years, in this house, to anyone who would listen. No hour was taken in March. None was returned. Mr Fenn was right. It is a manner of speaking.

I know that two brass rods and a tide dial do not make a road into the past. I cut them. I know what they are. A differential subtracts one rate of turning from another, and that is all that it does.

I know that the woman who came down my stairs was my daughter. I knew it when she said 'Papa.' She is a brave girl. She did for me what I had not the courage to ask of anyone, and she has not once reproached me with having let her.

And I know that I struck my son upon the head with a piece of lead, in an ice house, because he laughed at me. I know that he fell, and that I left him there for dead and pulled the gate shut. And I know that he lived through it by the thickness of his skull, and by the kindness of a cook and a young fool with a wheelbarrow, and by no engine of mine. He was not given back to me. He had never gone. I found him in the tower because he had climbed there, upon his own legs, with his head broken.

I have known all this upon certain days. I knew it, I think, upon the Tuesday after. I have known it on perhaps one day in four. Miss Quaile will bear me out that upon those days I have been very quiet.

On the other days I have known the other thing.

§ 82. I have set down the other thing. I have dictated it upon forty evenings, as carefully as I could, with numbered sections. I have not altered a word of it today, though I might.

I shall say why, and then I have done.

I told the reader, at § 5, that a regulator and a sundial may stand side by side and disagree by sixteen minutes, and each be right. The one tells the time that we have agreed upon, which is even, and useful, and by which the trains run. The other tells where the sun is. The difference is called the equation of time. It is not an error in either instrument. It is a real quantity. It can be tabulated.

There is an account of that night by which the trains run. It is the one that I have given in the section before this. A sick old man struck his son; a daughter put on a dress; a clock was moved from a library to a tower; somebody fired a gun. I do not dispute a line of it.

And there is the other account, which is this paper.

I have asked myself which of them is true. I have been a servant of the true time all my life. I stopped my house for it.

And I find that I cannot say that the first is true and the second false. For consider what the first leaves out.

In the first account, my wife is dead, and did not come down the stairs. But I gave her the tide. I said the words. I had carried them twenty years, and they are not in me now; I have looked. They were said, and something received them.

In the first account, I never struck my son a second time, since there was no second time. But I knelt in front of him in the tower, and I had the weight of what I had done upon me, entire, and he was within reach of my hand. I put the hand upon his head. I do not know what else to call that but a passage gone through again, and done differently.

In the first account no hour was returned. But I had from one o'clock until two, that night, in which I said four things that I had not said in a lifetime. Where did I get it? It was not upon any of my clocks. I have a hundred and eleven of them and they had none of them shown it to me before.

I think that my wife knew what the Act was as well as I did. She was not a fool about time. She knew that the clocks were only put back. And she stood in my hall, every year, and told her family that an hour had been given to them. She said it so that they would behave as though it had. And when they did, it had.

I think that is the equation. I think that there are two times in a house: the one that the regulator keeps, and the one in which a man may turn round and ask his wife a question. They do not run at the same rate. For most of the year they are sixteen minutes apart, or sixteen years. And upon certain nights, by an old custom, or the kindness of a woman, or the accident of somebody else's gun, they stand for an hour together, and what is done in the one is done in the other.

I did not build an engine that does that. I see that today. But I built something, with a great deal of labour, that made me believe for one night that it could be done. And believing it, I did it.

So I shall let the relation stand as I dictated it. It is not what the world's clocks would say. It is where the sun was.

§ 83. The third reason.

I said that I should give it if I had not forgotten. I have not forgotten. I have held on to it today with both hands.

I have made this paper for my son.

I am aware that he is dead. I am clear today, and Miss Quaile has told me, and I recollect Fenn's face. I know also that tomorrow, or the day after, I shall ask where he is, and wonder that he does not come.

That is the reason. I shall not always know it. And I find that I cannot bear that there should be nowhere in this house, when I have gone, where it is written down in my hand that I said what I said to him in the tower. He may not have believed me. He had not much cause to believe a kind word from that quarter. I gave him a quarter of an hour, after fifty-two years, and he went from it into the sea.

So I put it here, at the foot, where it cannot leak.

Edmund Vane cut the lunar train of the Copy of the Thorne Sidereal between the last week of November 1973 and the eighth of February 1974, in the Shop at Compline, with his own hands, unaided, at the age of thirty-one. I have examined it under a glass of ten diameters. It is better than the original. It is the finest piece of wheel-cutting that I have seen in sixty-six years at the bench. I was his master. I said at the time that it would serve.

I was wrong, and I knew it, and I say so.

I ask whoever comes after to see that the Copy is found. Mrs Edmund Vane took it away with her, I am told, in a motor car, and sold it. I do not want it back. But if it is ever opened, I should like the person who opens it to look at the moon work, and to know whose it is.

§ 84. I have some instructions.

The Thorne is to go to the Museum, as in my will. The key is lost. A new one may be cut; there is a drawing in the bench, top drawer, left.

The Jack is mended. He is upon the trestles. He is to be put back upon his pin when the weather is fit. It wants two men and a rope. I do not like to think of her striking by herself.

When I am dead the clocks are to be stopped. All of them. I should be obliged if Miss Quaile would do it; she knows where they are. The pendulums are to be held with the hand and not with a feather. They need not be started again. I have been listening to them for a long time.

To Miss Quaile I leave nothing, since I have nothing that she would want, and Fenn tells me that I gave the best of it away in one night. But I record that she did not lie to me, except three times, and that I knew of each, and that upon consideration I think that she was right in all three.

§ 85. One thing more, for whoever reads this.

The hour is given back every year. I have said so, and I have said also that I know it is not. Take it in whichever sense you can.

It comes in the small hours, upon the last Saturday night of October. You will be asleep. Nearly everyone is. You will wake in the morning and find that you have been given something, and you will put the kettle on.

I should advise you to be awake for it. Open a door. There will very likely be somebody in your house to whom you have not said a thing that you ought. You need no engine. I spent five years upon one, and it was shot to pieces at the first stroke, and I got through the whole of what I had to say without it.

Ask the question. Whatever it is. She is standing behind you at the gallery clock. She has been there five minutes.

Turn round.

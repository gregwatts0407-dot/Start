## 3. Which One O'Clock

After the lights went out there is much less in the notebook. I had the torch, but I did not dare to use it where anyone might see, and I wrote most of what follows crouched on the back stairs, by feel, in letters an inch high. Some of it I cannot now read. I shall give what there is, and fill in the rest from memory. I have not forgotten any of it. I could not if I tried.

*11.20. All the lights went out. The piano stopped and then went on. I was behind the curtin in the long room. It is VERY dark. All I can here is clocks.*

*11.24. Suspect No. 1 has come out of the room with the fire. He has a lamp. He is going to the libary.*

I went down the main stairs behind him on my hands and seat, a step at a time. The hall was empty. He had left the library door open.

He had set his lamp on the table in the window. There was a wooden box on the floor that I had not seen before, a tea chest, with a blanket in it. He took the green cloth off the big clock. He opened the glass.

He did not lift it out at once. That is what I remember. He stood in front of it for quite a long time, with his arms hanging. Then he put out his hand and laid it flat against the dial, the way you would touch the face of somebody who was asleep.

He said something. I was at the door and could not hear it.

Then he was quick. He had it out, and into a pillowcase, and into the blanket, and the lid on, and a cord round. I watched him tie the knot. I have never seen hands move like that. They did not seem to belong to the rest of him, which was swaying.

He put the cloth back over the empty glass. He picked up the box. I got behind the hall table.

*11.30. He has TAKEN THE BIG CLOCK. In a box. He went out the side door. The girl with no hair held it open for him. He said thank you Celia. She is not Celia. She has the same DRESS as Celia. I notised that at dinner. There are 2 green dresses and I dont think the grownups have counted.*

That is in the notebook. I want it on the record, since a detective sergeant of the Metropolitan Police went to his grave not having put it together. I had it by half past eleven. I was not cleverer than Mr Cade. I had simply not been hit on the head yet, and I was at the height where one notices boots.

I followed Edmund Vane out into the rain.

It was the first time I had been out of doors. The wind nearly took me off the step. I had my blue coat, that Mrs Pask had found for the funeral, which came down past my knees, and my canvas shoes. I kept the torch in my pocket. He had a lamp, and I had only to stay behind it.

He went along the front of the stables, with the box against his chest. I heard the little bell on the tower ring twice over my head, and I looked up, and saw the two wooden people in their clothes, and did not like them.

He went down a track into some trees. There was a hump there, with a door in it. He went in. I stood behind a trunk.

And I saw that I was not the only one.

*11.36. There is SOMEBODY ELSE. Under a holly bush. A man with a hat. He has a lamp but its turned right down. It is the DETECTIVE. He is following Suspect No. 1.*

*So it goes: Suspect. Then Detective. Then me.*

*He doesnt know Im here.*

I have Mr Cade's account of those five minutes. He says that he had rain down his collar and a twig in his ear, and that he had spent some of the best years of his life like that. I was eleven feet behind him. I could see the back of his neck.

Edmund came out without the box. He stopped on the track and looked back at the house. He said, "Sorry, Ma."

I heard that. The detective heard it too; he has written it down. He could make nothing of it. I could not either, then. I thought only that it was a queer thing for a thief to say.

When Edmund had gone the detective went into the hump. He was in there a long time. I crept to the gate. There was a passage, and a lamp at the end of it, and I could see him on his knees with the lid off the box, looking in. His face was lit from underneath. He looked for a moment like a man who is very hungry and has been shown food. Then he put the lid back. He tied the cord again. He did it badly.

He came out, and I got behind the mound. He went off through the trees toward the sea.

I went in myself, when he had gone. I had the torch. It was colder in there than any place I have ever been, and there were steps going down into the dark. I tried to lift the box. I could not move it an inch.

*11.50. The box is in an ICE HOUSE (it has ice in). I cant lift it. I am going to tell Mrs Pask in the morning where it is so she can tell the Old Man and he can have his clock back.*

*That is the case SOLVED. Suspect No. 1 did it and I know where the loot is.*

*I am wet. I am going in.*

I thought that it was over. I went back to the house quite pleased with myself.

* * *

There is a thing that happened next which belongs to somebody else, and I have asked his leave to tell it.

I went in by the side door. I was in the passage by the gunroom, taking my shoes off to carry them, when the door banged open behind me and someone came in out of the rain, running.

It was the boy who played the piano. I flattened myself against the coats.

He did not see me. He would not have seen a horse. He was as white as paper, and his mouth was open, and he was making a sound in his chest like a dog that has been running. He got as far as the gun rack and held on to it. Then he was sick on the floor.

A door opened further up, and the girl with no hair came down the passage with a candle. She said, "Kit? Kit, what is it? Where've you been?"

He said, "I've killed him."

"Killed who?"

"The man. Sears's man. The one with the face. He was in the tack room. He was waiting for Dad." His teeth were chattering. "I hit him with my bat. From school. It was on the wall. I only meant to—he went down, Sass. He just went down. There's blood. He isn't moving."

"Oh my God."

"I told him to leave Dad alone. And then I ran." He put his hands over his face. "I've killed a man with a cricket bat."

She got hold of him. She was a year or two younger than he was, with a ring in her eyebrow, and she took charge of him as though he were six. She said that he was not to tell anyone. She said that they would go and look, together, in a minute, when he could stand. She took him away up the passage with her arm round his waist.

*12.14. The piano boy says he has KILLED A MAN in the stable with a bat. He thinks it was a bad man come for his dad. I think it was the DETECTIVE.*

*I am going to look.*

I know that I should not have. I was nine. But I ask you to consider what I had been reading for a year. In those books, when somebody says there is a body, the girl goes to see. And I had a reason that was better than the books. If the detective was dead, there was nobody left who knew about the box but me.

I put my shoes on again. I went back across the yard.

There is a door at the foot of the tower. It was open. Inside there was a strong smell of paraffin and something else, sharp. I shone the torch.

He was lying on his face on some horse blankets. His hat was beside him. There was blood on the back of his head, and a lamp on the floor, broken. A cricket bat lay just inside the door.

I had seen a dead person. I had seen my mother. I knew that you cannot tell by looking.

I knew what to do. It was in the fourth book. You hold something shiny to their mouth. If they are alive it goes misty.

I had my watch. I took it off. I got down on the floor and held the glass of it under his nose, with the torch on it.

It went misty. It cleared, and went misty again.

*12.22. He is NOT DEAD. I did the mirror test with my watch. He is breathing. He is asleep with a bump. He smells of wisky.*

*I put a blanket on him. I cant do anything else. He is too big.*

*The piano boy has NOT killed anybody. Somebody should tell him.*

Nobody did. I should have run after the pair of them. I was afraid of being caught.

Kit Vane went back to that harness room twenty minutes later with the girl, to look at his dead man, and found a broken lamp and some blood, and no one. Mr Cade had got up at a quarter to one and walked out. Kit did not know what to think. He came into the hall for the ceremony with hay in his hair, holding on to the stairs. He watched the baize door open, and a young man come through it carrying a box, and he thought that it was the body.

He spent the whole of that night not knowing whether he had killed a moneylender's man or not, or where the corpse had gone. In the morning he asked the girl who waited at table, three times, whether the gentleman from the insurers had been at breakfast. When she said that Mr Cade had driven off at six with his mother, he sat down on the stairs and laughed until he cried. Hester saw him do it, and could not think why.

He told me this himself, in the hall at Compline, thirty years later. He is fifty-four. He writes music for films, in Berlin, and has a way of keeping his hands in his pockets.

"I've never hit anyone since," he said. "Not once. I can't even kill a wasp."

I showed him the page. *I did the mirror test with my watch.* He read it twice.

"You were nine," he said. "You went into a dark stable by yourself to see if a man was dead. And you thought to check that he was breathing."

"It was in a book."

"There was a detective sergeant on that island," Kit said. "There was a trained nurse. There were about fourteen adults. Do you know how many of us, that night, came on a body and bent down to see whether it was breathing?"

I did know. I had the sheet of lining paper.

"One," I said. "Toby. And he only did it because Hester had told him how."

* * *

I am coming now to the thing I saw. I have been a long time getting to it.

I went back into the house. I meant to go up to the nursery and stay there; I had had enough. I had got to the first landing when I heard voices below, in the room with the fire. A man's voice said that he was going to have a look at the generator.

It was Edmund.

I thought: he is going for the box. He does not know the detective has been at it. He does not know the detective is lying in the stable with a bump.

I thought: I said that I would watch him.

There are two ways down from that landing. The main stairs come out in the hall, in the light. The back stairs come out by the gunroom. To get from the one to the other on that floor you have to go the whole length of the long room with the picture.

I ran. I ran down it in my socks, with my shoes in my hand, as hard as I could go, past all the clocks, to the far end where the lady was. I did not think about who was underneath.

Two women in the drawing room heard me. One of them was sure that it was a ghost, and went upstairs to meet it. The other went and put on a dead woman's dress. I have had to get used to that. A good deal of what happened in that house after half past eleven happened because a child ran along a floor.

*12.33. He has gone out the side door. I am behind him. It has stopped raining nearly.*

*12.34. THE OLD MAN HAS COME OUT TOO.*

I had not expected that. I had got out of the door and into the shadow of the wall, ten yards behind Edmund's lamp, and I heard the door open again at my back.

He came out slowly. He had no coat and no hat. His hair was white in the dark. He walked straight past me, a yard away, and never turned his head. He was looking at the lamp ahead.

He was carrying something in his right hand. It hung down by his knee on a short string. I put the torch on it for one second, behind my fingers. It was a round metal thing about the size of a tin of beans.

I did not know what it was. I thought it might be a lantern that had gone out.

So there were three of us again, going across the yard. The son in front. Then the father. Then me. Nobody was following me, I am fairly sure, though by that point I would not have been surprised.

They went down the track. Edmund went into the hump, and a minute later the old man went in after him.

I did not go to the gate. I do not know why. I think that I had begun to be frightened. I stood among the trees, a little way off, where I could see the arch.

I could hear them. It was only voices at first, one low and one loud. I could not make out any of it. Then the loud one got louder, and I began to catch words.

*12.44. They are having a FIGHT. In the ice house. Not hitting. Shouting. It is the Big Man shouting. The Old Man is quiet.*

*He said IT WILL SERVE. He said it twice like it was a bad word.*

*He said SHE WAS LEAVING YOU.*

*The Old Man said something.*

*Big Man LAFFED. It was horrible.*

*He said IT WAS NEVER THE*

That is where the entry stops. The pencil goes off the edge of the page.

I heard the laugh. I have heard it described by a nurse who never heard it and a father who heard it too well, and by my own father, who heard another like it an hour later from the top of a tower. It was not a happy sound. It went on and on. And through it I heard him shout. *It was never the clock. It was never the bloody clock.*

Then there was a small noise. It was like somebody knocking once on a door.

The laughing stopped.

There was a crash, and another, a wooden thing falling. Then something else fell. It was heavier, and it went on for longer. I could count it. It hit, and hit, and hit, twelve times, and then it was quiet.

I stood under the trees with the notebook against my chest. I knew that sound. A year before, in a rented house in Pincher Creek, my mother had got up in the night, when she ought not to have, and had fallen down the stairs. There were nine of them. I was in bed. I had counted.

Nothing happened for what seemed a very long time. I heard water dripping.

Then I heard the old man's voice. He was calling a name. He said it over and over, quite gently, as you would to somebody you wanted to wake.

Nobody answered him.

A light came toward the arch, and went out. I heard him feeling along the wall. He came out through the gate. He turned, and took hold of it, and pulled it shut behind him, and it made a clang, and a click.

He stood there. He lifted his right hand and looked at what was in it.

I ran. I ran up the track ahead of him. I got as far as the corner of the stable wall and my legs would not go any further. I stood against the bricks.

He came up out of the trees. He was walking very slowly. He came round the corner, and he saw me.

I have his account of that. He says that I was looking at what he had in his hand. I was. The moon had come out. It was a grey metal weight on a string, and one side of it was dark, and shining, and wet.

He says that I was not afraid of him. That is true as well. I do not know why I was not. He was a very old man with white hair, in evening clothes, in the rain, and he had just done something at the bottom of some steps. But he looked at me with such a puzzled, gentle face. He looked like somebody who has lost his way in his own house.

He said, "Go in, Immy. You will catch cold."

He thought that I was his little girl.

I could not speak. I wanted to say, *you have to go back, he's hurt, you have to fetch somebody.* It would not come out. I turned round and ran.

* * *

I do not remember crossing the yard, or the stairs. I remember that I was in the nursery, with the door shut behind me, and that I had my back against it, and that something was coming up through my chest that I could not stop.

It came out. It was one sound. I got the eiderdown off the bed and pushed it against my mouth with both hands, and stood there, and shook.

*There is no child at Compline. I heard a child cry out in the old nursery at five minutes to twelve.*

Hester has that right to the minute. It was five to one by my watch. I looked, afterward.

She has written that it was not a cry of pain, nor for a mother. She says that it was the sound a child makes who has seen a thing done. She was a nurse, and she had it exactly.

But she thought that she knew whose voice it was. She knelt on that floor, with my drawing in her hand, and decided that it was a boy who had slept in that iron bed forty years before, and had seen something, and was still crying about it. She was wrong about the child. But I have often thought since that she was not very far wrong about the boy.

I heard her on the stairs. She was coming up fast, and saying, "I'm coming, I'm coming."

I did not want to be found. I had sworn, and there was Mrs Pask's place. And I had just seen an old man come out of a hole with blood on a weight. I could not have told it to a stranger in a blue dress. I wanted the only grown-up in that house who knew my name.

I was out of the door and across the landing before she reached the top. I had the hatch open and was in the lift with my knees under my chin. I pulled the rope. I went down into the wall, in the dark, as she came through the nursery door with her lamp.

She says that when she came out again the ropes were trembling. They were. I was on the end of them.

I went down three floors. I was going to the kitchen. I was going to tell Mrs Pask.

And the lift stuck.

* * *

*1.08. I am STUCK in the lift. It is between the pantry and the kitchen. The rope has gone crooked. I have been here for ages.*

*Somebody opened the little door at the bottom. A man. I said Hello. I said please could you pull the other rope.*

*He said IM VERY SORRY I CANT HELP YOU. YOU ARENT THERE.*

*I said I am tho.*

*He shut the door.*

*It was the Pink Man. I know his voice. Why did he say I wasnt there???*

I have read Mr Pargeter's version of that conversation a number of times. It is one of the few things in this entire business that I can think of without any pain at all. He was polite to me. He says that one is not frightened by a thing that is simply not so. He had had a great deal to drink, and a vicar had died on him and risen again, and he had fallen in love on a staircase. When the wall spoke to him, he declined, courteously, to believe it, and went and asked the cook whether the lift generally did.

She came. I heard the bolt. The hatch opened and there was her face, with a candle under it.

I have never been so glad to see anyone. I began to cry properly then.

"Not a sound, I said," Mrs Pask whispered. She was working at the rope. "What did I tell you? Not a sound. And you're talking to the gentry out of a cupboard. Wait till I get you home."

"Mrs Pask—"

"I'll skin you. I'll skin you and hang you on the line. That nurse has been up to the nursery. She's found your orange peel."

"Mrs Pask, please. There's a man."

"There's a dozen men, and none of them's to know you're here. Hold still." She had got the lift down level with the hatch. She put her arm in and felt me all over, quickly, as she would a hen, to see that I was whole. "You're soaked. You've been outdoors. Wren Garrow, you have been out of this house."

"The old man hit Mr Edmund."

She stopped.

"What?"

"The old man. With the white hair. He hit Mr Edmund with a thing on a string. In the ice house. He's fallen down the steps. He's at the bottom and he isn't moving." It came out of me all in one piece. "He called him and he didn't answer. The old man shut the gate and came away."

I could see her face. It had gone quite still.

I had expected her to shout for somebody. I had expected her to run. That is what I wanted. I wanted a grown person to hear what I had seen and take it from me.

"You never saw that," said Mrs Pask.

"I did. I did see it. I heard him fall. I counted."

"You saw nothing. Listen to me." She had hold of my chin. Her hand smelled of goose fat. "There's things go on upstairs in this house that I've made it my business for forty year not to know. I'm not going to start with what a child tells me out of a lift at one in the morning. Mr Vane's eighty-one. He's never raised his hand to a creature in his life. He couldn't hit a clock."

"He had blood on it."

"That's enough."

"But Mr Edmund's lying in the cold!"

She shut her eyes. I saw her breathe.

I understand her now. I did not then, and I hated her. She had been in that house since 1952. She had put that old man's dinners in front of him for forty-two years. She had picked his son up off the Wade, crying, and walked him home, and never told. Everything in her was made for keeping what went on above stairs from coming down. And here was a child she had smuggled in against every rule, telling her that the master had killed his heir with a clock weight.

If she believed me, she would have to raise the house. She would have to say how she knew. *A little girl told me. A Garrow. I hid her in the nursery.* And it would all come out together.

But she could not leave him there. She was not that kind of woman. If there was one chance in fifty that I was telling the truth, Edmund Vane was lying on wet straw at the bottom of twelve steps in a place as cold as a grave.

She opened her eyes.

"He'll be drunk," she said. She was not speaking to me. "That's what it'll be. He's drunk every year on this night. He'll have gone down them steps on his backside and he'll be sat at the bottom singing." She let go of my chin. "It wants somebody to look. That's all. Somebody that can go there natural, and find him, and no questions."

"I could show you."

"You'll show nobody. You'll go up in this lift to the nursery and you'll get in that bed and you'll stay in it, or as God's my witness I'll tan you so you can't sit." She was already pulling the rope. "I've the ice to send for. I've had it out there since the lights went. It wants fetching for one o'clock."

The lift went up. Her face went down below me, and the light with it.

I heard her go back into the kitchen. I heard her say, in quite a different voice, loud and comfortable:

"There. All it wanted was a firm hand. Now then. If you've nothing to do but sit there eating my stock, you can run me an errand."

* * *

That is why Toby Pargeter was sent for the pudding.

He has wondered about it. He says in his memoir that she looked up at the clock on the wall, and then at him, and that he saw her come to some decision; he thought that she wanted him out of the kitchen. She did. But she wanted him somewhere in particular. She gave him her own keys, with the red wool on the one for the gate. She told him to mind the steps. She told him that the box would be at the bottom.

The bombe had been in the ice since half past ten. She had no need of it for forty minutes. Gail could have gone, when she had her shoes on.

She sent him at twenty-five past twelve by the kitchen clock. That clock was eight minutes fast. It was seventeen minutes past by the house, and seventeen minutes past one by my watch. I had told her at eleven minutes past.

She took six minutes to think of a way. That is all. In six minutes she had found a means to get a man down those steps with a candle, on an honest errand, who would come back and tell her what was at the bottom. She did it without leaving her kitchen or admitting that she knew a thing. And when he came running back to say that Mr Edmund was lying there with a lump on his head, she untied her apron and went for the wheelbarrow.

Mr Cade had been at that gate two minutes before. He had put his arm through the bars with a lighter, and looked for the best part of a minute, and gone away to find the wife. He was trained. He was the only person on the island whose job it was. He looked at a man lying on straw and made up his mind, from the door, as he had learned to do in twenty years of looking at the dead.

And a child told a cook, through a hatch in a larder. The cook did not believe her. But she sent a young man with a spoon.

Toby got down those steps and heard him snore. He turned him on his side. He and Dolly Pask got sixteen stone of him up twelve steps and into a barrow and under a horse rug, in the dry.

A doctor told me, years afterward, what would have become of a man with that much chloral in him and a fractured skull, left on his back, head down, on wet straw, in a brick chamber full of ice, for four and a half hours until the road opened. I had not told him why I was asking. He said that the cold alone would have done it by three.

So I am going to claim that. I have little enough to my credit in this story. I saw a man struck, and I ran away, and cried into an eiderdown. But I went down a shaft in the dark to tell somebody, and I made her hear me, and because of it he was lifted out.

Edmund Vane did not die in the ice house. He did not die of his father's blow, or of his wife's bottle. He was carried out of there alive at twenty-three minutes to two in the morning, by a wine merchant and a cook.

It was the first time that night that he was saved. I did not go up to the nursery. I got out of the lift at the little pantry behind the dining room, and went through the dark house to the side door, and followed the young man with the spoon.

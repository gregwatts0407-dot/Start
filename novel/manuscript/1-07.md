## 7. Harwich

I did what I'd said I would do before it was light. I'm putting it down because I said I'd put down all of it.

At about four I went back to the spinney with a stable lantern I'd found and the cook's kitchen saucer in my pocket. The gate was as we'd left it, wide, with her ring of keys hanging. I went down the steps for the third time that night. I took the straw where his head had been and the straw round it, a double armful, and carried it up and out and along to the wall, and threw it into the sea. I went back and scrubbed the bottom step with a handful of sacking and meltwater until the smear was gone and the hairs with it. I raked the rest of the straw about with my foot so that there wasn't the shape of a man in it any longer. Then I broke a few pieces off the ice with the iron bar and let them lie on the wet, where he'd been, to melt.

It took me twenty minutes. I've spent longer destroying evidence that didn't matter. When it was done I stood at the bottom with the lantern and looked round. It was a round brick room with ice in it. There was nothing there to say anyone had died.

I took the keys out of the lock and pulled the gate shut and heard the tongue drop. I hung the ring on its nail by the kitchen range, with the red wool to the front, and stood the saucer on the draining board by the empty dish. The serving spoon I put in the sink.

Nobody saw me. There was a light under the door of the servants' hall, and somebody snoring behind it. Out in the scullery passage, as I came through, I thought I smelled cigarette smoke, fresh, and there was a plate on the kitchen table that hadn't been there at half past two, with some bones on it, and a tumbler. Somebody had been in and eaten cold goose with his fingers. I looked at the plate. I thought, the boy. The boy's come in from wherever he's been hiding and had his supper. I felt sorry for him.

I can tell you to the minute when that plate was used, and who by. I've been told since. I didn't know it then. I rinsed the tumbler under the tap and stood it on the rack with the others. I was tidying up.

* * *

It began to get light about a quarter past six. It was a dirty yellow light, low down in the east behind the stables, and the tower stood up against it. I'd been sitting in the Granada in the yard since half past five with the engine running and the heater on, trying to stop my teeth.

I'd looked up at the tower when I came out. I'll admit I looked. There was one of them up there under the little roof. The woman. She stood on the right of the bell in her painted gown with her hammer up by her shoulder. Somebody's shawl was still on her, soaked, hanging straight down. On the left, where the little man had been in his black jacket, there was a post and a bit of iron sticking up, and the sky.

I sat in the car and looked at that gap. I'd seen something come off that tower at two o'clock, with its arms out. I'd seen where it had come from. I knew there'd been two wooden people up there at midnight and there was one now. All three of those things were in my head together, in the front seat of a Ford. I had an hour to put them side by side.

I don't know what to tell you. I've sat in the day room at the home with the telly on and gone back to that hour in the car, and I've wanted to reach in through the window and take myself by the collar. But I'd seen a face at the bottom of the ice-house steps. I'd seen it by a flame, twelve feet off, and it was a man's, and I knew whose. After that the thing in the creek was Edmund Vane, to me, and it stayed Edmund Vane. Whatever had happened to a wooden dummy on a clock could wait. I think I thought the gun had brought it down. I think I thought, well, somebody shot the Jack, that's another one for the file. I didn't follow it over the edge and ask where it had landed.

That's the last of them. I've given you every chance I had that night to get it right and what I did with each one. There won't be any more.

At ten to six the vicar's Morris came round from the coach house with the nephew at the wheel, crouched over it like a man driving a pram. The old clergyman was in the passenger seat in a rug, asleep still, or again. The nephew saw me and stopped and got out. He came across the yard in the half-dark with his hands in his pockets. He'd lost the hopeful look. He looked about fifteen.

"I say," he said. "Mr Cade. Might I have a word? Only I gather you're by way of being a sort of detective. Kit says. And I think I'd better own up to somebody, and I'd rather it was a professional."

I wound the window down. I thought, here it comes. The box. He's going to tell me where he got the box.

"It was me," he said.

"What was?"

"The clock. What Uncle Leo said at dinner. One of you is robbing me. I mean, it wasn't *robbing*, I never meant to take it out of the house. But there was a little one in my room, on a sort of table, with a handle on top, and I caught it with my elbow, changing. And then I trod on it. Getting it out from under the bed. There was a noise like someone stepping on a budgerigar." He took his hand out of his pocket. He was holding a fistful of brass: a bent plate, some wheels, a spring that had got out and was hanging down like a bit of bracken. "I've had it on me all night. I couldn't think where to put it. I kept thinking he was looking at me. Do you suppose it was one of the good ones?"

I looked at what was in his hand. I'd spent Friday night with photographs of the good ones.

"It's a ten-pound carriage clock," I said. "They sell them in Woolworth's."

"Oh, *thank* God." He actually swayed. "You've no idea. I've been through absolute torment. I nearly confessed during the syllabub."

"I saw."

"Did you? Gosh." He put the wreckage away. He stood there, shifting his feet on the gravel, and didn't go. "There's another thing, actually. Rather worse. I don't know whether I ought to mention it."

"Then don't."

"No. Only I can't find him to apologise, and we're off now, the vicar's got early service, and I shouldn't like him to think I'd just—" He stopped. He looked at the tower, and then at the ground. "It was the wheel, you see. On the cobbles. They aren't built for it."

The Morris's horn went. A thin little parp. The vicar had woken up.

I'd had no sleep and I was waiting for a woman. I had a stolen clock in a blanket on the back seat, because I'd walked into the hall at five and picked it up off the table in front of a sleeping dame and nobody had said a word. I had no room in my head for a boy's second confession about another piece of the furniture.

"Write him a letter," I said.

"Yes. Yes, I suppose I could do that." He looked relieved. "I'll write to Edmund from town. He'll have cooled off by then. Well. Cheerio. I'm awfully glad about the Woolworth's."

He loped back to the Morris and they went off round the end of the stables, toward the Wade, at about nine miles an hour.

I wound the window up. He'd said the name. *I'll write to Edmund.* It went past me. I thought, poor young fool, he doesn't know yet. I watched his tail lights out of sight and I felt sorry for him too. I was sorry for everybody that morning.

* * *

She came at six. She came out of the garden door in a camel coat with a headscarf on, and a small case in her hand, and dark glasses, though the sun wasn't up. She had flat shoes. She got in beside me without a word and looked over her shoulder at the thing on the back seat. Then she faced front.

"Did anyone see you?" I said.

"Mrs Pask. She was lighting the range. She said, 'You're never going out, madam, at this hour.' I said I had a headache and Mr Cade was running me to the chemist's in Maldon."

"On a Sunday."

"She didn't say anything. She just looked at me." Celia Vane folded her gloved hands in her lap. "She had her keys back. She was holding them. She said, 'Somebody's been at my nail.'"

I put the car in gear.

We went out under the arch of the stables and down a track with the sea wall on one hand, and came to the ramp where the Wade went off the island. The tide was a long way out. You wouldn't have known it had ever been in. There was the mud again, miles of it, shining pink and yellow where the light caught it, and the birds walking about. The road lay across it, wet and black.

I drove out onto it. Neither of us spoke. I had it down to fifteen miles an hour, for the weed.

Halfway over we came to the dip, and the plank bridge, and the black box on its legs beside the bridge. I slowed for the planks. I looked at the box as we came up to it, the way you look at anything on an empty road.

The door of it was open. It was swinging a little on its hinges. And the ladder up the post was wrong. I'd noticed that ladder on the way in, and thought somebody should see to it. There'd been rungs all the way down, the bottom ones gone soft. Now there weren't. From about the height of a man's chest to the ground there was nothing but the two uprights, and where each rung had been socketed in there was a stub, snapped off short. The wood in the breaks was white. It hadn't had time to go grey.

On the shingle at the foot of the post, half sunk, was a child's torch. It was a small one, red plastic. The tide had been over it.

I saw that. I say so because it's the one thing I've got to show for twenty years of being trained to see. I saw four broken rungs and a toy torch on the Wade at ten past six on the Sunday morning, and I've never been able to do anything with them. They weren't in my case. I had a dead man and a widow and a clock. There wasn't any child. There'd been no child in that house, and nobody had been out on the road in the night; nobody could have been, there was six feet of water on it. Somebody's kid had dropped a torch there in the summer, and the storm had finished the ladder.

"What is it?" she said. I'd nearly stopped the car.

"Nothing."

I took us over the planks and on.

Where the Wade came up onto the mainland, I looked across at the farm under the wall. There was smoke from the chimney. In the yard stood the grey horse. It had a rug thrown over it and its head was down, and it was wet; its legs were black with it to the belly, and steam was coming off its back in the cold. There was nobody with it. A lamp was burning in a downstairs window of the house.

"Whose is that?" I said.

She turned her dark glasses to it and away.

"The Garrows'," she said. "It's nothing to do with us."

I filed it. For the last time. Then we were up on the wall and through Thorney Staithe, where there wasn't a soul about and the street lights were back on, burning away in the daylight.

* * *

She telephoned from a box outside a shut garage near Manningtree. I sat in the car and watched her do it. She had the number by heart. She talked for about two minutes with her back to me and her head bent. She came out and got in and said, "Parkeston. The car park by the quay. Half past eight."

"That was Sears?"

"That was somebody who said he'd tell him. He rang me back." She took her glasses off and rubbed her eyes. In the daylight she looked every hour of it. "He laughed. He said, 'Tell Mrs Vane Ronnie's always pleased to do business with a lady.'"

"Did you tell him about your husband?"

"I said he'd been taken ill."

"And me?"

"I said a friend was driving me."

I let that go. I said, "When we get there, you stay in the car. I'll hand it over. I want to see his face."

"No. Harry. No. You said you needn't see anyone. He'll know you, you said so, and then it's all—he'll think it's a trap. He'll think you're still a policeman." She put her hand on my arm, the one the boathook had found. "Let me do it. It's my husband's debt. It's the last thing I can do for him. Please."

I'd like to say I argued more than I did.

Parkeston Quay on a Sunday morning in October is a mile of wet tarmac with a railway line down one side and a ferry down the other. The boat was in. She was a big white thing with a blue funnel, and there were lorries going up into the back of her. It was twenty past eight. The car park was half empty. I pulled up at the far end, by a chain-link fence with rags of polythene blown against it, where I could see the way in.

"I could do with a coffee," she said. "I'm so cold. There's a place in the terminal. Would you?"

I looked at her. She looked back. Her eyes were steady and tired and they were thinking about something a little way behind me.

"Two sugars," she said.

Now, I knew. I'm not going to pretend I didn't. I'd sent men for coffee myself. I sat with my hand on the door, and I knew that if I got out of the car and walked two hundred yards to that terminal and stood in a queue, I'd come back to something different.

I've had twenty-five years to ask why I went. I think it's this. I was tired, and it was her husband's clock, and her husband's debt, and her life. If she wanted to do it with me out of the way, then she'd weighed me up and decided, and she'd probably got it right. And there was a bit of me that didn't want to see Ronnie Sears's face after all. I was afraid of what I'd see there. I thought he'd be pleased to see me.

I went and got the coffee. There were five people in the queue. I carried two paper cups back across the car park with the lids on, and I could see from a long way off.

The Granada was where I'd left it. Both the back doors were open. There was nobody in it and nothing on the back seat. Thirty yards off a big dark Jaguar was pulling away toward the exit, taking it slowly over the ramps. I saw the back of a man's head in the rear window, a wide head, with grey hair cut short. I'd have known that head across a football ground. There was a grey blanket beside him on the seat.

She wasn't in the Jaguar.

I found her with my eyes a few seconds later. She was a long way off, at the foot of the passenger gangway, a small figure in a camel coat and a headscarf, with a case. A man in uniform was looking at something she'd handed him. A ticket. He gave it back. She started up the gangway.

She stopped halfway. She turned round, and looked back along the quay at the car park. She'd have seen me. I was the only thing standing still in it, a man by a Ford with a cup in each hand.

She didn't wave. She lifted one hand, in its glove, as far as her shoulder, and held it there. Then she went on up and in through the door in the side of the ship.

I stood there till the coffee was cold. Then I drank both of them, sitting on the bonnet. Hers had the two sugars in.

On the passenger seat, where she'd been, there was an envelope. She'd written *Harry* on the front in eyebrow pencil. There was four hundred pounds inside it in twenties. I counted it. Double what Fenn was paying, she'd said, for one day. She'd kept her word to the pound. There wasn't a note.

*He's quite good at notes*, she had said. She wasn't. I've wondered since whether that was the better way to be.

* * *

They found him on the Monday afternoon. A man digging for bait came on him at low water on the Ray Sand, seven miles down the coast from Compline. I read it in the *Evening Echo* on the Tuesday, half a column on page five. *Body on beach is missing landowner.*

A detective inspector called Brand came down to Leigh to see me on the Thursday. He was a decent enough bloke, young. He didn't know who I was, or he was polite about it. He sat on my one chair and had tea out of my one good cup. I gave him what Fenn and I had agreed to give, which was nothing. Pemberton's, the valuation, the tide. I'd dined, and I had turned in early with a headache; I'd fallen on some steps in the dark when the lights failed, which accounted for the dressing. I'd left at first light. Yes, I'd given Mrs Vane a lift to Harwich. She'd asked me. She said she was going to a sister in Holland for a few days and was upset about a quarrel with her husband. No, I hadn't seen Mr Edmund Vane after the port. He'd been drinking heavily. He'd seemed to have something on his mind.

He wrote it all down. He said, "You were in the job, I believe, Mr Cade?"

"A while ago."

"You'll know I've got to ask. Did you hear anything in the night? Any kind of disturbance?"

"The wind."

"Nothing like a shot?"

I looked at him. "A shot?"

"We've a witness in the village says he heard a gun go off twice, over on the Ness, round about two. Likely nothing. They're always after duck out there." He shut his book. "It's only that the deceased had some pellet wounds. In the shoulder. Fresh."

I kept my face still. I'd had a lot of practice that week.

I went to the inquest. It was held in December, at Maldon, in a room over the magistrates' court with a radiator that knocked. I sat at the back. The family were there, what was left of them. The boy was in a black tie and looked about forty. Imogen Vane sat very upright, and next to her, to my surprise, was a lean brown man in a suit that didn't fit him, with a hat on his knee. Fenn was there. They'd left the old man at home. The nurse was at the end of a row by herself, as white as paper, twisting a handkerchief. The nephew wasn't called. Nor was I. There was no Mrs Vane. She'd sent an affidavit from an address in Rotterdam.

A pathologist read from a sheet. I've heard a great many of them read. He'd a flat Scots voice.

The deceased was a well-nourished man of fifty-two. The cause of death was drowning; there was sea water in the lungs and the stomach, and diatoms in the marrow, and I needn't go on. He had been alive when he went into the water. He'd been in it approximately thirty-six hours.

I heard that. *He had been alive when he went into the water.* I sat on a hard chair at the back of that room with my hat in my hands. I felt my whole case go, like a ladder with the bottom rungs out of it.

The man went on. There was more.

There was a depressed fracture of the left temporal bone, consistent with a single blow from a heavy blunt object of small surface, or with a fall against a projecting edge. It had been sustained some hours before death. I thought of a lead weight in a brass jacket, hanging at the bottom of its line. There was a second injury, a large contusion over the occiput, the back of the head, which was separate and somewhat later. There were eleven pellets of number five shot in the left shoulder, the left side of the neck and the back of the left ear, fired from a distance and from below; the wounds were superficial. There was chloral hydrate in the blood, in a quantity that the witness would call substantial. And alcohol.

The body was clothed in evening trousers, a dress shirt and braces, and one sock. There was no jacket. There were abrasions to the palms of both hands, and to the inner sides of the forearms, with tarred wood fibres in them, as though the deceased had been gripping something for a long time.

The coroner was an old solicitor with half-glasses. He looked at the sheet for a while when it was finished. He said that in thirty years he had not seen a man arrive at his own death by so many roads at once. He said that the deceased had evidently been struck, and drugged, and shot at, and had fallen or been struck a second time, all on the one night. And none of it had killed him. What killed him was the sea. There was no evidence before the court to show how he came to be in it. The family could offer none. He was not satisfied that it was an accident and he was not satisfied that it was anything else. He recorded an open verdict, and he hoped that somebody's conscience would one day supply what the evidence did not.

He looked round the room when he said that. He looked along the rows over his glasses. I've sat in a few courts, and I can tell you, he might have been looking at anybody. Every single person in that room dropped their eyes. I did. I saw the nurse do it, and the boy, and the daughter. I saw the brown man in the bad suit look down at his hat. Fenn studied his papers.

There wasn't one of us looked back at him. I noticed that. I wondered about it for years, what the rest of them had to look down for.

* * *

I've got one more thing to tell. It was the second week of January.

There were two of them, and they came in the afternoon, when I'd just got back with the paper. One was young and had a neck wider than his head. The other was getting on, a thin man in a car coat with a cough. They were quite civil. The thin one asked if I was Mr Cade and I said I was. He said Ronnie would like to ask me something, and they'd been told to ask it for him.

They took me down to the garages behind the flats. The young one did the work. He was good at it. He did the ribs first and left the face till later. The thin one stood and smoked.

When I could listen, the thin one squatted down by me on the concrete. He said, "It's a wrong 'un, Harry."

I said I didn't know what he meant. That got me another one.

"The clock," he said. "The million-pound clock. Ronnie had a feller over from Geneva to look at it, for a customer. Flew him in. The feller's there two minutes, with a little glass. He turns it over. He says, 'Where's the thorn?' Ronnie says, 'What thorn?' The feller says it's a very nice thing. It's worth eight or nine thousand of anyone's money, as an example of the English craft revival. Made about 1965." He coughed. "Ronnie give that woman sixty grand cash in a car park on top of what her old man owed him. On your say-so."

"I never said anything."

"You was driving."

"I didn't know. I looked for the mark. The light was bad."

"That's what Ronnie thought you'd say," the thin man said. "He says he don't reckon you're bright enough to've been in on it. He says you'd have run. That's why you're only getting this." He stood up. His knees cracked. "He wants to know where the real one is."

"I don't know."

"And the woman."

"I don't know. I swear to God I don't. She went up a gangway."

He looked down at me for a bit. Then he nodded to the young one, and I got the face.

It was three weeks before I could go out. I lay on the bed in Leigh and looked at the ceiling, the way I used to, and thought about her.

I thought about whether she'd known. I went over it and over it. There was the way she'd said, *Does it matter? If the man who's paying thinks it's the real one?* She'd asked me that straight out, in the library, kneeling. Did a woman who knew she was passing a dud say that out loud? Or was it just exactly what she would say, so that afterwards I'd remember she'd as good as told me? I can't decide. I've had her both ways, all these years, and I've had her true in the morning and false at night.

I think now she didn't know. I think she took the old man at his word about a copy as much as I did, which is to say not at all. She thought she was selling her husband's father's clock to pay her husband's debt and buy herself a start. And she had the sense to be on a ship by the time anyone looked underneath. She left me standing there because somebody had to be. It wasn't personal. It's what I'd have told her to do, if I'd been advising her.

And the real one? Fenn wrote to me that spring. One line, typed. *You may care to know that the article of which we spoke has been found, in the clock room of the stable tower, where the late Mr Vane had apparently placed it some months since, and has been identified beyond doubt.* The late Mr Vane. The old man had died in March. So it had been up there the whole time, forty feet above my head, while I lay on a horse rug underneath it listening to the big clock cut bread. He'd hidden it himself, and forgotten. Or he hadn't forgotten. I'll never know that either.

I had one card from her. It came two years after, with a Portuguese stamp. It was a picture of a tram. On the back, no signature, in a hand I'd only ever seen once, in eyebrow pencil on an envelope:

*It isn't the right time here either.*

* * *

That's all of it. I've put it down in the order it happened to me, and I've given you the time off my own wrist for every part. I haven't made myself look any better than I was. You'll have seen that.

I know what the pathologist said. I was there. *Alive when he went into the water.* I've turned it every way there is. I've told myself the old man hit him, and I've told myself she doped him. Then somebody carted him out of that ice house, and somebody put shot in him, and somebody put him in the fleet. And through all of that he had some flicker left in him, deep down, that a lighter couldn't show and a doctor could. So it was the sea that finished it. That's how I've had to leave it. It makes five or six people in that house a party to it, one way and another. And I've got no names for half of them.

It's not good enough. I know that. I never closed it.

But I'll say this, and then I've done. I stood at that gate. I put my arm through the bars with a flame in my hand and I looked at him for the best part of a minute. He was at the bottom of twelve steps with his feet higher than his head, and his eyes were open and the light was in them and they didn't move. I'd done it for twenty years. I knew my job when I knew nothing else. They can read me whatever they like off a sheet of paper.

Edmund Vane was dead at a quarter past one by my watch. I'd swear to it. I've sworn to worse.

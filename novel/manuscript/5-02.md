## 2. The Ground

On the Friday he walked the boundary.

He had done it as a boy with his father every year on the day after Michaelmas. It was the way the old man reckoned up. He would go round the whole of it on foot, the hundred and forty acres, with a stick, and look at every gate and every yard of fence and the state of every ditch, and say nothing. At the end he would stand on the sea wall and look back over it. Then he would go in and pay the rent.

The man took the child. She had her notebook, in the pocket of the blue coat, and a pencil on a string.

They went north first, along the foot of the wall. It was a still grey morning with the tide making. The home pastures lay inside the wall, eleven fields of them, small and square, cut out of the marsh by somebody's grandfather with a spade. The grass was good. It had been grazed right and rested right for a hundred and eighty years and it had a sole on it like a carpet. He bent and pulled a handful and smelled it.

What are you doing, the child said.

Seeing what he's left.

What has he?

He's left it better than he found it.

The ditches were clean. That surprised him. The old man had been seventy-seven and alone and the ditches had been dug out that spring, every one, to the clay. The gates hung true. On one of them the bottom rail was new, split oak, and he could see the adze marks.

He stood and looked at that rail. His father had cut it and hung it with a bad chest in the last summer of his life, on ground that he held from year to year, for a landlord who did not know his face.

Why did he fix it if it's not his, the child said. She was looking at the rail too.

I don't know.

I wouldn't.

No. He put his hand on the top bar. I reckon he didn't think of it that way. I reckon he thought it was the gate's.

She wrote something in the book.

They went on. Beyond the last pasture the wall turned east and there was a sluice, a brick arch with an oak door that let the land water out at the ebb and shut against the flood. He looked at that. The door was sound. The brickwork wanted pointing. It was the landlord's to do, by the lease, and it had not been done in his lifetime or his father's.

Outside the wall were the saltings.

They ran north and east for half a mile, eighty acres of them, flat as a table and cut all over by creeks. It was not land and it was not sea. The big tides covered it and the small ones did not. Nothing grew there but sea lavender and purslane and the coarse grey grass that sheep will eat if they are bred to it. The Garrow sheep were bred to it. They were out there now, three hundred ewes, moving ahead of the water in a long file the way they had been taught by their mothers.

He stood on top of the wall with the child and watched them come.

Who tells them, she said.

Nobody. They know.

How?

The old ones know and the young ones follow. He pointed. See that one in front, with the black on her face. She'll have been doing that ten year. She can feel it. She's a good quarter hour ahead of the water and she'll bring them in over the hard by the sluice and never wet a foot.

What if she got it wrong?

She don't.

But if she did.

Then they'd drown, he said. The lot. They'd follow her.

The child watched the sheep. She had her head on one side.

That's a lot to put on one sheep, she said.

* * *

They ate their bread on the wall. The tide came up the stones below them. It came slowly and without any noise. He had forgotten that. On the range the weather came at you. You could see a storm for forty miles, walking on its legs of rain. This came from underneath. One minute there was mud and the next there was a skin of water on it, and you had not seen it arrive.

The child had her boots hanging over. She was looking across at the island.

It had come nearer with the flood. That was a trick of it. With the tide out, the Ness looked a long way off, at the end of its road. With the tide in it seemed to float up close, and a man could count the windows.

Why is there a road if it goes under, she said.

There was always a road. The monks made it. They had the island before the Vanes.

What monks?

I don't know. Old ones. They had a church over there. They'd sing a thing at night, the last thing before bed. That's where the name's from.

What name?

Compline.

She tried it over. She wrote it down and showed him and he said he thought that was how it went.

What's the box?

He looked where she was looking. Halfway across, with the water already round its legs, the Perch stood up black.

That's for if you get caught, he said. If you're on the road and the sea comes. You climb up there and wait.

How long?

Till it goes down. Five hours. Six.

In the dark?

If it's dark.

Has anyone?

He did not answer at once. He was looking at the box. He had passed it ten thousand times and touched the post each time, the way his father did, without ever asking why.

I've heard there was a man once, he said. A long while back. A carrier with a cart. He lost the horse and the cart and sat up there all night and they fetched him off in the morning with his hair gone white.

Is that true?

It's what's said.

She looked at the box for some time.

The lady didn't, she said. The one that drowned. She didn't get in the box.

No.

Why not? It was right there.

I don't know, the man said. She was past it. She was a good way past it, coming this way. And then she went back.

Back to the box?

Back toward it. She never got in.

The child turned that over. He could see her doing it. She had a way of holding a thing in her head and walking round it, the way a buyer walks round a horse.

That's a clue, she said.

It's a what?

A clue. It's a thing that doesn't fit. When there's a thing that doesn't fit, that's where you look. She had the notebook open. It's in my book. The girl in it finds a glove on the wrong side of a fence and that's how she knows.

Knows what?

Who did it.

He looked at her. She was nine. She had sat for eight months on the edge of a bed in a rented house reading aloud to a woman who was dying, and after that she had not spoken for some weeks. Then one day she had come into the barn with a paper book from the school library, about a girl who solved things. She had been reading that kind ever since. He had thought it a good sign. He had thought, she wants there to be an answer.

Nobody did anything, he said. The sea did it.

Then why did Grandpa want you to ask?

He had not known she heard. She had been in the cottage across the yard, in bed. But there was a window, and it had been a still night, and she missed nothing.

That's not for you, he said.

OK.

I mean it, Wren.

I said OK. She shut the notebook. She did not put it away. She held it on her knee with her finger in the place.

* * *

In the afternoon he went through the buildings.

The barn was dry. There was hay to the tie-beams, good hay, cut early. There was a cake store, and a root house with mangolds clamped in straw. In the stable there were two stalls and a loose box. The mare had the loose box. The other stall had a Fordson tractor in it that he remembered his father buying second-hand in 1961.

He looked at the mare's feet. She wanted shoeing behind. He found his father's tools in the forge, a lean-to at the end of the barn with a hearth no bigger than a kitchen fire. The anvil had been his grandfather's. The hammers hung on their nails in order of weight. He took down the rasp and felt the edge. It had been kept oiled.

He had learned the trade in that lean-to, the summer he was twenty-three, from the smith at Thorney. He had made a ring there out of a horseshoe nail. He remembered beating the head flat. He had spoiled eleven before he got one that would do.

He hung the rasp back.

In the house he went through the old man's papers. They were in a biscuit tin on the dresser. There were the rent books, every one, back to 1919, in his grandfather's hand and then his father's. He turned the pages. *Lady Day. Paid.* *Michaelmas. Paid.* There was never a quarter missed. In 1947, the year of the great frost, when they lost half the flock, somebody had written *Paid* and underlined it twice.

There was the lease. It was a single sheet. It said that the tenant held from year to year at the landlord's pleasure.

There were his own letters. They were at the bottom of the tin, tied with binder twine. There were twenty of them. Not one had been opened.

He sat at the table with the bundle in his hand. *I don't read them. I can't see to.* The old man had been able to see well enough to hang a gate. He had kept them, all the same. He had put each one in the tin as it came and tied the string again.

Under the letters there was one thing more. It was a tide table, a little yellow book, such as they sold at the chandler's. It was for the year 1974. He opened it. Someone had gone through it in pencil and put a ring round certain nights. It was a woman's hand, small and upright. Against the twenty-sixth of October there was a ring, and a word.

*Come.*

He closed the book. He put it back under the letters and put the lid on the tin.

He sat a while. Then he took out the oilcloth packet from his shirt and unfolded it on the table. There was the blue paper from the solicitor. He read it again. *Vacant possession. The twenty-fifth day of March.*

He thought about three hundred ewes that knew the tide. They could not be moved. A marsh flock was hefted to its ground; put them on another farm and they would stand at the gate and pine. They would have to be sold, as mutton. The old one with the black face would go up the ramp of a lorry with the rest.

He thought about the gate with the new rail.

A hundred and eighty years. His people had kept that wall and dug those ditches and bred that flock. They had done it well, and gone on doing it, for wages they paid themselves. And at the end of it a man they had never spoken to could write one page and have them off by spring. He would put caravans on it. Dolly had told him. There would be a shop, and a road laid in concrete across the home pasture, and lights.

He had seen men in the west lose ground. He had seen a family that had ranched a creek for three generations sold up in an afternoon by a bank, and the wife standing in the yard holding a clock. He had thought it hard. He had not thought that there was anything to be done. That was how it was; land belonged to whoever had the paper.

He found that it was different when the gate was one his father had hung.

Dad.

The child was in the doorway. She had been with Dolly Pask, learning to make pastry. She had flour on her chin.

There's a lady on the road, she said.

* * *

He went to the window.

It was nearly dark. There was a band of green light left in the west, over the flats, and the first of the moon coming up behind the house. The tide had gone out again and the Wade lay bare.

Someone was walking out along it.

It was a woman. She had come down the ramp from the wall, not thirty yards from his door, while he sat reading, and he had not heard her. She was a good way out already. She was thin and dark-headed and she wore a long coat. She had a bag in her hand. She walked quickly and did not look down at her feet.

He stood with his hand on the sill.

Who is she, the child said.

I don't know.

Is she going to the house?

There's nowhere else that road goes.

Is she allowed? It's nearly night.

She's two hours yet. More. He was reckoning it without thinking, from the feel of the air. She knows it. Look how she goes. She's not minding the stones. She's walked that before.

The woman went on. She got smaller. The moon came clear of the roof behind him and put her shadow down on the road in front of her.

She came to the dip. She came to the bridge, and the box on its legs. And there she stopped.

He saw her put the bag down on the planks. He saw her stand. She was too far off now, and the light too poor, for him to see more than a shape. But he saw the shape put out an arm and lay its hand on the post of the Perch.

She stood like that for a long time.

He did not move. He did not know why he could not. A woman had stopped on a road and touched a post. Every Garrow did it. Half the village did.

She's sad, the child said.

How d'you know?

You can tell. She's got her head down. The child had her nose against the glass. Is she the lady's girl? The one that drowned?

He looked down at her.

What makes you say that?

Mrs Pask said the lady had a girl and a boy. She said the girl went away to Scotland and doesn't come. But that's who'd be sad there. She said it reasonably. It's where her mom was.

The man looked out again. The woman had picked up her bag. She was going on, toward the island, up the far slope of the dip. The moon was full on her back. He could see how she held her head.

He had seen that head held so across a harvest supper, at the far end of a barn. He had seen it bent over a lantern on the saltings, with the hair coming down.

It could not be. Dolly had said it plain. *She's in Scotland. She never comes home this time of year.*

He stood at the window until the woman had gone up off the Wade into the trees and there was nothing on the road. He stood after that. The green went out of the sky. The lamp in the porch of the house across the water burned small and yellow, as it had every night of his boyhood.

Dad.

What?

You're holding the window.

He looked. He had hold of the frame with both hands. He let go.

Go and wash your face, he said. You've flour on it.

* * *

He did not sleep that night.

He put the child to bed in the small room over the kitchen that had been his. He sat by the range. At ten he made up the lamp and set it on the sill, in the window that looked at the island. He did it without deciding to. His hands knew where it went.

He sat and looked at it.

He thought about his father. He thought about the question that had been put on him. He had turned it over all the week and he had not known what he would do. He had thought that he might write to the man. He had thought he might go to the lawyer and say, I'll be off your ground by March, and before I go I want five minutes with Mr Edmund Vane. He was not a man who went to another man's door at night.

He thought about a woman with her hand on a post.

If it was her. If she had come. Then she was in that house now, half a mile off. She was sitting at a table with her brother.

He got up and walked about the kitchen. He was forty-three years old. He had been married nine years to a good woman and had buried her. He had a child asleep overhead. He had thought that the other thing was done with. He had carried a piece of paper inside his shirt for twenty years the way a man carries a scar. He did not expect it to open.

It had opened. It had opened at a window, at the sight of a shape half a mile away that might be anyone.

Toward two he heard the tide come over the road. A little after, he went to the door and looked out. The moon was high and the water lay white to the island.

There was a light over there besides the porch. It was on the upper floor, on the west side. It was a window he knew.

He stood in the doorway in his shirt. The night air was cold on his arms.

It burned for a long time. He thought that somebody stood behind it. He could not see. But he stood and looked at it, and he had the feeling that he had on the range sometimes, at night, riding fence, when he would know without turning that there was a deer at the edge of the trees watching him.

After a while it went out.

He went in. He turned down his own lamp. He did not go up. He sat in his father's chair until it was light, with his boots on.

In the morning he took the gun down from the corner and looked through the barrels.

He was not going to use it. He told himself so. A man looks at a gun that has been left to him. It was a twelve-bore, a plain one, by a Birmingham maker, with the blueing worn silver at the breech where his father's hand had carried it for fifty years. The barrels were clean. There was a box of cartridges on the dresser. Number five shot, for duck.

He set it back in the corner.

He would go to the lawyer first. That was the right way. He would put on a clean shirt and drive to the county town and see this man Fenn, and ask him civil who a Garrow was to speak to. If there was a way to do it with his hat in his hand, he would do it that way.

He woke the child at seven. She came down with her notebook.

I've to go to Colchester, he said. To see a man about the paper. You're to stop with Mrs Pask. She'll be at the cottage with her sister.

Can't I come?

No.

Can I ride Mab?

Not on your own. Not till I've shod her.

Can I go on the road?

He took her by the two shoulders. He had never raised his hand to her and he never would. But he held her so that she looked at him.

You are not to set foot on that road, he said. Not a foot. Not if the Queen of England was to call you from the other end. You don't know it, and it don't care who you are. It took a grown woman that had lived beside it thirty year. Do you hear me?

Yes.

Say it.

I'm not to go on the road.

Not for anything.

Not for anything, the child said.

She looked at him steadily while she said it. She was a truthful child. He had never caught her in a lie, and he never did. He let her go.

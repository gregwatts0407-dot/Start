## 5. Both

We came down the ladder the way we had gone up, with me doing his feet. At the bottom he sat on the corn bin for a while with his eyes shut and the handkerchief against his neck. The bleeding had nearly stopped. He said that it was only the edge of the pattern, and that he had been peppered worse by his own father at a pheasant shoot in 1966.

Then he took me across the yard by the hand, in the moonlight, in full view of every window on that side of the house. I said that someone would see. He said that by this hour of the Odd Hour everyone in that house was looking at something else. He was right. Nobody saw.

He took me in by the kitchen yard. The kitchen was empty. He put me in the larder, among the hams, and gave me a leg of goose off the carcass and told me to eat it and be quiet. Then he sat on the kitchen table, as Toby has described, with his own goose and a tumbler of the cooking brandy, and his feet on a chair.

I have the notebook entry. It is in very large letters, written by feel, in the dark of the larder, and most of a page is taken up by the one line.

*3.15. I AM IN A CUBBORD EATING A GOOSE.*

Toby came down the stairs. I heard everything that he has written: that he was engaged, that Edmund nearly broke his hand, that he should not wait for the right time because there was not one in that house. I heard Edmund say, "I've just had a talk with my father." I heard him say, "You were only a boy," and laugh, and say that he had been thirty-two.

I heard the step in the scullery passage, and Edmund send Toby away.

And I heard Imogen.

I am not going to give it again. She has written it, and she has written it straight; she did not spare herself. I sat on the floor of that larder with a goose bone in my lap and listened to a woman tell a man that she hoped he would never sleep again as long as he lived. I had not heard a grown person speak like that to anybody. I had heard my parents quarrel, once or twice, at the end, about the hospital. It had not been like that.

I heard him say yes. I heard him try to tell her. *It's what she did, at the end.* I heard her say that she would not hear another word.

I heard him give her something and say, *Go on, Immy. Don't look back. She looked back.*

Then she was gone. He stood in the passage for a long time. I could hear him breathing.

When he opened the larder door he had a candle in the kitchen saucer. He looked at me on the floor among the hams, and I saw that he had forgotten I was there, and was trying to remember.

"Did you hear that?" he said.

I nodded.

"All of it?"

"Yes."

"Well," said Edmund Vane. "She's quite right. You'll find that people mostly are, about me. It's only that they're right about the wrong thing." He held out his hand. "Come on. My sister's going across the water with your father, on a horse. I think we should go and watch. One ought to see a thing like that, if it's to be had."

* * *

I want to put down exactly what happened at the orchard gate, because it is the one thing in this whole history that I have had to piece together from my own memory alone, and because I am the reason for it.

We went out by the garden door. He did not go by the orangery; he went round, by the wall, slowly, with his hand on my shoulder. I think that it was to lean on. The moon was very bright. There was no wind at all now. At the bottom of the orchard there was a gap in the sea wall with a gate in it, and beyond the gate the ramp went down into the water, where the Wade had been.

We stood behind the gate, in the shadow of the last apple tree. He set the saucer on the gatepost.

They were already in the sea. They were a long way out. I could see a white horse, up to its chest, with the moonlight all along its back, and a lady sitting on it with her hair coming down, in a dark coat. And at the horse's head, walking in the water to his waist, with one hand on the bridle, was my father.

I knew him by the way he walked. I would know him by that at a mile.

I said, "That's Dad."

"Yes."

"He's going home."

"He's taking my sister home. To your house. She'll be there in the morning when you get back. I shouldn't wonder if she's there for good." He was quiet. "I hope you'll like her. She's better than she sounds. She's had a bad night."

I did not hear any of that properly. I heard only that he was going home, across the sea, and I was on this side of it. I was not supposed to be there. Nobody but a cook and a drunk man with a hole in his neck knew that I was there. He was going home to Saltcote with a lady, on the horse, and he thought that I was asleep in Vi Pask's bed. And he would get there, and go across to the cottage, and look in, and the bed would be empty.

I had promised him that I would not set foot on the road.

I stood behind that gate and watched him wade away from me into the moon, and I could not breathe.

Edmund said, "Ah." He put his hand to his forehead. "Wren. I'm going to sit down for a minute. I've gone rather peculiar." He sat down on the grass with his back to the gatepost, quite slowly, and put his head between his knees. "It's nothing. It's the head. It goes round. Give me a minute and I'll be right."

I looked down at him. Then I looked out at the water.

They had come to the place in the middle where the road went down. My father had got up behind the lady. I saw the horse stop, at the edge of something. I saw my father slide off into the sea up to his neck. And then the horse went down. It went right down, and the lady with it, and I saw them carried away sideways, and I heard myself make a noise.

They came up. They came up a long way off to the left, and the horse was standing, and my father was holding on to its neck.

The lady turned round in the saddle. She looked back at the island. She looked straight at our gate, at the place where I was standing, with the candle on the post beside me. And she lifted her arm, and waved to me. It was a long slow wave, the whole arm, the way you wave from a train.

I have thought about that wave more than about anything else in my life, more than about the Perch. I know now what it was. She thought it was her brother. She thought he had come down to see her off, after what she had said to him, and she was saying goodbye, and she was sorry.

I did not know who she was. I knew that she was on my father's horse, going to my father's house, and that she had seen me, and waved.

I thought that she was telling me to come on.

* * *

I had my torch in my coat pocket. I had not used it all night where anyone could see. I took it out and I switched it on.

I opened the gate. I did not look at Edmund. I think that I knew that if I looked at him I should not go. I went down the ramp into the water.

It was so cold that it did not feel like water. It felt like being bitten. It came over my shoes and up my legs and I went on. I had the torch up in front of me, pointing at the horse. I could see the white of it, a long way off, going up out of the sea on the other side.

I was not on the road. I mean that I did not think of it as the road. I thought of it as the way to my father. I had made a promise about the road on the Friday, to a man who was standing in a farm kitchen with a paper in his hand, and it had been about not going. It had not been about coming back. He had not thought to make me promise anything about coming back.

He says that in his own part. He says it twice. I think that he knew.

The water came to my knees, and then to my middle. It pulled at me from the side. I held on to one of the sticks that line the road and went from one to the next. I could not see the horse any more; there was a cloud. There was only the torch, making a little yellow road of its own across the black, and the sticks coming up out of it one at a time.

I heard Edmund behind me. He was shouting my name.

I did not stop. I have been asked why. I do not know. I was nine. My father was on the other side.

Then the bottom went.

I had come to the place where the road goes down. I did not know it was there. My foot went forward and there was nothing under it, and the water closed over my head.

I do not remember much of the next part. I remember that it was dark and very loud and that I was being turned round and round. I remember that I still had the torch. I remember thinking, quite clearly, that it was like the time I fell off the pony into the creek at home, only colder. I remember that I could not find which way was up.

Then something had me by the collar of my coat.

It hauled. My head came out. I got a breath of air and half the sea with it. I was against something big and warm that was swimming, and swearing, and a voice was in my ear saying, "I've got you. I've got you. Stop kicking, you little devil, I've got you."

He had come in after me. He had been sitting on the grass with his head between his knees, drugged, with a broken skull and a hole in his neck, and he had seen my torch go down the ramp, and got up, and come in.

He has his account of the Rill from 1974; I am coming to it. He knew that water. He knew that it set to the north and that there was nowhere to land below the bridge but the tail of the bank. He let it take us. He held me against his chest with one arm and swam with the other, across the current, as my father's mare had done twice that night. Something came up out of the dark over us. It was black, with legs.

He got hold of it. He got hold of one of the legs of the Perch with his free hand and he held on, with the whole of the Rill trying to pull us off it.

"Ladder," he said. "On the other side. Round the post. Can you hold on to me?"

I held on round his neck. He went round the post hand over hand, with me on his back. He found the ladder with his foot.

"Up," he said. "Go up. I'm behind you."

I got on to it. I put my foot on the bottom rung that I could reach under the water, and stood up, and put my hand up to the next.

It came away.

* * *

I have Toby Pargeter's account of what he did to that ladder at five o'clock on the Saturday afternoon. He pushed the bottom rung back into its socket, with the nail sticking out, because it seemed rude to leave it.

It was not his fault. I want that understood. It was one rung among nine, and I have been told since by a man who builds these things that every rung on that ladder below the high-water mark had been rotten for ten years. Edmund had been going to see to it since Kit was at school. Every Garrow who passed had touched the post. Nobody had looked at the rungs.

The second rung came away in my hand, and the third came away under my foot when I put my weight on it to get past. I went down into the water again to my chest. Edmund caught me.

He did not say anything. He reached up past me into the dark. He found the fourth rung and pulled on it, and it held. He found the fifth.

"Right," he said. "Listen to me. Are you listening?"

"Yes."

"I'm going to lift you up as high as I can reach. You put your hands on that rung, there, and you climb, and you don't stop till you're inside the box. It's dry in there. There's a door. Will you do that?"

"What about you?"

"I'll be along."

He took off his jacket. He did it in the water, holding on to the ladder with one hand and getting out of the sleeves with the other. It was the black one, with silk on the front. He folded it into a bundle, very fast, the way you would fold a flag, and pushed it inside the front of my coat and buttoned the coat over it.

"That's for in there," he said. "Put it on. It's warm. It's got a key in the pocket; don't lose that. It's not mine."

Then he got his hands round my middle and lifted me up out of the sea, straight up, as high as he could reach, at arm's length.

I got the rung. I climbed. There were four more, and they held. I came to the little door at the top, and it was not locked, and I fell through it on to a wooden floor, and lay there.

When I put my head out he was still in the water at the foot of the ladder.

He had his arms round the post. The sea was up to his chest, running past him, and pulling at him, and he was holding on to that tarred leg with both arms and his cheek against it, like a man holding a tree in a flood.

"Come up," I said.

"I can't, love. The bottom's gone. I can't reach the first one that'll bear me, and I've not got the arms to pull up to it, not tonight." He said it quite calmly. "It doesn't matter. The water's on the turn. It'll go down. I'll hold on here till it does."

"How long?"

"An hour, perhaps. Less. Put the coat on."

I put it on. It came down to my ankles. It was wet on the outside and dry inside, and it was warm from him.

I lay on the floor of the Perch, in a dead man's jacket, with my head out of the door, and looked down at him. My torch was gone; I had dropped it when the rung broke. It was lying on the bottom under the water, still lit, I could see the glow of it. There was a moon. I could see the top of his head and his arms round the post and the water going by.

I said, "Are you cold?"

"Freezing."

"Are you going to die?"

"I shouldn't think so," said Edmund Vane. "I've had several tries tonight, and none of them took. Talk to me, Wren. Keep me awake. I've a tendency to drop off."

* * *

So we talked. For fifty minutes. I had my watch; I had put it back on after the tower, and it had a light. I looked at it a great many times.

I told him about Pincher Creek, and the Oldman River, and the chinook wind that comes over the mountains in January and melts the snow in an hour so that the whole town goes out in its shirtsleeves. I told him about my mother. I told him how she had read me the Nancy Drew books, and then I had read them to her when she was too tired, and I had found I liked them better than anything. I told him about the clues.

"What have you got in your book?" he said. "About tonight?"

So I told him. I told him that his father's clocks were all an hour wrong on purpose and that you had to ask which one o'clock. I told him that there were two green dresses. I told him that the detective had a watch in his sock. I told him that the pink man had broken clock number 44 and put it in his pocket.

He laughed so much that I was afraid he would let go of the post.

"You've solved it," he said. "You've solved the whole damned house. Two green dresses. My God. Celia will be livid."

I told him that I thought he had done it. I told him that he had been Suspect Number One since half past one in the afternoon.

"Quite right," he said. "I did do it. I took about thirty clocks. I've got a list."

"But you said sorry. In the trees. You said sorry, Ma."

He did not answer for a while.

"She's the other one," he said. "That's a different case. That's twenty years old. Nobody's ever solved that one, because there was only one witness, and he's a coward."

"Was it you?"

"Yes."

"What did you do?"

He told me.

* * *

I am going to give it here as nearly as I can in his words. I had it by heart by morning. I said it over all the way to the cottage. I have said it over most nights since.

"It was the same night as this," he said. "The night the clocks go back. I was thirty-two. My mother was leaving my father for a farmer across the water. Your grandfather. My sister told me so at tea, to hurt me, and it did. So I drank a bottle of port, and at one in the morning I saw my mother's lantern go across the lawn and out on to the Wade, and I went after her.

"I caught her on the far side of this. The Rill. Past this box. She was three-quarters of the way. I could see your grandfather's light on the wall.

"I got hold of her arm. I begged her. I did all the things that a drunk man does. I cried. I said that she couldn't leave me with him. I said I'd kill myself. She was very patient. She'd always been patient with me. She stood there in the dark with the lantern and let me say it all, and said, Edmund, darling, you're thirty-two, you have a wife and a little boy, you must learn to stand up without me watching.

"And she said that she was going. She kissed me. She turned round, and she walked on, toward his light.

"And I sat down."

He stopped. The water went by.

"I sat down on the road behind her, on the stones, like a child that won't walk. I thought she'd come back. She always had. And she didn't. She went on. I watched her light go ten yards, and twenty. And I thought, she's going. She means it. And I got up and went the other way. Back toward the house. I don't know why. I think I wanted her to see me go, and be sorry.

"I was so drunk. I didn't think about the water. It had turned while we stood there; I'd felt it round my feet and paid no attention. And I came to the Rill, here, going back, and it had filled. It was up to my chest and running like a mill race. I went in, and it took my legs, and I went under.

"She heard me. I must have shouted. I don't remember.

"She turned round."

He was quiet so long that I thought he had gone to sleep. I said his name.

"I'm here," he said. "She turned round, Wren. She was thirty yards from your grandfather's ramp, and he was standing there with his light up, and she'd done it, she'd got away. And she heard her son go into the Rill behind her, and she turned round and came back.

"She came into the water after me. In her stockings. She got hold of my collar. Just as I got hold of yours. She knew this water; she was born on this coast. She let it take us down to this post, and she got my hands on it.

"And the ladder was good then. Every rung. She got me on to it. She pushed me up it. I was sick and crying and too drunk to climb, and she got her shoulder under me and pushed, from the water, and I went up into the box, as you've done.

"And then she was at the bottom. Where I am. She had hold of the post. I put my hand down to her. She could have come up. There was room."

His voice changed.

"She had her hand up. Out of the water. I thought she was reaching for mine. She wasn't. She'd got her ring off. She put it in my hand and shut my fingers on it, and she said, Give that to your father. And she said, Tell him I'm sorry. And she let go of the post."

I did not say anything. I could not.

"I don't know why she let go. I've had twenty years and I don't know. Perhaps the water took her. Perhaps she was tired; she'd been in it a long time, getting me up. Perhaps she thought there wasn't room for two. Perhaps she'd made up her mind on that road that she was going one way or the other and she'd not go back to the house. I don't know. I put my hand down and she wasn't there. Her lantern was floating. It went out.

"I stayed up there till the tide went down. Then I walked home. It was getting light. I said at breakfast that I'd slept in the stable. My father said that he'd put the clocks back early and she'd misread them. And I let him. I let him for twenty years. I let my sister think I'd dragged her back. I let your grandfather think she'd changed her mind about him. I let them all think whatever they liked, so long as nobody knew the one thing, which was that she'd got away, and got clear, and come back into the sea for me. Because I'd sat down on the road and wouldn't walk."

The water had gone down. It was at his waist now. I could see more of the post.

"So that's the case," Edmund Vane said. "It was me. It was never the clock."

* * *

After a while I said, "She was coming and she was going."

"What?"

"You said it on the tower. My dad asked was she coming or was she going. And you said both."

"Did I." He sounded very tired. "Yes. That's it. That's the answer to his question. She was going to your grandfather. She was going, and she got there, as near as makes no matter. And then she was coming back. For me. Not from him." He breathed. "Not from him. That's the bit he never knew. He thought she'd turned away from him. She didn't. She turned toward me. It's not the same."

"You have to tell him."

"I meant to. I was going to, tonight. On the tower. Then he shot the Jack, and I lay down."

"You have to tell the lady."

"I tried. She'd not hear it. You heard her."

"You have to tell your dad."

"I've told my dad. That's done. That was the best quarter of an hour of my life, up that tower, with you sneezing in the cupboard." He was nearly laughing. "So that's one. There's two more. And I'm stood in the sea holding on to a post."

"I'll tell them."

He was silent.

"Will you?" he said at last. "Will you, Wren? I've no right to ask you."

"I'll tell them. What do I say?"

He thought about it. I could see him thinking, with his cheek against the post.

"Say she turned back for me," he said. "Not from Amos. Say that. To my sister, and to your father. Say she turned back for me, not from Amos Garrow. They'll know what it means. Say it."

I said it. *She turned back for me. Not from Amos Garrow.*

"Again."

I said it again. I counted it, the way I counted everything. It was nine words.

"Good girl," he said. "That's it. That's all of it. That's twenty years in nine words. I could have done it in a minute any time I liked, and I never did." The water was at his hips. "You're a better messenger than I am. I had a ring to carry two hundred yards and I've had it in my waistcoat since 1974."

"Where is it?"

"I gave it to Immy. An hour ago. In the passage. She'll give it to him. That's done too." He let out his breath. "There. I've cleared my pockets. I don't think I've been so tidy since I was nine."

* * *

It was a quarter to five by my watch.

His voice had got slower. He had stopped making jokes. I could see that he was shaking, all of him, the way the mare had been shaking in Tom's story, in long hard shudders.

"The water's going down," I said. "Look. It's only at your middle."

"So it is."

"Soon you can stand."

"Yes."

"Then you can come up."

"I don't think I'll need to," he said. "I'll walk. Listen, Wren. When it's down far enough you're to climb out and go to the lights. You see them? On the far side? That's your house. That's your father's kitchen. Go straight to it. Don't come back for me. I'll be behind you."

"I'll wait."

"No. Count to a thousand. Then come out and walk to the lights. Forward. Don't look back. People in this family look back, and it never does them any good. You're not one of us. You walk straight on."

"I want to wait for you."

"Count," said Edmund Vane. "Go on. Out loud. I want to hear you. One."

I counted. I lay on the floor of the Perch with my chin on the sill of the door and counted out loud into the dark. He said the numbers with me, for a while. Then he said them a little behind me. Then he only said every ten.

I got to two hundred and something. He said, "Good girl." I got to three hundred. I did not hear him.

I said, "Edmund?"

The water said nothing.

I put my head out. I could see the post. I could see the water going by it, lower now, below where his arms had been. The moon was on it. There was nobody holding on to it.

There was nobody anywhere.

I called him. I called him for a long time. I called him the way his father had called him in the ice house, over and over, gently, as you call someone you want to wake.

I did not go down. He had told me not to come back for him. And I was nine, and I was more frightened than I had been all night, and the ladder had no bottom.

I counted to a thousand. I did it because he had told me to, and because I could not think of anything else to do. I counted it all, out loud, with my teeth knocking together, in his jacket.

When I got to a thousand I looked at my watch. It was twenty past five.

I climbed out. I went down the rungs that were there and hung from the last one and let go. The water was at my waist. It was hardly moving. I could see the stones of the road under it, in the first grey of the light. My torch was lying on them, still on, very faint. I left it there. I did not look for him. He had told me not to.

I walked to the lights. Forward. I did not look back.

* * *

I was at the cottage door by ten to six. Vi Pask let me in. She was in her nightdress with a candle, and she looked at me standing on her step, soaked to the chest, in a man's dinner jacket down to my ankles, and she said nothing at all. She took me upstairs and dried me with a towel and put me in my vest into the bed, and took the jacket off me and rolled it up, and pushed it under the bed because she did not know what else to do with it.

She said, "Where have you been, child?"

I said, "I'm not allowed to say."

She looked at me a long time with her little bright eyes. Then she said, "No. I don't suppose you are." And she went down and sat in her chair by the cold grate until her sister came at seven, white, in her cobwebs, running.

I lay under the blanket with it pulled over my head and said nine words over to myself. I must have slept in the end, because I did not hear him come up the stairs. I half woke when he lifted me. I heard him say to Dolly, downstairs, that I would have had a bad night, that there had been a wind. I smelled the sea on him, and the horse. He carried me across the yard and into the farm kitchen, and there was a lady in his father's chair, in his shirt, with her hair down.

It was the lady from the horse. It was the lady who had waved.

I looked at her a long time. She asked me which lady. I said the one who touched the box, on Friday. I asked if she had come on Mab. She said she had. I said I would have been scared. She said she was.

And then I put my face in my father's neck, because I could not hold it any longer, and I asked the only thing I had left.

"Is the man all right?"

He said, "What man?"

And I understood that he did not know. That nobody knew. That Edmund Vane had gone into the Rill at a quarter to five in the morning, holding a post for a child that was not supposed to be on the island, and that I was the only person in the world who had seen.

I did not answer. I went to sleep instead. I slept until four in the afternoon. When I woke up, the policeman's car was at the end of the ramp, and I had already decided.

* * *

The coroner said that the palms of his hands were torn and the insides of his arms, with splinters of tarred wood in them, as though he had held on to something for a long time. He said that the deceased was in his shirt and trousers and braces and one sock, and no jacket. He said that he had been alive when he entered the water. He said that he could not find how he came to be in the sea.

They were all of them in that room, and they all looked down.

I was not in the room. I was ten by then, and at school in Thorney, and I knew nothing about inquests. But I have the report. And I have sat at a table this year with every one of them who is still alive, and asked them what they were looking down for.

Hester was looking down because she had cleaned the weight. Kit was looking down because he thought he had killed a man in the harness room and that the man had somehow become his father. Imogen was looking down because she had said a thing in a scullery passage. My father was looking down because he had put eleven pellets in a man's neck from below. Mr Cade was looking down because he had told a widow how to wash a glass and helped her sell a clock. Celia was not there; she was in Rotterdam, writing an affidavit, and she has told me that she did not sleep that night either.

Toby was not called. If he had been, he would have looked down too.

Not one of them had done it. Every one of them had a piece of it. Nobody but me knew which piece mattered.

The palms. The tarred wood. One sock. No jacket.

The jacket was under a bed in Wade Cottage, rolled up, and it stayed there until my father found it in the spring, clearing out Vi's things after she died. He took it for one of old Vi's late husband's and put it on the bonfire. I had got there first. I had taken the key out of the pocket in the November, and wrapped it in a page torn from the back of the notebook, and put it in the biscuit tin with the rest. My maple-leaf handkerchief I never saw again. Kit Vane picked it up off the boards of the clock chamber on the Sunday morning and asked whose it was, and nobody knew. He gave it back to me this year, in the hall at Compline. He had kept it in a drawer in Berlin for thirty years, he said, without knowing why. It is in my pocket now.

It is about three inches long, of steel, with a bow pierced in the shape of a sprig of thorn. Leopold Vane says that it fits nothing else.

He was wrong about that. He forgot what his own copies were.

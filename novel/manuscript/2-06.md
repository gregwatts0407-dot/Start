## 6. In Which I Am Accepted

I have proposed marriage, in the course of a full life, on five occasions. Three were successful, if that is the word, and one of those is still pending. I mention the figure so that the reader will understand that I speak with some authority when I say that the proposal I made in the hall at Compline was, technically, the worst.

I crossed the floor. She was standing by my uncle's chair. He had his head back and his eyes closed, and his long hands lay on the rug. She was looking out through the front door at the stable tower. When I came up she didn't turn her head.

"Miss Quaile," I said.

"Mr Pargeter."

"I don't know your Christian name."

"Hester."

It suited her. It was a name like a plain white plate. I said it over to myself, and then I found that I had nothing ready to follow it. I had come across the hall on a wave of feeling, and it had set me down at her elbow and gone out again, as waves do.

"The vicar's still asleep," I said. "I've been looking at him every half hour. As instructed. He's on his left side."

"Thank you."

"And he's breathing. I counted. Eight to the minute."

"That's right."

She still hadn't looked at me. I followed her eyes. There was nothing out there but the gravel in the moonlight and the tower, with its clock face, and something or other up at the top under a little roof. I could make out one of the wooden figures. It seemed to have its arm in the air.

"What are you looking at?" I asked.

"Nothing," Hester Quaile said. "There's nothing there now."

I should have noticed the *now*. I didn't. I am no good at small words. I was working myself up, and it's a process, with me, like getting the Midget started on a cold morning. It involves a good deal of choke and several false tries.

"There's something I ought to tell you," I said. "I promised I wouldn't. But I think you ought to know, being a nurse, and it's been on my mind. It's about Edmund."

That got her attention. She turned right round. I've mentioned her eyes. They fixed on me as though I had spoken in a foreign language that she happened to understand.

"What about Mr Edmund?"

"He's all right. I want to say that first. He's perfectly all right; he's asleep. But he's had rather a bang on the head. Two, actually. He had the same stuff as the vicar, you see, out of that glass, and it must have come over him all at once, and he fell down some steps."

"What steps?"

"In the ice house. I found him at the bottom. I'd been sent for the pudding." I was speaking very low, and fast, with one eye on my sleeping uncle. "I did exactly what you said. I turned him on his left side. And then Mrs Pask and I got him up, and we've put him in the stable. In the end loose box, in the straw, with a rug over him and another under his head. She says nobody's to know, and particularly not you, because you'd be bound to tell Uncle Leo, and after what Uncle Leo said at dinner it would be the last straw. She says it happens every year. She's going out to him with a basin."

Her lips had parted. She put a hand on the back of the chair.

"When?" she said.

"When what?"

"When did you find him? When did you put him in the stable? What time?"

"Well, I was sent at twenty-five past twelve. I know that, because I looked. We'd got him tucked up by a quarter to one, or just after. The clock on the tower struck the quarter as we were coming away." I thought she might want the clinical details. "He spoke to me. Twice. He was quite sensible. He asked what I was holding, and I said the pudding, and he said, 'Well done.'"

"A quarter to one."

"About then."

"In the stable. Under the tower."

"Next door to it. Yes."

"Asleep. You left him asleep."

"Snoring like a grampus."

She looked back at the open door. It was the most extraordinary look. I have thought about it for nine years. I know now what was going through her head, more or less, because I have read what she wrote about that night; she let me see it, a long time afterwards. I shan't steal her thunder. I'll say only that she had seen something at one o'clock, through that door, on that tower. I'd just told her where my cousin had been lying fifteen minutes before it. She was putting the two together, and what she made of them was not what I'd have made.

"Then he woke," she said, almost to herself. "He woke, and he went up."

"I don't think so. He'd had enough to sink a ship. You said yourself the vicar would be out for twelve hours."

"Yes," she said. "I did say that."

She was shivering. I've said she wasn't a person who showed things. But I was standing very close, and I could see it. It was a fine, steady tremor, like a tuning fork. I wanted to put my jacket round her. I remembered what was in the pocket, and thought it would clank.

So I proposed instead. It was the only other thing I could think of to offer.

* * *

"Miss Quaile. Hester. May I call you Hester? I want to ask you something. I know this isn't the time. I've been told that in this house it never is. But I've also been told that for the next half hour it doesn't count, whatever one says, so if you think it frightful cheek you can simply forget it at two o'clock, and I'll never mention it again."

She said nothing. I took that as leave to go on.

"I've known you for eight hours and twenty minutes. I've been counting. In that time I've broken a clock, and drowned a car, and I thought I'd killed a clergyman. Then I dropped a—well. Never mind what I dropped. The point is that you were there every time it mattered, and you didn't laugh, and you told me what to do. Nobody's ever done that. People generally tell me what I ought to have done, afterwards." I was going rather fast. I had my hands going too; I could feel them. "I'm not anybody. I've got three hundred and twelve pounds and a share in some Bulgarian wine I haven't bought yet. I'm not clever. But I'd look after you. I mean it. Whatever's happened tonight, the glass, and the tray, and Edmund, and whatever it is you saw out there that's upset you so. I'd look after all of it. You wouldn't have to say a word to anyone. You wouldn't have to explain. I'd simply be there."

I stopped. I'd run out of road.

I started again. "What I'm trying to say. The thing I came over to ask. I expect you know what it is. People always do, with me. I'm told it's written all over my face in large print." I swallowed. "Will you?"

She looked up at me.

It was a long look and I couldn't read it. I had expected surprise, or pity, or possibly a kind word and a pat on the arm. There was nothing like that in it. It was a grave, measuring look, the look she'd given the vicar's pulse. I had the impression that she was not thinking about me at all, but about a much larger thing that I happened to be standing in front of.

"You're asking me to say nothing," she said slowly.

"I'm asking you to let me—yes. If you like. That's part of it."

"About any of it."

"Not a word. Unless you want to. I'd never press you."

"And you'd say nothing yourself. About the glass. About where it came from."

"Good Lord, no. Why should I? It was nobody's fault. These things happen."

She shut her eyes for a second. When she opened them she had made up her mind. I saw it happen.

She nodded, once.

"Yes," said Hester Quaile.

* * *

She said yes.

I've set it down as it happened. Those were the words I used and those were hers. I asked, "Will you?" and she said, "Yes."

I am aware that the reader may by now have a suspicion. The reader has had the advantage of seeing that conversation written down, in cold print, with both sides of it. I had only my own side. I knew what I was asking. It didn't cross my mind that she might have heard a different question. I asked a girl to marry me and she nodded and said yes, and I went up like a rocket.

I think I said, "Oh, I *say*." I know that I seized her hand. It was very cold. She let me have it for a moment, in an absent way, and then she drew it back. She didn't do it unkindly. Her attention had gone elsewhere.

"He's awake," she said.

My uncle had opened his eyes. He was looking at me.

"You're Dorothea's grandson," said my Uncle Leo. "You were to ask me something at one. I've a note of it." He hadn't, of course. But he tapped his forehead. "It's past one. Ask."

Now here was a thing. I had come to Compline to borrow five thousand pounds. For the last several hours I had thought of nothing but clocks, vicars, and a pair of Cornish eyes. I'd honestly forgotten the Thracian valley. But I was now an engaged man, with responsibilities. And Dame Octavia had told me to get it in writing.

"Well, sir," I said. "It's rather a cheek. The fact is, I'm in the wine trade."

"Are you good at it?"

"No, sir."

"Why do you do it?"

I had never been asked. "I like the people," I said. "And I like the stuff. I like the fact that somebody spent a whole year making it and you can tell what sort of year he had."

"That is a reason," said my uncle. "It's very nearly mine. Go on."

So I told him about the Bulgarian and the four hundred cases. I told him the price. I told him I had three hundred and twelve pounds and needed five thousand by the end of the month.

"Is it a good wine?" he asked.

I have been proud of very few things in my life, but I'm proud of what I said next. I'd had a great deal to drink and I had just been accepted, and I suppose I was past lying.

"No, sir," I said. "It's a cheap wine. It's a perfectly honest cheap red that tastes of plums and doesn't give you a headache. People will be able to buy it for two pounds ninety-nine who can't afford anything else, and they won't be cheated. That's all I can say for it."

My uncle looked at me through his glass.

"It's the same as your watch," he said. "Lionel!"

Mr Fenn came. He had the look of a man who has been called to a bedside too often.

"My cheque book. The left-hand drawer of the morning-room desk. And a pen that writes."

"Leo, I really must advise—"

"You've been advising me for forty years, Lionel, and I've taken it for thirty-nine. It is the Odd Hour. Fetch the book."

He fetched it. My uncle wrote the cheque on his knee, on top of the rug. He wrote it in a hand like copperplate, without a tremor. *Pay T. Pargeter Esq. Five thousand pounds.* He signed it, and he blotted it on his own cuff, which already had a mark or two on it. I noticed that. I remember thinking he had been at the claret himself. I thought nothing more about those marks for years.

Where the date should go, he hesitated. He looked up at the nearest clock. Then he wrote the time. He put *1.31 a.m.* and nothing else.

"There," he said, and held it out. "For plums. Cash it on Monday morning before I forget that I wrote it."

"Sir, I don't know how to—"

"Don't. I can't bear to be thanked. It makes me feel that I've been stingy up to now, which I have." He was struggling up out of his chair. Hester went to help him and he put her aside, quite gently. "No, my dear. I'm very well. I've never been better. I have had an hour I didn't expect." He stood, swaying a little, and looked round his hall as if he were counting it. "I must just see to something. There's a clock stopped."

I had the cheque in my hand. I had one more thing to do, and I felt that there'd never be a better moment. I felt in my pocket and got hold of a wheel.

"Uncle Leo. There's something else. I ought to have told you at once. It's about a clock. A small one. In the Blue Room. I'm afraid I—"

He held up a hand. It was a long white hand, with the palm toward me, and it stopped me like a policeman's.

"No," he said.

"But sir—"

"I said that the matter of the clocks was closed. I said that I didn't wish to hear it spoken of in this house again. You were present. I meant you along with the rest." He looked down at me quite sternly from his great height. "Whatever you have done to a clock, young man, I forgave it half an hour ago. I'll not have it dug up to be forgiven twice. It is untidy."

He walked away. He went across to the fireplace, to the tall dark clock on the left of it, the discreet one, and opened the long door and stood looking into the case. I saw the man Cade come in from the back of the hall and go over to him. Cade was soaked to the thighs and had dried blood down one side of his face. He looked like a man who has been in a fight with a canal.

I didn't hear what they said to each other. I turned to Hester.

She had gone a few steps after my uncle and stopped. She was watching the two of them at the clock.

"I only wanted to tell him," I said. "It's been on my conscience all night. You don't think it was one of the good ones?"

"What?"

"The clock. In the Blue Room. I trod on it."

"I don't know anything about clocks," Hester said. It was the last thing she said to me that night. I'd only turned my back for a minute, to fold the cheque and put it in my note-case. When I looked again, my uncle had gone out of the front door and she was standing at the tall clock with her hand on its glass. She shut the case, and went upstairs.

* * *

I told Dame Octavia. I had to tell someone.

She was in her chair by the fire with her shoes off and her feet on the fender. They were large feet, in grey wool stockings. She heard me out.

"The nurse," she said.

"Miss Quaile. Hester."

"You met her this afternoon."

"On the stairs. At twenty past five."

"And she's accepted you."

"Just now. By the door."

"In so many words?"

"In one word. But it was the right one."

Dame Octavia looked at me for some time. She had small, shrewd, pouchy eyes, like an elephant's.

"Your grandmother took three years to accept a man," she said. "And then it was the wrong one. So perhaps there's something in doing it quickly. I refused Leo Vane, did you know that? In 1935. I've never told anybody. He asked me at a meet, on a horse, and I thought he was asking because his mother had told him to, and I said no to teach him a lesson. I thought he'd ask again. He went off to Switzerland to learn about watches, and when he came back he'd got Marguerite." She drank some champagne. "She was the right one. I'll say that. I never could dislike her, and God knows I tried. She had more life in her than the rest of this house put together. She used to stand in the middle of that floor on this night and make them all say what they thought of each other. It was like lancing a boil. They hated it, and they were better for a year."

"Was that her dress? The grey one?"

"That was her dress. That was the one she's painted in. I don't know what Imogen was thinking of. Or I do, and it was a brave thing, and I couldn't have done it." She was quiet. "He thought it was her. Did you see his face? He's waited twenty years for somebody to come down those stairs, and tonight she came. And he got to tell her whatever it was. I've no idea what he said. I hope it was the right thing."

"He told her the time of the tide," I said. "I heard him."

"Did he." Her big face worked. "Yes. He would. That's about as near as Leo ever got."

She held out her glass. I filled it.

"Did you get your money?"

I showed her the cheque. She held it at arm's length.

"He's not dated it."

"He's put the time."

"So he has. Half past one in the morning. Well, that's Leo all over; it's the only thing he's ever been sure of." She gave it back. "Cash it Monday. First thing. Be on the doorstep when they open. Fenn'll stop it if he can; he's got that look."

"He wouldn't. Would he?"

"He's a solicitor. It's what they're for." She patted my hand. "Never mind. You've got the gel. That's more than I came away with."

* * *

The tower clock struck two.

I heard it through the open door. It was the big bell only, twice, quite slow. I remember thinking that there was something missing. All night there had been that little *ting-tang* before the hour, like a man clearing his throat before he speaks. This time there was nothing. Just the two strokes, out of the dark, and then the dark again.

All the clocks in the hall took it up. They struck two, one after another, the silvery one and the cracked one and the deep slow one by the stairs.

"That's that," said Dame Octavia. "It's over. Nothing we've said counts. You may consider yourself unengaged, if you like."

"I don't like."

"No. I didn't suppose you would." She shut her eyes. "Go away, boy. I'm eighty next month and I've been up since six."

The hall had emptied. Mr Fenn had gone off to the library with his envelope. Gail came and took the plates. Kit and Saskia had vanished. The vicar slept. My uncle had not come back; I supposed that he was seeing to his clock, wherever it was, and that Hester had gone after him. I shut one leaf of the front door, because of the draught, and left the other.

The clock was still on the hall table, on its blanket. The Copy. Nobody had come to take it away. Its little moon looked at me out of the corner of its eye. I thought it had the air of a guest who has stayed after the others have left and doesn't know how to go.

I found that I was ravenous. I'd had a plate of trifle since dinner, and I had carried two men and a clock. I thought of the goose.

So at a quarter past two I went down the kitchen stairs. I know the time, because I looked up at the wall clock from the bottom step, from habit, and it said twenty-three minutes past. I've since had that corrected.

The lamp was burning on the table, and somebody was sitting on the table next to it, with his feet on a chair.

It was Edmund.

* * *

I stood still. I think my mouth was open.

He had a plate beside him with the remains of the goose on it, and he was pulling the meat off a thigh bone with his fingers and eating it. He had a tumbler of something. He had taken his tie off and opened his collar. He was soaking wet. His hair was plastered down, and his trousers were dark to the knee.

He was a sight. The lump on the left side of his forehead had come up to the size of a hen's egg and gone purple, and the eye below it was half shut. There was dried blood in his hair at the back. And there was more, which I hadn't seen before and couldn't account for. The left side of his neck and his left ear were speckled with it, in little dots, like a man who has cut himself shaving a dozen times. His collar on that side was red. He kept hitching his left shoulder, as if his braces were too tight.

But he was sitting up, on a table, eating. He looked round when he heard me on the stairs. And he grinned.

"Hallo," he said. "It's the pudding."

"Edmund! Good Lord. You're awake."

"So they tell me. I'm not altogether convinced." He waved the bone at me. "Come in. Sit down. Have some goose. Was it you who put me in a wheelbarrow?"

I sat down on the chair nearest the stairs. My legs had gone.

"It was Mrs Pask, mostly. I helped."

"I thought I'd dreamed it. I dreamed I was being wheeled to my own funeral. I was holding a church on my stomach. It was heavy, and somebody kept telling me to go back to sleep." He drank. "I've had some bad nights on this island, but I don't think I've ever before been buried by the cook."

"You'd fallen down the ice-house steps."

"Had I? Is that what I did?" He put his hand up, gingerly, to the lump. He looked for a moment as if he were listening for something that he couldn't quite hear. "No. I didn't fall. But let's say I did. It's as good as anything."

"You'd had a sleeping draught. In your brandy. The vicar had the other half; he's still out. Miss Quaile says—"

"I know what I'd had. I knew when I tasted it. I've lived in the same house as that bottle for three years." He laughed, shortly. "Poor old girl. She must have been desperate. I'd no idea she cared that much."

I didn't follow that. I let it go by.

"Edmund, your neck's bleeding."

"Is it still? It stings like the devil." He hitched the shoulder. "Some damned fool letting off at duck. I was up the tower, with a lamp. I suppose I looked like a goose. I got the edge of the pattern. If I'd been standing a foot to the left you'd be picking shot out of me with a fork." He shook his head. "Don't mention that upstairs. Father's had quite enough to forgive for one night."

"Up the tower? But you were asleep. We left you asleep. You were snoring."

"I woke up."

"With that much inside you? The vicar—"

"The vicar's seventy-four and weighs nine stone, and his idea of a debauch is a second sherry. I've been drinking a bottle a day since 1974. It takes more than that to keep me down." He pulled off another strip of goose. "Mind you, I shouldn't care to do it again. I got up those stairs on my hands and knees. I had the idea that there was a boat, and that I had to tell it to go away."

"A boat?"

"Never mind. It's gone. It went without me, and without what it came for, and I find that I don't give a damn." He looked at me with his one good eye and the other. "Do you know, Toby, I haven't felt like this since I was a boy? I feel as if somebody had taken a hundredweight off my back. I've been hit on the head and wheeled about and shot at, and I'm as happy as a king. I suppose it's concussion."

I was very much relieved. I had been carrying that mounting block about with me for an hour and a half. I had my mouth open to tell him. I was going to say, *Edmund, there's something I must own up to. The second bang was me.* I had got as far as "Edmund, I'm afraid I—"

"I've just had a talk with my father," he said.

He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the bone in his hand.

"In the clock room. Up the tower. He came up, by himself, in his pumps. God knows how he managed the ladder. He stood there in the door and looked at me as if I'd risen from the dead. He kept touching me. He put his hand on my face." He turned the bone over. "It's the first time I've talked to him in twenty years. I mean talked. We've said good morning. He thinks he's travelled in time, or that I'm a ghost, I couldn't make out which. It doesn't matter. He was listening. So I told him a thing."

"What thing?"

"A thing I should have told him in 1974. And my sister. And a man across the water who's dead now, so that's one I've missed." He was silent a moment. "I've had it in my throat for twenty years, like a fish bone. And tonight I just said it. It took about two minutes. I'd always thought it would take longer."

"What did he say?"

"He said, 'Then she didn't leave me.' That's all. He said it about six times, as if he were winding something." Edmund put the bone down on the plate. "And then he said, 'You were only a boy.' I was thirty-two."

I didn't understand a word of it. I don't think he expected me to. He was talking to me the way one talks to a dog, or a stranger on a train.

He wiped his fingers on his trousers and reached for his glass.

"Well," he said, in his ordinary voice. "That's me. What about you? You wanted a word, about nine hours ago. You said you'd pinched a clock, and I laughed at you. What was all that?"

Now I should have told him. I see that. I had only to put my hand in my pocket. But I had a bigger piece of news, and it came out first.

"I'm engaged," I said.

"You're what?"

"Engaged to be married. To Miss Quaile. I asked her in the Hour and she said yes."

He stared at me. Then his whole battered face lit up. He looked ten years younger. He put down his glass and held out his hand, and I got up and took it, and he nearly broke my fingers.

"My dear chap. That's the best thing I've heard tonight, and I've heard one or two. She's worth ten of anybody in this house. She's the only one in three years who's ever told my father the truth. She told him last week that his wife was dead; she said she wouldn't lie to him about it, and he could shout as much as he liked. He shouted. Then he thanked her." He let go of my hand. "She's had a rotten time of it somewhere. I don't know where. She doesn't say. You be good to her."

"I mean to."

"And don't wait. That's my advice, and it's the only piece I've ever given that was worth having. Don't wait for the right time. There isn't one in this house. We don't keep it." He laughed. "God, listen to me. I'll be giving away the bride."

There was a step in the stone passage that led to the scullery. It was a light, quick step.

Edmund heard it. He turned his head. I saw his face change. He didn't look frightened. He looked like a man who has been expecting a visitor and hears the gate.

"That's my sister," he said quietly. "Run along, Toby."

"Oughtn't I to—"

"No. She's got something to say to me. She's been saving it up for twenty years. I can tell by her feet." He picked up the tumbler and finished what was in it. "I've said mine tonight. It's only fair that she should have hers."

I went up the kitchen stairs. At the top I looked back.

The last I saw of my cousin Edmund that night he was sitting on the kitchen table at a quarter past two, with a plate of cold goose beside him. He had a lump on his head the size of an egg, and he was in the best temper I ever saw him in. He lifted his hand to me. I have seldom seen a man more alive.

I shut the baize door.

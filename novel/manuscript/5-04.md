## 4. The Gun

He cleaned the gun that afternoon at the kitchen table.

It did not want it. His father had kept it as he kept everything. But the man took it down and broke it and drew a rag through the barrels, and oiled the action, and wiped the stock. It was something to do with his hands while he thought.

He thought it out as he would have thought out a piece of work with stock.

Vane would be at the Hythe at a quarter to two. That was sure. A man who has paid two hundred pounds for a boat is where the boat is. The Hythe was on the north shore of the Ness, away from the house, at the end of the sea wall. There was nothing there but the staging and a spinney of thorn. A man could wait in that spinney and not be seen.

To be at the Hythe he must be on the island. To be on the island he must cross before five past ten.

He would not go to the house. He had never been in it and he would not begin on a night when they had company and a policeman. He would cross at nine, on the mare, and put her in the old glasshouse on the south side, where no one went. He knew that place. He would walk round by the wall to the spinney and sit down under a thorn. He would wait there four hours and a half, if he had to.

And when Edmund Vane came along the wall with his box, he would step out.

He turned the gun in his hands. The wood was dark with fifty years of his father's palm.

He was not going to use it. He had told himself that in the morning and he told himself again. He was a stockman. He had killed sick beasts and he had killed coyotes and once a bear that was at the calves. He had never pointed a gun at a man and did not mean to.

But he knew Vane. He knew what would happen if he stepped out of that spinney with nothing in his hands. Vane would look at him. He was six foot two and had been to a school where they teach it. He would say, Garrow, isn't it? You're trespassing. And he would walk on to his boat, and the man would be left standing on the wall with his question in his mouth, as his father had stood for an hour with a lantern.

His father had said it. *He always minded a gun. He was gun-shy as a boy.*

He would carry it broken, over his arm. He would let Vane see it. That was all. He wanted him stopped for the length of time it takes to answer one thing. *Which was it. Was she coming or was she going.* And when he had his answer he would stand aside, and Vane could go to Holland or to hell.

He put two cartridges in the pocket of his coat. He looked at them first. Number five. He told himself that an empty gun is a thing a man can see is empty, in a good light, and that he would not have Vane laugh.

He found the canvas case in the cupboard under the stairs and strapped it under the flap of his own saddle. He had brought the saddle with him across the Atlantic in a crate. It was the one thing he owned that he would not have sold. It had a deep seat and a horn, and the men in the village had come to the yard gate to look at it.

When it was done he sat down in his father's chair. It was four o'clock. He looked at the window, and the lamp on the sill, unlit.

He thought about the woman on the road.

He had not let himself, all day. He had kept it out with the lawyer and the public house and the gun. But it had been there underneath, like water under ice.

If she was in that house.

He would not see her. He was not going to the house. He would be in a spinney at the other end of the island, in the dark, and she would be at a table with candles on it. She would never know that he had been within a quarter mile.

He told himself that that was best.

* * *

At dusk he went across to the cottage.

The old sister opened the door a hand's breadth. She had a shawl on and a candle. She looked up at him with her head on one side like a hen.

I've come to see the child, he said.

Eh?

Wren. The child.

She's abed.

It's not six.

She were tired. She been drawing all day. She had her tea and went up with her book. The old woman's eyes were small and bright and they did not quite meet his. Don't you go waking her, Tom Garrow. She's a good little thing and she wants her sleep. You'll have her in the morning.

I'd only look at her.

You'd wake her, with them boots. And then I'll have her down here till midnight asking what things are for. She began to close the door. Dolly said she's to stop the night and that's what she'll do. You go on home.

He stood on the step. He could have put his hand on the door. She was eighty-three and weighed less than a ewe lamb.

But he had been brought up in that yard, and Vi Pask had boxed his ears there when he was six for chasing her ducks. He took off his hat.

Tell her I came, he said.

I'll tell her.

The door shut. He heard the bolt.

He stood a moment in the yard looking up. There was a small window under the thatch at the gable end, where the stairs came out. It was dark. He thought of her up there with her notebook under the pillow.

He has been asked since if he felt nothing. Standing in that yard. He felt that he would have liked to see her face before he did what he was going to do. That was all he felt. He put his hat on and went to see to the stock.

* * *

It was the stock that undid his plan.

He went round them at eight with a lantern, as his father had every night of his life. The wind had got up. It had gone into the south-west, as he had known it would, and it came across the flats in long hard gusts with rain in them. The tide had turned an hour since. He could hear it, out in the dark, beginning to talk in the creeks.

The ewes should have been in. On a making tide with weather coming they should have been up on the hard ground by the sluice, in a bunch, with their backs to the wind. He stood on the wall and held up the light.

They were not in. He could see them, a long way out on the saltings, a pale smear in the rain. They were standing. They were all facing one way. And they were making a noise, a low steady uneasy sound, that he had not heard since he was a boy and knew at once.

The leader was down.

He went out to them. It was half a mile, over ground that was all holes, and he went at a run with the lantern swinging. He jumped the small creeks and waded the big ones. There was water in them already. He could feel it pulling at his boots.

He found her by the noise the others made. They were stood round a creek on the far side, a deep one, with steep clay banks. She was at the bottom of it.

She was on her back. A sheep that gets on its back in a hollow cannot rise; the weight of the fleece holds it. She had gone to cross, as she had crossed ten thousand times, and the bank had given under her. She lay with her four legs in the air and her black face turned up to him. Her fleece was full of water. The tide was in the creek, a foot of it, and climbing. It was over her ears.

She did not struggle. She had done that and it was past. She looked at him out of one yellow eye.

He put the lantern on the bank. He went down into the creek.

He has told this part more than the rest. The one who listened would ask for it. It took him two hours.

She weighed, wet, as much as a man. The clay would not hold his feet. He got his arms under her and heaved and went down on his face in the water with her on top of him. He got up and tried again. He got her onto her side and then onto her breast, and she could not stand; her legs had no feeling in them. He had to hold her head up out of the tide with one hand and rub her legs with the other, there in the creek, with the water at his waist and the rain coming flat.

He talked to her. He did not know what he said. It was what he said to any beast.

When she could bear weight he tried to drive her up the bank. She could not climb it. He got under her. He put his shoulder to her rump and pushed her up that clay a foot at a time, and slid back, and pushed. He lost count of the times. He tore the nails of one hand.

He got her out. She stood on the top with her head hanging and the water running off her. The others closed round her. They put their noses to her.

And they would not move.

That was the thing. She was the one who knew. They had stood out there on the open saltings for two hours with the sea coming, three hundred of them, rather than leave the place where she was. Now she was up, and they waited for her to lead them home. And she could not. She took three steps and stopped. She was spent.

The water was over the low ground between them and the wall. He could see it in the lantern, a sheet of it, shining, with the grass tops showing through. In half an hour it would be over their backs.

He picked her up. He got her across his shoulders, as a man carries a deer, with her forelegs in one fist and her hind legs in the other. He took the lantern in his teeth by its wire.

He walked. He went straight for the hard by the sluice, through the water, with a hundredweight and a half of wet sheep on his neck. He did not look round. He heard them come. He heard twelve hundred feet go into the tide behind him.

They followed him in. All of them. They came up onto the hard in a long file, at his heels, and stood.

He set her down under the wall, out of the wind. He knelt by her. He was shaking so that he could not unclench his hands. She lay with her legs folded and her eyes shut. He thought she had died. He put his palm on her side, and it rose.

*That's a lot to put on one sheep,* the child had said.

He looked at his watch. He had to hold it close to the lantern. The glass was smeared with clay.

It was twenty past eleven.

* * *

He stood up. He turned and looked at the Wade.

There was no Wade. There was the sea. It ran from the foot of the ramp to the island, black, with the white coming off the tops of it and blowing. It had been over the road for an hour and a quarter. He could see the nearest of the withies standing out of it, and beyond them nothing.

He had been going to cross at nine.

He stood on the hard with the rain in his face. He had forgotten. For two hours and more he had not once thought of Edmund Vane. He had thought of a sheep. There had not been room in him for anything else. He had been his father's son. He had done what any Garrow would have done on that ground on that night, and it had cost him the one thing his father had asked.

At a quarter to two there would be a boat at the Hythe. He would be standing here. He would watch its light go out past the point and down the river.

He began to laugh. It was not a good sound, and the wind took it. He had saved the old man's flock. They would be mutton by March; the paper was in his shirt. He had carried a ewe half a mile on his back so that she could go up the ramp of a lorry on Lady Day with the rest. And for that he had let the man go who was putting them off.

He walked back to the yard. He was so tired that he could not feel his feet.

As he came round the end of the barn, every light in the village went out.

He saw it happen. There had been a string of them along the wall behind him, small and yellow. They went together, like a hand closed. There was the dark, and the wind, and across the water one lamp, in the porch of the house. That one burned oil.

He stood in the yard. He was wet through and plastered with clay. He had lost his hat in the creek.

He thought, I will go in. I'll light the range. I'll sit in his chair and tell him that I tried.

The mare whickered.

She was at the door of the loose box. He could see the white of her in the dark. She had her head out over the half door. She was not looking at him. She had her ears pricked, hard forward, and she was looking past him, down the yard, at the ramp, and the sea.

She whickered again. It was low. It was the sound a mare makes to a foal that has gone too far off.

He went to her. He put his hand on her neck. She was trembling. It was not fear. He knew every sign in a horse, and that was not it. She leaned on the door so that the bolt rattled.

What is it, old lady?

She threw up her head. She struck the door with her knee.

He looked where she was looking. There was nothing. There was the black water.

No, he said. Not you. You're twenty year old.

She stood. She breathed. She looked at the sea.

He had drawn her out of her dam on that marsh, by lamplight, beside that road. She had been foaled within sound of the Rill. She had gone over the Wade a thousand times, at every state of it, with his father on her back, in fog, in snow, when the old man had lain late at the market and cut it fine. She knew where the stones were as she knew where her feet were.

She had been his father's horse for twenty years. She had stood in this yard every night, looking over that door at a lamp in a window. He wondered what she knew.

It can't be done, he said to her. There's five foot on it. There's seven in the Rill. *An hour and a half before, the Wade belongs to God.* That's Dad's own word.

The mare put her nose against his chest. She pushed.

He stood there in the rain with his forehead on her face.

He has said since that he did not decide it. He says that it was decided in the stable, by a horse. He does not expect to be believed. He only knows that he went into the loose box for the saddle with his mind made up to hang it on its peg, and that he found he was putting it on her.

He cinched it. He strapped the gun in its case under the near flap. He put the two cartridges where he could reach them. He lit his father's big lantern, the one that had been held up on the wall, and hung it from the horn.

He led her out. She came like a colt.

At the foot of the ramp he stopped her. The sea was at his boots. It came in and drew back. He could hear the Rill, out in the middle, a long way off, like a train on a bridge.

He looked back at the cottage. The window under the thatch was dark.

If I go in this, he said aloud, and don't come out, she's nobody.

He has thought about that too. He stood on the edge of that water for perhaps a minute, with one hand on the horn, and he thought of the child asleep under the thatch. He very nearly turned the mare round.

But he thought of what he would have to say to her when she was grown. *Your grandfather asked one thing of me. I'd the chance, and I was afraid of getting wet.*

He looked at his watch. It was twenty to twelve.

He put his foot in the stirrup. The mare did not wait to be asked. Before he was fairly in the saddle she had walked down off the land into the sea.

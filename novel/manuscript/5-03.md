## 3. The Town

He took the child across the yard to the cottage at half past seven. It was a raw morning with the light just coming.

Dolly Pask was loading her van. It was a small grey van with the name of a baker painted out on the side, and she had the back doors open and was putting in crates. There were geese in the crates, plucked, four of them, and a sack of something, and a tin trunk. Her niece was helping, a big red girl of seventeen who stopped and stared at his hat.

I'd forgot, Dolly said. It's the night. I'm over there till morning. They've a dinner.

What dinner?

The same as they've always had. For the clocks. Thirteen to table and one of them a ghost. She shut her mouth on that. Vi'll have the child. She's deaf, mind, but she's not daft. She raised six.

The old sister was in the doorway. She was eighty-three, small and bent, in a man's cardigan. She looked at the child and held out her hand and the child went and took it.

You be good, the man said.

I'm always good.

You be good anyhow.

He walked back to the farm. He did not look round. If he had, he would have seen Dolly Pask standing by the open doors of the van with her hands on her hips, looking at the old woman and the child, and then at the tide board on the wall, and then at the child again.

He caught the mare and brought her into the yard. He lit the forge and blew it up. He had the two hind shoes off her and was dressing the near foot with the rasp when he heard the van start. He did not lift his head. It went up the ramp behind him in low gear and along the wall, and down onto the Wade.

He glanced at it then, under his arm. It was going out over the stones with the sea in the creeks on either hand. It had twenty minutes to spare, not more. He could see the crates through the back window, and the sack on top of them, and Dolly's niece in the passenger seat with her head turned, saying something over her shoulder into the back.

He thought nothing of it. He bent to the hoof.

He went on thinking nothing of it for the rest of his life.

* * *

He was fitting the second shoe when the car came.

It came along the wall from the village and stopped at the head of the ramp. It was a big car, a town car, the colour of mud. A man got out of it. He was heavy and grey-faced and wore a raincoat and a hat with the brim turned down. He stood and read the tide board. He read it and looked at his watch, and looked at the road, and read it again.

The man in the yard watched him.

He knew the kind. He had seen them in the stock towns, at the edge of a sale ring, with their thumbs in their belts. It was the way the stranger stood, square, and the way his eyes went over a thing and took its number. He had a policeman's shoes.

The stranger turned and looked down into the yard. He looked at the horse and the forge and the man. He took his time. The man let him. He had the hoof between his knees and he did not put it down.

Then the stranger looked at the island. He looked at it the way a man looks at a job of work.

He got into his car and drove down onto the Wade.

The man watched him the whole way over. The tide was at the stones. The car went slowly through the dip and across the planks, past the Perch, and up the far side, and out of sight behind the point.

So the Vanes had sent for the law. Or the law had come for the Vanes. He did not know which. Either way there was a policeman going to that house on the day of its dinner, in a plain car, alone, and he had gone over on the last of the ebb so that he could not get back.

He finished the shoe. He ran his thumb round the clenches. He let the foot down and straightened his back and took off his hat and wiped his face.

He looked across at the house.

It was the habit of a fortnight. He had done it every morning since he came. He looked at the long roof and the chimneys and the row of upper windows on the west side.

He could not have said what he was looking for. He was half a mile off. But he stood there with his hat in his hand, and he felt it again, what he had felt at the door in the night. Something was looking back.

He put his hand inside his coat. The oilcloth was there.

He put the mare in the barn.

* * *

He drove to the county town in the hired car. It was twenty miles. He had a clean shirt on and his father's black tie.

The lawyer's office was up a stair over a building society. A girl at a desk said Mr Fenn did not see people on a Saturday without an appointment. He said he would wait. He stood by the door with his hat in his hands. He stood for forty minutes. People came and went. The girl looked at him now and then, and at last she got up and went through.

The room she showed him into had a desk as big as a hay wagon. There was a man behind it. He was old and dry, with a stiff collar, and he had his fingers together.

Mr Garrow, he said. I am sorry about your father.

You wrote him a letter.

I wrote to his representatives. It reached you, I think, at an unfortunate moment. I was not informed of the date of the funeral. Had I been, I should have held it back a week.

It'd have said the same in a week.

It would. The lawyer did not look away. He had pale tired eyes. I act for the estate, Mr Garrow. I was given an instruction and I carried it out. I do not expect you to think well of me for it.

Who give the instruction?

Mr Edmund Vane.

It's his land?

It is his father's land. Mr Leopold Vane is the owner. He is eighty-one. He has not concerned himself with the estate for some years, and Mr Edmund has his authority to manage it.

Then I'll see the father.

You will not find that possible. He sees no one. He is not always— The lawyer stopped. He is an old man.

Then I'll see the son.

On what matter?

The man stood in front of the desk. There was a chair. He had not been asked to sit in it and he would not have.

There's two, he said. There's the farm. My people have had that ground since 1811. There's three hundred ewes on it that can't be moved; they're hefted. If he wants it for caravans, I'd like him to tell me so himself. I'd like him to stand on it and say it.

And the other?

The other's not about the farm.

The lawyer was quiet. He took his spectacles off and held them.

It's about a thing that happened on the Wade, the man said. Twenty year back tonight. My father saw the half of it from our wall. He's dead. He left me to ask after the other half.

I see, the lawyer said.

He said it very low. He put the spectacles down on the blotter and looked at them. The man waited. Out in the street a bus went by.

Mr Garrow. I knew your father for thirty years. He came up those stairs every Lady Day and every Michaelmas and paid his rent in notes, in this room. He never once sat down. I asked him to, each time. He would say, I'll not stop, Mr Fenn. In thirty years I do not think that we exchanged a hundred words. But I formed an opinion of him.

He looked up.

I am going to say something to you that I ought not. I have been solicitor to the Vane family since 1954. There are matters in that house which I have made it my business not to know. I was there on the night you speak of. I have been there on the same night every year since. I do not know what your father saw from his wall. I have never asked what anyone saw. I am not a brave man, and I have a wife.

But I will tell you this. If you go to Compline and ask Edmund Vane that question, I think that he will answer it. I think that he has been waiting twenty years for somebody to ask.

Then I'll go.

Not tonight.

Why not tonight?

Because tonight the family keeps its anniversary. There will be guests. The old man intends to say something; I do not know what. There is a person in the house, sent by me, on a matter that does not concern you. And Edmund will have been drinking since noon. The lawyer put his hands flat on the desk. Go tomorrow, Mr Garrow. Go on Monday. I shall speak to him myself this evening; I dine there. I shall tell him that you have a right to be heard, on both counts. I can do that much. I ought to have done it for your father.

The man turned his hat round in his fingers.

Tomorrow, he said.

Tomorrow. In daylight. By the road.

He nodded. He put his hat on. At the door the lawyer spoke again.

Mr Garrow. He is not a wicked man. I should like you to know that before you see him. I have watched him for twenty years. Whatever he did, he has not had a day's peace from it.

Nor had my father, the man said.

He went down the stairs.

* * *

He meant it. He would wait till the Sunday. He drove home through the flat fields with the tie loosened and the window down and he thought that it was the right way. The lawyer had talked sense. A man did not go to another man's house on the night of a family dinner with a question like that.

He would go in the morning. He would walk across at the ebb with his hat in his hand.

He stopped at the public house in the village. He had not been in it since he was twenty-three. He wanted bread and cheese and he wanted to hear what was said of the farm.

It was the same room. There was the same long settle under the window and the same fire. The same dart board, he would have sworn. There were eight or nine men in it. They were men he had known as boys, grown thick and grey, and their fathers, grown small. The talk stopped when he came in.

He went to the bar. The landlord was a stranger, a young man.

A half of bitter, he said. And something to eat.

You'll be Garrow.

I am.

Your father had a slate here. It's clear. He come in the week before he was took bad and paid it to the penny. He said he'd not go owing.

The man put a note on the bar.

Then I'll pay for mine, he said.

He took his glass to the end of the settle. He did not drink it. He had not drunk in nine years. His wife had asked it of him when the child was born, and he had found that he did not miss it. He sat with the glass in front of him and ate the bread.

The talk came back by degrees. They talked round him. They talked of the price of lambs and a man who had lost a boat. One of them said across the room, You'll have had your letter, Tom. He said he had. Another said it was a wicked thing. A third said the Vanes had always been hard landlords and soft-headed with it, and that Mr Edmund would sell his own grandmother for the price of a case of Scotch.

He's sold near enough everything else, somebody said.

That was a man by the fire. He was sixty or so, broad, in a blue jersey, with a face like a board that had been left out. He had hands on him like a pair of dock fenders. The man on the settle knew him. He was Ostler, from the point, who had the oyster layings. He had been a hard drinker and a good boatman twenty years ago, and he was plainly both still. He had three empty glasses in front of him and a fourth going.

What's he sold now, Dick, somebody said.

I couldn't say. I'm not paid to say. Ostler drank. I'm paid to be at the Hythe at the slack with my launch and no lights, that's all I know. Two hundred pound. In an envelope. Give me in that car park yesterday dinnertime by a feller in a camel coat, out of a Jaguar motor car.

For doing what?

For taking a box off. And a passenger.

What passenger?

A big feller with a gammy leg, I was told. Who's that sound like to you?

There was a laugh. It was not a kind one.

He's running, a man said. By God. He's doing a flit.

I never said so.

Where to?

Parkeston Quay. By five in the morning. There's a boat goes from there to the Hook of Holland at nine o'clock of a Sunday. I'll leave you to do the sum.

What's in the box?

Whatever's left, I should think, Ostler said. He was pleased with himself. He had the room. Whatever the old man's got that's worth the lifting. He's had the silver, I've heard, and the pictures. There'll be a clock or two.

Tonight, the landlord said. On the night of their dinner.

Top of the tide. Quarter to two. That's the only time you'd get a boat in there, a night like it's going to be. There's wind coming. Ostler tapped his nose. I didn't ought to have told you. I was told to keep it close. But I've took against that family. My father fished off that Hythe forty year and old Vane put a chain on it.

The man on the settle had put down his bread.

He sat still. He looked at the glass of beer. There was a ring of froth going down the inside of it.

Tomorrow, the lawyer had said. In daylight. By the road. *I shall speak to him myself this evening.*

And at a quarter to two in the morning Edmund Vane would step down off the Hythe into a boat and be carried out of that country. By nine he would be on a ship. By Monday he would be somewhere in Europe with a box of his father's clocks, where no Garrow could ever find him.

The lawyer did not know it. He had sat behind his desk with his dry honest face and said, go tomorrow. He was going to that dinner tonight to plead for a hearing from a man who would be gone before the port was round.

There would be no tomorrow. The man saw that quite clearly. If the question was to be asked, it would be asked tonight, on the Ness, before the slack. Or it would not be asked at all, and his father had held on for it for nothing.

He got up. He left the beer.

You've not touched your drink, Tom, somebody said.

No.

You're a changed man.

I've a child, he said.

He went to the door. Ostler was looking at him from the fire. The oysterman had gone a little red. He had remembered, late, whose son was sitting on the settle, and whose landlord he had been talking about.

Here, he said. You'll not go saying where you had that.

I never heard it, the man said.

He went out. The wind had begun. It was coming off the land, as yet, light, with rain in it. He stood by the car and smelled it. It would back, by dark. It would go round into the south-west and blow.

He looked at his watch. It was a quarter past two. The Wade would open a little after five, and shut at five past ten.

He had seven hours and three-quarters to think of a way to stand in front of Edmund Vane.

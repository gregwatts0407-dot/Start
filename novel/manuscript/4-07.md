## Chapter Seven

They went down through the orchard. He led the mare, and Imogen walked at his other side with her hand in the crook of his arm. The grass was long and soaking. The apple trees stood about them, black and bent, with a few late fruit still hanging, and the moon came through the branches and lay in pieces on the ground.

Neither of them spoke. There was the sound of the mare's feet, and the creak of leather, and ahead of them, growing, the sound of water.

The orchard ended at the sea wall. There was a gap in the bank there, with a gate, and beyond the gate the ramp where the Wade left the island. She had come up that ramp on the Friday night with a bag in her hand.

There was no ramp now. The sea began at the gate.

It lay in front of them, flat and bright, from their feet to the far shore. The moon had laid a track across it, a long road of white light, straight as a rule, that ran out from the gatepost and went away over the water to the black line of the mainland. On either side of the track the two rows of withies stood up out of the tide. Only their tops showed. They went off into the distance like the masts of a drowned fleet.

"That's a foot and a half down on what I came through," Tom said. He had stopped at the edge. He was not looking at the water. He was looking past it, and up, and she saw his nostrils move. "The wind's gone round north-west. That'll hold the ebb back a bit in the Rill. Your father said three foot?"

"Three feet, and a strong set to the north."

"He's not far out. I'd have said three and a half." He glanced at his watch, and then, with a half smile, at her. "Eighteen minutes to four. He said twenty to. I'd not like to tell him he was two minutes slow."

"He'd say that you were two minutes fast."

"Likely he would." He turned to the mare. He ran his hand down her neck and along her back, and bent, and felt each of her legs to the hoof. He spoke to her under his breath. Then he stood up and looked at Imogen.

"Can you ride astride in that?"

She gathered up the grey silk in both hands, above her knees, under the tweed coat. "I rode astride before I could read."

"She's no saddle to speak of for two. You'll go up in front, in the seat, and take the reins. I'll walk at her head as far as I can, and get up behind you when it deepens. At the Rill I'll get down again."

"Get down? In that?"

"I'll swim it with her. She'll carry one through that set, with the tide falling. She'll not carry two. I asked it of her once tonight and that's enough." He said it quite simply. "You'll have nothing to do but sit still and hold her mane. Don't pull at her mouth. Don't try to steer. She'll go down and be carried, and you'll think she's lost it, and she'll come up on the road again thirty yards on. If you feel her go right under, you slide off on the upstream side and keep hold of the stirrup. I'll have you."

"And if you're carried?"

"I'll not be. I've swum the Oldman in spring with a calf across the saddle." He put his hands at her waist. "Up."

He lifted her as though she weighed nothing. She found the stirrup and swung her leg across. The saddle was a strange one, deep, with a high horn in front, such as she had only seen in films. It held her like a chair. The mare stood like a rock.

She was a long way up. She looked down at him, at his wet dark head.

"Tom."

"What?"

"If I don't—if anything—"

"You will. Your father's give you the time, and I've give you the horse. Between the pair of us, we ought to get one Vane woman over that water." He took the rein under the mare's chin. "Don't look at the sea. Look at the far wall. There's a light."

She looked. Half a mile off, low down, at the end of the moon's road, there was a small yellow point.

"I left the lamp in the kitchen window," he said. "It's where Dad stood."

He led them in.

* * *

The water was so cold that she felt it through her mother's boots as a kind of burning. It came over the mare's fetlocks, and then her knees. Tom walked ahead, to his thighs. He did not hurry. He went as he had always gone at anything, a step at a time, feeling for it.

The ramp fell away under them and they were on the Wade.

She could feel the road beneath the mare's feet. It was firm and ribbed, and the mare knew it. Her ears went forward. She put her head down and walked into the sea, like an old horse turning for home.

The withies went by on either side. The water rose. It was at Tom's waist, and at the mare's belly, and it pulled. Imogen felt it take hold of the horse and lean on her from the left. The mare leaned back against it and kept on.

"She's all right," Tom said. He had not turned his head. "That's only the top of it. That's nothing."

He stopped her, and came back along her side. He put his foot on Imogen's in the stirrup and his hand on the horn, and was up behind her in one movement, with the sea pouring off him. His arms came round her and took the reins. His chest was hard and wet against her back, and she could feel his heart.

"Now sit down in the saddle and let your legs hang," he said in her ear. "We'll have ten minutes of this, and then the Rill."

They went on. She had never been on the Wade at the flood. No one had. She had lived beside it for twenty-four years and had only ever seen it as a road or as the sea. She had not known there was a time between, when it was both. The stones were under them, and the water over, and the moon lay on the top like a skin. It was a world with two floors.

She thought of her mother.

She had been thinking of her all night. But that had been in a house. This was the place. It had been on this road, in this hour, twenty years ago, that her mother had come walking with a lantern, in her stockings, quickly, toward a light on a wall.

She had been past the Perch. She had been as far as this. Further.

And then a voice behind her, shouting. A second light. Her son.

Imogen had seen it from the orangery as two small stars. She had never before been where it happened. She looked at the black water sliding by, north, toward the sea. She thought of what it had been to stand on this road and feel it begin to rise round one's ankles, and to argue.

*It's what she did. At the end. You ought to know what she did.*

*She drowned. I'll not hear another word.*

She had the ring in the pocket of the coat. She could feel it against her hip, through the tweed, small and hard.

"There," Tom said.

She looked up. In front of them the two lines of withies seemed to sink. The water between them was darker, and it was moving in a different way. It had a kind of smooth swift shine on it, like oil, and little creases, and it made a sound.

And to the right of it, twenty yards off, standing up black against the moon, on its four tall legs, was the Perch.

* * *

She had not known that it would be so near. The tide was halfway up its posts. She could see the ladder going down into the water, and the little door, shut, and the tarred roof with the moonlight on it.

It was the nearest that she had ever been to the place where her mother died.

Tom had reined in. The mare stood with the sea streaming round her chest, blowing, looking at the deep water with her ears flicking. He sat still behind Imogen for a moment. Then he slid off.

He went in to the armpits. He gasped, once, and got his breath. He waded to the mare's head and took hold of the bridle by the cheek strap.

"Hold her mane," he said. "Both hands. Shut your eyes if you like."

"I'm not going to shut my eyes."

"No. You never would." He looked up at her. The moon was on his face. He was up to his neck in the North Sea in October, at four in the morning, and he was smiling. "I'll say this now, in case I've no breath for it after. I'd go through this every night for another twenty year, and count it cheap."

He turned, and said one word to the mare, and walked off the edge of the road.

It went from under them. She felt it go. One moment there was stone beneath the horse's feet and the next there was nothing, and the whole great warm body dropped, and the sea came up over the saddle and over Imogen's knees and into her lap. The cold took her breath. She had her fingers wound in the white mane. She saw Tom's head, dark and sleek as a seal's, a yard in front, with one arm stretched back to the bridle.

The mare was swimming. Imogen could feel it, the great shoulders working, the legs striking. But the Rill had her. It took her sideways, as it had taken the light that Imogen had watched from the window. The withies went away to the left. The moon's road slid out from under them. They were being carried north, down the gut, toward the open flats and the sea.

The Perch went by.

It went by so close on the right hand that she could have touched one of its legs. She saw the weed on the post, and the rusted heads of the bolts. She saw the ladder. She looked up at the black box over her head, with its shut door.

And she thought, quite clearly, in the middle of that water, with the cold at her waist: *Someone was in there.*

She did not know where the thought came from. It was like a voice. She saw nothing. She heard nothing but the sea. But it came to her with entire certainty that on the night her mother died, someone had been in that box. Someone had sat up there in the dark, above the tide, and been safe.

She had seen one person come home.

"Hold on!" Tom's voice, half choked.

The mare's feet struck. Imogen was flung forward on her neck. There was a scrabbling, a heave, another. The water fell from the saddle to the girth, to the knee. The old horse stood, shuddering, with her head down and her sides going like bellows, and under her feet there was stone.

They were on the road. They were thirty yards north of the withies, on the far side of the Rill, and the bottom was hard and ribbed and rising.

Tom came up out of the water at the mare's head, on his hands and knees. He stayed there a moment, coughing. Then he got up. He put both arms round the white neck and laid his face against it.

"That's my girl," he said. "That's my beauty. That's the last time. I'll never ask it of you again."

He looked up at Imogen. He could not speak for a moment.

"You're over," he said. "That's the deep of it. There's nothing between here and the wall now but a walk."

She was shaking so that her teeth rattled. She had lost one of her mother's boots. The grey silk was a sodden rope round her legs. She looked at him, standing in the sea to his waist, with the moon behind him, and she began to laugh, and found that she could not stop.

"I've crossed it," she said. "Tom. I've crossed it."

"You have."

"Twenty years. It took me twenty years to walk half a mile."

"You've not walked a yard of it," said Tom Garrow. "You've been carried the whole road, by a horse you named yourself. Now sit still. I'm coming up."

* * *

He was behind her again, with his arms round her. The mare went forward at a walk. The water sank to her knees.

It was then that Imogen looked back.

She had been told not to. Her brother had said it, in the scullery. *Don't look back. She looked back.* She had obeyed him in the house. She had gone down through the orchard with her face to the sea and had not once turned her head.

But they were over. The lamp in the farm window was large and yellow in front of them. She thought that it could not matter now. And she wanted to see it. She wanted to see Compline, from this side, from the back of a Garrow's horse.

She turned in the saddle and looked over Tom's shoulder.

The island lay black on the bright water. She could see the line of the trees, and the long roof, and the chimneys. She could see the tower. There was one lamp burning, in the porch, the one that her father had lit at dusk every night of his life, since he held that a house with a tide in front of it owed the traveller that much.

And there was another light.

It was very small. It was low down, at the water's edge, where the orchard gate was and the Wade began. It was not yellow, like a lantern. It was whiter and thinner, a mere point, like a glow-worm. As she looked, it moved. It came down a little way toward the sea, and stopped, and came on again.

She thought: that is Edmund.

She was sure of it. He had followed them down through the orchard with his candle in its saucer. He had come to the gate to watch them over. He had said that he would not be about in the morning. This was his way of seeing her off.

Her eyes filled. She had said such things to him. She had said that she hoped he would never sleep. And he had put their mother's ring in her hand and told her to go, and now he was standing at the edge of the tide, alone, with a lump on his head and shot in his neck, holding up a light.

She lifted her arm. She waved to it, a wide slow wave, as one waves from the deck of a ship.

"Who are you waving at?" Tom said.

"My brother. He's come down to the gate. Look."

Tom turned his head. He looked for some time. She felt him go still against her.

"That's a queer little light," he said. "That's low down, for a man."

"He's carrying a candle in a saucer."

"Is he." He was frowning. "It looks to me as if it's out on the Wade. It looks to be coming this way."

"He wouldn't. Not on foot. He knows the tide better than anyone."

"No. You're right. Nobody would." But he looked a moment longer. "There's three foot on the Rill yet. A man'd be off his legs."

A cloud went over the moon. The white road on the water went out. The island was gone, and the tower, and the little light with it. There was only the dark, and the lamp ahead.

When the cloud had passed she looked again. She could see the porch lamp. She could not find the other.

"He's gone in," she said.

"Likely." Tom shook the reins. "Come up, Mab. You can smell your stable."

The mare walked up out of the sea.

Imogen has gone back to that many times. She has stood on the wall at Saltcote, on other nights, and looked across at the place. She saw a light at the edge of the tide and gave it her brother's name. She waved to it. Then she turned her face to the shore and did not look again.

She does not know what else she could have done. She asks herself, all the same.

* * *

The kitchen at Saltcote was low and warm and smelled of wood smoke. There was a range, with the fire banked. There was a deal table, scrubbed white, and a dresser with blue plates, and a clock on the wall with a painted face. On the sill, in the window that looked toward the island, stood an oil lamp.

Tom carried her in. She could not have walked. He set her down in a high-backed wooden chair by the range. It was an old man's chair, with a cushion flattened to the shape of one body. He knelt and pulled off the one boot. He took her feet between his hands.

"They're like ice," he said. "Don't move. I've the mare to see to. I'll be ten minutes. There's a blanket."

He was gone twenty. She heard him in the yard, talking, and the sound of straw. She sat in Amos Garrow's chair with a horse blanket round her and looked at the lamp in the window.

It was where he had stood. Tom had said so. The old man had gone out to the wall with the lantern, that night. But this had been the house's light. It had burned in this window for a woman who was coming to live here.

She was the wrong one. She was twenty years late. But she had come.

She put her hand into the pocket of the wet coat. The ring was there.

When Tom came in he had a dry shirt on and his hair was rubbed up on end. He put the kettle on the range. He found a towel, and a pair of his own thick socks, and a flannel shirt that came down to her knees. He turned his back while she got out of the grey silk. It took her some time. The hooks had swollen.

She held it up, when it was off. It hung from her hands, dark with sea water, heavy as a drowned thing. It had been painted in 1956. It had been worn to twenty Hours. It had been left on a hanger for twenty years, and tonight it had crossed.

She did not know what would become of it. She found, later, that salt water does less harm to good silk than one would think. She had it cleaned by a woman in Colchester who did church vestments. In January she carried it back over the Wade, in a box, and hung it on its padded hanger at the end of the rail, and locked the door.

"Tea," Tom said. He put a mug into her hands. It was thick and white, and the tea was nearly black. "There's whisky in it. Dad's. He'd want you to have it."

She drank. The heat went down into her like a hand.

He sat on the edge of the table, facing her. He looked at her in his shirt and his socks, with her hair coming down.

"I ought to go and look at Wren," he said. "I've never left her a night before."

"Go."

"I'll not wake Vi at this hour. She's eighty-three, and deaf as a post, and she'll think it's the Germans." He rubbed his jaw. "I'll go at seven. She'll be asleep. She sleeps like the dead, that child, once she's off. I'll carry her home and you'll see her when she wakes."

"What will she think of me?"

"I don't know." He was honest, as always. "She'll look at you a good while before she says. She did me, when I come back from a month on the range once. She was four. She sat on the step and looked for an hour, and then she said, 'You've got a different hat.'"

Imogen smiled. She looked down into the mug.

"Tom. In the Rill. When we went past the Perch."

"Ay?"

"Did you feel anything? As if—" She did not know how to say it. "As if somebody had been there."

He was silent for a time.

"I've felt that all my life," he said. "Every Garrow has. Dad would never pass it without he touched the post. He said it was for them that had sat in it." He looked at the window, and the lamp. "It's a queer old thing. Your brother's let it go. Them bottom rungs wouldn't bear a child."

She thought of her brother. She thought of a small white light at the edge of the sea.

"I'll go across on Sunday night," she said. "By the road. I've a ring to give my father. And I'll find Edmund. I said some things to him that I'm going to unsay. And then I shall make him tell me the rest."

"I'll come with you. I've a question of my own."

"Without the gun."

"With my hat in my hand," Tom Garrow said.

* * *

She did go across on the Sunday. She walked over at the evening ebb, in a borrowed skirt, with the ring in her glove.

The house was upside down. Celia had gone. Edmund had not been seen since the night. Kit met her on the gravel, grey in the face. They had found the Jack in the outfall that morning, he said, in Grandfather's old dinner jacket, and for a minute they had thought—

She went up to the Admiral's room. Her father was in bed, propped on pillows. The nurse got up from her low chair and went out without a word.

"Imogen," he said. He knew her at once. "They said that you had gone over to the farm."

"I have, Papa. I'm going to stay there. I'm going to marry Tom Garrow."

"Are you? That is very suitable. I gave them the ground last night; you'll be on your own freehold." He nodded, pleased. "I ought to have done it thirty years ago. One puts things off."

She took off her glove. She opened her hand.

He looked at what lay in it. He fitted the glass into his eye. He took the ring from her palm, between finger and thumb, as he would have taken up a pivot, and turned it to the lamp.

"That is Margot's," he said. "I had it made by a man in Hatton Garden. Twenty-two carat; it wears, you see, it has worn to a wire. Where was this?"

"Edmund had it. She gave it to him. That night, on the Wade. She told him to give it to you." Her voice shook. "He's carried it ever since. He asked me to bring it."

Her father was quiet. He closed his fingers on the ring.

"Yes," he said. "That would be so. He told me. In the tower, last night. She turned back, you know. She had got nearly across, and she turned round on the Wade and came back. He was there. He saw. She was coming home." He looked up at her, mild and certain. "I thought for twenty years that she drowned going away from me. She was on her way back to this house."

Imogen stood by the bed. She looked at his face, so peaceful.

She thought: he has made it into the story that he needs. Edmund told him something, up there, and he has turned it round until it will bear his weight. She *turned back*. Amos Garrow had said it, dying. *I never knew if she turned of herself or was turned.* And here was her father, sure that she had turned of herself, and toward him.

She did not correct him. She had learned something in that hall, about truth, and when to give it. She thought that she understood exactly what her father had done with what he was told.

So she bent, and kissed him, and said, "Yes, Papa. She was coming home."

It was the third time that night and day that she had been offered it. Tom had tried to tell her one thing in the orangery, and she had stopped his mouth. Edmund had tried in the scullery, and she would not hear. And now her father had given it to her in plain words, from his pillow. She smiled at him, and thought him confused.

Her father slipped the ring onto the smallest finger of his left hand. It went on easily; he had grown so thin. He was buried in it.

They found her brother on the Monday.

* * *

She has written this as a love story. It is one. She was married to Tom Garrow in April, in St Botolph's, a month after her father died. She wore a blue coat. The ring was a horseshoe nail, bent round, with the head beaten flat. She had sent to Edinburgh for it. She has kept it oiled.

Dolly Pask cried all through. Octavia Prowse gave her away, since there was no one else left to, and said in the porch that it was about time one of them got something out of that family. There was a small dark child in a new frock and gumboots, who held the bride's flowers upside down and watched everything.

Afterward they walked down to the yard. The old mare was looking over her door. Tom had plaited a ribbon into her mane.

She has been happy. She is not going to pretend, for the sake of anyone's feelings, that she has not. She has lived for a long time in a low damp house with a lamp in the window, among sheep, and has learned to smell the tide. She has had a daughter given to her whom she did not bear and could not love more. She has had a man who cannot say things, and who shows her.

That was what she got, on the night of the twenty-second of October. She went into her father's house with twenty years of silence in her, and came out on a grey horse, across the sea, by moonlight. She was told the time by the one man and carried by the other.

She had her Hour. It was given back to her, as her father always said it was, and she was let do the thing over. This time she crossed.

And on the same night her brother, whom she had told that she hoped he would never sleep again, went down to that water by himself.

She does not know why. She has an idea. It is the idea that anyone would have, who had said what she said. He had put his mother's ring into her hand like a man clearing his pockets. He had said, *I've an idea I shan't be about in the morning.*

She saw a light at the edge of the tide. She waved to it.

She has never told her husband what she said in the scullery. He has never spoken again of the two spent cases on the orangery floor, and she has never asked. There is a paper in a drawer, from a coroner, that mentions shot. They have been married a long time and are very close. There are two rooms in the house that neither of them goes into.

The publishers of this book have asked for a happy ending. They are quite right. It is what the form requires. And she has one to give them, with a good conscience.

Here it is. On an evening in May, in the year after, she was standing on the sea wall above the farm. The tide was making. It came in over the flats in long bright sheets, without a sound, the way it does. The larks were going up.

Tom came and stood beside her. He had the child by the hand.

"Watch," he said to the child. "It'll stop."

"It won't. It's the sea."

"It will. In a minute. It stops at the top and thinks."

They watched. The water came up the stones of the wall. It came to within a foot of their boots. It slowed, and spread, and lay flat from the wall to the island, with the sky in it.

And it stopped. The whole of it. As if someone had laid a hand on it.

"There," Tom said. "That's the slack. That's the tide thinking better of it. You've a few minutes, then, when nothing's coming and nothing's going. You can get a boat anywhere. You can do near enough what you like."

The child looked at the still water for a long time, with her head on one side.

"What does it think about?" she said.

Nobody answered her. Across the bright flood the house stood on its island with every window shut. It was very quiet there now. Somebody had stopped the clocks.

Imogen put her arm through her husband's. She did not look at a watch. She had never worn one.

Then, slowly, with a small sound like breath, the sea turned and began to go out.

## 6. The Tower

The rain had stopped. The wind was dropping. There was a moon coming and going in the cloud, and by it he could see the top of the sea wall running north like a pale road.

He went along it fast. He had the gun in the crook of his arm with the muzzles down. On his left hand the tide lay right up to the stonework. It was full. He had never seen it so high. There was not a foot between the water and his boots, and it had stopped moving. It lay as still as milk in a pan.

That was the slack. He had shown it to a girl once from the other wall. *It's the tide thinking better of it.*

He had ten minutes, if Ostler had told the truth. Perhaps less.

The wall bent. He came round the bend and he saw the light.

It was a white light, low, a lantern on a mast. There was the staging, running out on its piles, and at the end of it a launch lying. He could hear her engine. He stopped where the spinney came down to the wall, and got in among the thorns, and looked.

There were two men.

One was in the boat. He was standing in the stern with a line in his hand, in a long oilskin. That was Ostler; he knew the set of him. The other was on the staging. He stood with his feet apart and his back to the wall, bareheaded, in a raincoat, and the water was over his shoes.

The man in the thorns looked for a box. He looked for a hitch in the walk.

There was neither. The one on the staging was short and thick. He stood like a post. And the lantern was on his face, and it was not the face of Edmund Vane. It was the stranger who had read the tide board that morning, the one with the policeman's shoes.

He did not understand it. He crouched with the gun across his knees and tried to make it come out.

Vane was to be here with a box. Instead there was the law, standing on the Hythe at the top of the tide, talking to the boatman. He could hear their voices. He could not hear words. The policeman said something short. Ostler answered him long.

So they had caught him. Or they were waiting to. Or the box had gone already and Vane with it, and this was what was left.

He had come too late. He had come through the Rill and stood in a glasshouse, and the law had got there first.

He ought to have been glad. A man who is taken by the police can be found again. He can be asked a question through a grille.

He was not glad. He found that he was angrier than he had been all night. It was not the policeman's to do. That one had come in a town car that morning and read a board. He did not know what was owed. He had not knelt on a brick floor with a dying hand on his wrist.

He stayed where he was. He did not know what to do.

It was the boatman who saw it first. He put his head up, and looked past the man on the staging, back along the island. He said something and pointed.

The man in the thorns turned his head.

* * *

Behind him, over the dark of the spinney, the stable tower stood up against the sky. He could see the clock, a pale round, and above it the little pitched roof.

There was a light up there.

It was a lantern. Someone had it in his hand. It was swinging, slowly, from side to side, in a wide arc, like a man waving a train out of a station. It went three times across. It stopped. It went again.

He knew the tower. Every child in the village knew it. There were two wooden people up there who struck the bell. They called them Jack and Jenny. As a boy he had been lifted up on the wall at Saltcote to watch them through his father's glass.

But there was a man up there now. Wooden people do not swing lamps.

He looked at the Hythe. The policeman had turned round. He was staring up at the tower with his mouth open. Ostler had the line half off the post.

And he understood.

It was a signal. It was to the boat. Vane was not at the Hythe; he was up there, forty feet over the yard, with a lamp, telling his boatman something. *Wait. I'm coming.* Or, *Go; it's off.* He had seen the law on the staging and gone up where he could be seen from the water.

He was not taken. He was not gone. He was two hundred yards away, on top of his own stable, by himself.

The man got up out of the thorns. He went back through the spinney, running.

There was a track. He found it with his feet. It went by a mound with an iron gate standing open in the side of it, and a ring of keys hanging in the lock; he saw them shine and did not stop. It went up between the trees and a wall. The moon was out. He came round the end of the stable block onto cobbles.

He was in the yard.

It was a square of wet stone with buildings on three sides, and a mounting block, and at the far end the arch. The tower went up from beside the arch. He had never stood beneath it. He had not known that it was so high.

The house was behind him across the gravel. He did not look at it. He heard something there, a great door opening, and voices. He did not turn his head.

He was looking up.

The lantern was right over him. It hung out from under the little roof. It lit the bell, a green shape, and the beams. It lit a face.

It was a big face, white, with dark hair wet on the forehead. One side of it was wrong. There was a swelling over the eye, black, as large as a fist, and blood had run down from it and dried. The good eye was open and staring out toward the sea. The mouth hung.

He had not seen that face close in twenty years. He had seen it through a glass, on the Thursday, from a churchyard. He knew it.

Vane, he said.

It did not carry. He filled his chest.

VANE.

* * *

The face turned. The lantern came round with it and the light fell down the front of the tower into the yard, and onto him.

He stood in it. He had the gun in his two hands, across his body. He was hatless and caked with clay to the eyes, and the sea was still running out of his coat.

Who's that?

It was a thick voice. It slurred, like a man talking in his sleep.

Garrow.

Who?

Tom Garrow. From Saltcote. You looked at me Thursday through a glass.

There was a silence. The lantern swayed. Up in the works of the clock something had begun to whirr.

Garrow, the voice said. Good God. It laughed. It was a short broken sound. How did you get over? You can't have got over. There's six feet on the road.

I swum it.

You swam the Rill? At the flood? Then you're a bigger fool than I am. The lantern dipped. He's swum the Rill, Ma. Did you hear that? Old Amos's boy. He's come for his rent.

The man in the yard did not know who was being spoken to. There was nobody up there with him.

I've a thing to ask you, he said.

Not now.

It's from my father. He's dead. He held on a week to give it to me.

Not *now*, man. Can't you see I'm busy? I've a boat. I've to tell the boat— The voice lost its way. It came back loud and angry and frightened. You've no business here. This is the Ness. Get off my land.

It's your father's land.

Get off it! You've no land. You've none. Fenn's sent the paper. Go home to your bloody sheep.

He laughed again. It went on. It rang down the tower into the yard.

It was the laugh she had told him of. *It's what he does when he's frightened.* He knew that. He had been told an hour ago, by a woman holding his sleeve. He had the knowledge in his head, and it did him no good. He stood in that yard at the foot of forty feet of brick. He had carried a ewe through the tide for that family's rent. His father was four days in the ground. And he was being laughed at from a height by the man who had put them off.

He felt his hands close on the gun.

Was she coming or was she going, he shouted.

The laughing stopped.

My father stood on our wall with a lamp. He see her light come three parts over. He see yours go after. He stood an hour. And hers turned back. He never knew which it was. He's laid awake on it twenty year. Did she turn of herself? Or did you turn her?

There was no answer. The lantern had gone still. The face above it looked down at him, with its one eye, and it had changed. It was not laughing. It was open, like a wound.

You tell me, the man said. You'll tell me now. I've come through the sea for it.

The whirring in the tower had grown. There was a clank.

And the small bell began to ring.

It rang over his head, sharp, two notes together, and again. He had heard it across the water all his boyhood, thin, like a bird. He had never stood under it. It was as loud as an anvil.

Something moved beside Vane.

It was on his left, on the near side of the bell. It had been in the dark behind the lantern. Now it came forward to the edge of the roof. It was a man. It was a man in a black coat with a white front. He stood straight up at Vane's shoulder, close, and the light caught him.

He lifted his arm.

He lifted it from the shoulder, stiff, with a thing in his fist. He swung it up and over, and brought it down, straight out, toward the yard.

The man below saw it come. It had a head on it, dark, and a long handle. It came down and pointed at his chest.

He had thought that Vane was alone.

There were two of them. There had been a second man up there the whole while, in the dark, in evening clothes, saying nothing. He had stood and let Vane talk. And now he had stepped out with a thing in his hand and levelled it.

The arm went up again. It came down.

The big bell struck.

It struck once. The whole yard shook with it. It went through the man's feet and up into his teeth. In the same moment the lantern swung, and the two shapes up there ran together, the white face and the black coat, so that he could not tell one from the other. There was only something tall, leaning out over him, with its arm stretched down, and metal at the end of the arm.

He did not think. He has said so, and he says it without excuse. He had been twenty years in a country where a man who sees a thing levelled at him from above does not stop to ask what it is.

The gun came up. He did not feel it come. It was at his shoulder, and his cheek was on the wood where his father's had been.

Vane stood up on the tower with something levelled and the man shot him off it. Both barrels. He watched him go down into the fleet.

* * *

He will tell exactly what he saw. He has gone over it enough.

The first barrel took the black coat full in the chest. He saw the white front go. The figure jerked. It did not double; it went rigid, all of a piece, and rocked back.

The second he fired before the first had done kicking. It went a little left, and high.

The lantern flew up. It turned over in the air, and fell, straight down the face of the tower, and struck the cobbles at his feet and went out.

By the last of it he saw the man fall.

He went backward. He went slowly, with his arms out from his sides, like a tree that has been cut through and takes its time. There was a moment when he lay on the air, stiff, with his white front to the sky. Then he had gone over the far side, where the tower stood on the creek.

There was a sound from down there. It was a flat hard slap, like a plank dropped on water.

And then nothing. There was the smoke, hanging. There was the ringing in his ears. Above him, under the little roof, in the moonlight, something tall and grey stood on the other side of the bell with its arm raised.

That was the wooden woman. He looked at her. She was looking over his head, at the house.

There was nobody else up there at all.

He broke the gun. The two cases jumped out and rolled on the stones. He bent and picked them up and put them in his pocket. He does not know why. A man who has shot rabbits all his life picks up his empties.

He went under the arch.

He had to feel his way. It came out at the back of the stables on a strip of grass, and below the grass was the fleet. He knew it. It was a ditch at low water. It was full now, twenty feet across and black, and it had begun to move. The slack was over. The whole length of it was sliding north, smooth, toward the wall and the sea.

He stood on the bank. He looked down at the foot of the tower.

There was nothing there. He looked along the water. The moon came out. Forty yards down, going away, in the middle of the stream, there was something long and dark. It lay low. It turned as it went. He saw a white cuff.

He started along the bank after it. He ran ten yards. There was a brick sluice where the fleet went out under the sea wall, and he saw the thing reach the arch, and check, and go under.

He stopped.

He could hear the water on the other side, falling into the saltings. It was the ebb. It would take whatever went through that arch out over the flats and down the river, to the sea.

He stood with the gun open over his arm.

He had killed Edmund Vane.

He put it to himself in those words, on the bank. He made himself say them. He had come over to ask a question and carry a gun for show. He had loaded it, in a glasshouse, with a woman's voice still in his ears telling him not to. He had stood in a yard and been laughed at. And when something moved up there that he did not understand, he had let go both barrels at a man.

He had not got his answer. He would never get it now. The one man living who knew what had happened on the Wade was gone down a creek on his face. His father had held on six days for nothing.

And there was the other one. The one in the black coat, who had stepped out with a thing in his hand. Where was he? He had seen only one fall. The tower was empty. He stood and tried to think, and could not make it come. There had been two shapes, and a lantern, and they had run together. He had fired at what was pointed at him. One man had gone over.

He does not know to this day how many were on that tower. He has been told a number of things. He knows what he shot at. He knows whose face was in the lantern. He knows what went through the sluice.

He never asked the question. That is what he says, when he comes to this. He says, I'd come through the sea to ask a man one thing, and I shot him before he could tell me.

* * *

He went back into the yard. He stood in the middle of it, on the cobbles, in the moonlight, by the mounting block.

He was waiting to be taken.

He had fired a twelve-bore twice within eighty yards of a house full of people. The front door of that house was open. He could see straight into the hall from where he stood. It was all lit up with candles, like a church. There were figures moving. A woman had cried out; he had heard it between the shots. There was a policeman on the island.

They would come. They would come across that gravel with lamps. He would hand over the gun, stock first, and say what he had done.

He thought of the child. She would wake in the morning under Vi Pask's thatch, and somebody would have to tell her. He thought of the woman in the grey dress. She was in that hall now. She had asked him twice.

He stood. He kept his eyes on the door.

Nobody came.

He could not understand it. He counted. He counted to five hundred. He saw a tall old man standing in the doorway, with white hair, quite still, looking toward the tower. A small woman stood beside him. Then the old man turned, and went in. The doorway was empty.

He heard a voice in there. It was a young man's, loud, and cheerful. He caught one word. He thought it was *ice*.

Somebody laughed.

He stood in the yard for a quarter of an hour by his watch. In that time nobody put a foot outside the house. Nobody called. There was only the light, and the voices going on, mild and easy, like people at their supper.

And then there was music.

It came thin across the gravel. It was a fiddle tune, slow, and sweet as treacle, off a gramophone. He knew the kind. They had played such at the harvest suppers when he was small, for the old people.

It was a waltz.

He stood with his dead man's blood on his hands, as he thought it, and listened to the Vanes dance.

He has said that it was the strangest quarter hour of his life. He was more afraid in it than he had been in the Rill. In the water he had only been going to drown. Here he had done murder, in the open, with noise. And the house had looked out, and seen, and gone back to its party.

He thought, they know. That was the old man in the door. He saw his son fall. And he turned and went in and called for a tune.

He thought of twenty years of rent, paid to those people, in notes, with a cap in the hand. He had thought them hard. He had not thought them that.

He did not understand then about the house. He was never in it. He did not know that it kept its own hour and its own rules. He knows now that there were eleven people in that hall and that only two of them had been looking out of the door. One was a nurse who thought she had seen a ghost, and the other was an old man who thought he had been paid a debt. He has been told so since, by one who has read what they wrote.

It makes no odds. He stood in the yard, and nobody came. A man who is ready to be taken and is not finds that he has nowhere to put himself.

He looked up, once more, at the tower.

She was there. The wooden woman. She had her arm lifted. The moon was on her painted face. She was looking down the length of the yard, over his head, through the open door, at the hall where they were dancing.

He thought that she had seen it all. He thought, she's the only one that has.

He closed the gun. It was empty. He put it under his arm and walked out of the yard, along under the wall of the house, on the grass, to the glasshouse. The mare lifted her head. He sat down on the upturned pot. He laid the gun across his knees and took the two spent cases out of his pocket, and stood them on the bricks by the lantern, side by side, where he could look at them.

He looked at his watch. It was twenty-five past two.

She had said two by her father's clocks. That was three by his. He had thirty-five minutes to think how he would tell her that he had shot her brother.

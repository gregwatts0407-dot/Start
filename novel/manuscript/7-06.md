## 6. Thirteen at Table

The clocks went back this year on the night of Saturday the twenty-sixth of October. High water at Compline Bar was at twenty-one minutes past eleven by the sun, and the Wade closed at a quarter to nine and opened again at ten past two. I had printed the times on the letters. I had printed them twice, once in Greenwich time and once in the time on people's telephones, and put a line between, and written underneath, in red: *please read to the bottom.*

They all came across before it shut. Toby Pargeter came first, at four in the afternoon, in a car that was not red, with his third wife, Philippa, who stayed in the car for the whole of the crossing with her eyes shut. Kit and Saskia came from Berlin by way of Stansted. Saskia is fifty-two and makes very large things out of steel and still has, in her left eyebrow, the hole where the ring was. Gail Thurlow drove over from Tiptree with a cake that she said her auntie used to make, and stood in the kitchen for ten minutes with her hand on the range without saying anything.

Hester Quaile came last but one, on foot, as she had come thirty years before, from the bus. I watched her from the gallery window. She stopped halfway, at the Perch, and stood a while. Then she put her hand on the post and walked on.

Celia Vane came last. She was seventy-eight. She came in a hired car from the airport, with a driver, and she got out on the gravel and looked up at the tower before she looked at the house. She had a stick, and she was very thin, and her hair was white. She stood in the hall in a camel coat and said to me, "You're the child."

"Yes."

"You're the one he said was under the bed."

"Yes."

She looked at me for a long time. She has grey-green eyes that look at something a little way behind you.

"Harry used to say," she said, "that in his trade, when the family hands you somebody in the first hour, you thank them and go on looking." She held out her hand, palm up. "I suppose you want my watch."

* * *

I did want it. I had put the Font on the hall table.

I had not opened the house for thirty years. Nobody had. Kit inherited it from his grandfather and never came; he let Imogen have it, and she kept it locked and aired and went over once a month with the key and a duster. The clocks were as Hester had left them on the morning of the twenty-sixth of March, 1995. Every pendulum still. Every weight down. A hundred and ten of them.

I did not start them. I want to say that. People have asked me why, since I could; it is my trade. I left them as she had stopped them, as he had asked. I started three clocks only, and the tower.

The tower I had seen to in the summer. I had found two men from Colchester who restore church clocks, and we went up together in August with a rope. The Jack was in the Shop on his trestles, where Leo had left him, mended. You can hardly find the join. It took the three of us a whole morning to get him up the ladder and out on to the leads and settled on his pin. When he was in place one of the men said, "There. She'll have someone to talk to." I had not told him anything.

So at five minutes to five on the twenty-sixth of October I stood in the yard with my guests and heard Compline tell the quarter for the first time since the night of the twenty-second of October, 1994. The little man lifted his arm. *Ting-tang.* Three times. Then he stopped, and the grey woman stood with her arm up, and waited for the hour.

Toby said, "Oh, Lord," and blew his nose.

The three clocks I had set on the long table in the dining room. I had laid it for thirteen.

* * *

I shall tell you how the table was laid, because they each noticed it when they came in, and each of them stopped in the doorway.

There were seven of us. There were thirteen places. I had put the six extra places in the order in which the accounts are given in this book, round the table, and at each of them, on the plate, I had set one of the manuscripts. Mr Cade's typescript in its Basildon Bond box. Toby's book, open at chapter eleven. Hester's three red exercise books. A paperback with a rose on the spine, and under it the typescript with the sixth chapter in. A sheaf of my own handwriting, which was my father's. Six more exercise books, in Hester's hand, with an old man's numbered sections.

At the head of the table, on the old man's right, I had laid the thirteenth place as it was always laid. Three glasses. A napkin like a bishop's hat. The chair drawn a little back. On the plate there was nothing. I could not think what to put there. In the end I left a small red diary beside the fork.

Hester sat down in front of her own exercise books, and then got up, and moved, and sat in front of Leo's.

"I took them down," she said. "He'd want someone there who could read his hand."

Celia stood behind the chair with Mr Cade's box on it for some time. Then she sat in it.

"Somebody's got to sit with Harry," she said. "He'd hate it, being on his own at a party. He'd go and stand against the wall."

So we were thirteen, with the dead. We had soup. Gail served it. She put a plate at every place. Nobody said anything about it.

* * *

After the soup I got up, and took down the curtains from the long wall, and showed them the lining paper.

It is nine feet long and three feet high. It is ruled in pencil in a grid. Down the left side are the names of every person who was on Compline Ness that night, sixteen of them. Across the top are the hours, from nine in the morning of the twenty-second to six in the morning of the twenty-third, in ten-minute columns. In each square is every statement that anyone has made about where that person was and what they were doing, with a reference to the page.

There are four colours of ink. Black is the time on Mr Cade's watch, and my father's, and mine. That is the time of the rest of England, an hour ahead of Greenwich. Red is the time of the house. Green is the time of the kitchen clock, which Mrs Pask kept eight minutes fast, every Monday, and never told anyone. And blue is what I have not been able to time at all: things people did not know the time of.

On the table I had set the three clocks. One was a little radio-controlled clock of the kind you buy for twenty pounds, which takes its time from the transmitter in Cumbria that my laboratory feeds. It said seven minutes past nine. One was the kitchen clock off the wall downstairs, which I had started, and set, and put eight minutes fast. It said fifteen minutes past eight. And the third was the plain dark regulator from the hall, the Vulliamy, which I had carried through, with its weight.

I had cleaned the weight in the summer, very carefully, with a soft cloth. It did not need cleaning. Someone had done it before me.

It said seven minutes past eight.

"Those are the three clocks," I said. "Everyone in this book was reading one of them. Nobody knew there were three. Not even Mr Cade, who had two."

"Two?" said Celia.

"His watch, and the kitchen. He looked at the kitchen clock at about twenty to two, on his way through, and read it as true. He had no reason not to. It said twenty-two minutes to two. It was a quarter to two by his watch, and a quarter to one by the house." I touched the green one. "I think that clock may have made more trouble than the Act of Parliament."

"She did it for the dinner," Gail said. "So it'd come up on time. She told me when I was twenty. She said she'd done it since 1952 and nobody had ever noticed."

"Nobody did."

* * *

I am going to give the summation in the order in which I gave it, which is the order of the book. I had promised Imogen that I would tell them exactly, and I had promised myself that I would not be kind, because kindness was what had kept every one of them carrying the wrong thing for thirty years. But I found, once I had begun, that exact and kind were not so far apart as I had feared. Exact was what they wanted. They had each been waiting for somebody to say a thing that could be checked.

I began with Mr Cade, though he was not there, because Celia was sitting at his place.

"*By my watch it was a quarter past one*," I read, "*and Edmund Vane was lying at the bottom of the ice-house steps with his eyes open and the left side of his head the wrong shape. I have seen what dead looks like. It looks like that.*"

I put my finger on the lining paper, on the black square at one-fifteen.

"That's true," I said. "Every word of it. He was there. He was lying like that. His father had struck him on the left temple at ten to one by this watch," I touched the radio clock, "which was ten to twelve by that one," the regulator, "and he went down twelve steps and landed with his feet up. He was under a heavy dose of chloral and his breathing had gone to about eight times a minute. Toby counted it later. If you look at a man by a cigarette lighter, through a gate, from twelve feet, for one minute, you will see his chest move eight times if you know where to look. Mr Cade didn't. He saw the eyes. He was right about the eyes. They don't move."

Celia said nothing. She had her hands folded on the Basildon Bond box.

"*At a quarter to two the gate was open and the steps were empty.* That's true too. At seventeen minutes past one, by Mr Cade's watch, Mrs Pask had sent Toby out with her keys. She sent him because I told her. Toby looked at the kitchen clock as he went. He has told you it said twenty-five past twelve. It did. It was eight minutes fast." I touched the green clock. "So it was seventeen past twelve by the house, and seventeen past one by Mr Cade, and Mr Cade had just walked away from that gate two minutes before. Toby walked in at one twenty and found him snoring. They were three minutes apart in that wood and neither of them saw the other, because one of them was walking toward the house to find a widow and the other was walking away from it to find a pudding."

Toby said, "Oh, my *God*."

"And then Mr Cade wrote this." I read it. "*At two o'clock somebody fired a twelve-bore twice. At a quarter past, a man in a dinner jacket went down the fleet on the ebb, face down, in no hurry.* That's true as well. It was the Jack. He was wearing your grandfather's dinner jacket, Kit, that you and Saskia put on him that afternoon. My father shot him off his pin with both barrels from the yard. He went into the fleet on his face, and he rode high, because he was oak, and he went out under the sluice and fetched up in the grating at the outfall, where you dug him out on the Sunday."

Kit said, "I thought it was Dad. For a minute. In the mud."

"So did Mr Cade. For thirty years." I put my hand on the box. "He'd seen one man in a black jacket lying still, and then another going out to sea, an hour apart. He joined them up. He said himself that a detective is a man who can't leave two things lying next to each other. He'd been struck on the head at ten past twelve and he says in his own words that a knock on the head doesn't make you lose what you know, it makes you lose the string between things. He had everything. The candle in a kitchen saucer. The spoon. The one wheel in the mud. He stood in that kitchen at twenty to two with his hand on an empty pudding mould and felt the answer go past him out of the door."

"He knew that," Celia said. "He wrote that he knew it."

"He knew it was there. He never knew what it was."

I read the last one.

"*'I killed him,' she said. I believed her. I still do.*"

I looked at Celia.

"He believed you because you believed it," I said. "You'd put half a bottle of chloral hydrate in your husband's brandy at ten to midnight by this," the black, "and handed it to him and smiled. And at five past one you'd gone out to the ice house with a candle in a jam jar and seen his hand on the straw through the bars, and it hadn't moved. And you came back and sat on a hearthrug and told the first person who came in at the window."

"Yes," said Celia Vane.

"It didn't kill him. You need to know that. It's on the paper." I touched the square. "He drank about half of it. Toby made sure of that by accident, and the vicar drank the rest, and slept on a settle in this hall till morning and woke up with a headache and took a service at Thorney on the Monday. Your husband walked out of the drawing room at half past eleven on his own legs. He crated a clock. He talked to his father in the ice house. He climbed forty feet of tower at two in the morning with my hands on his ankles. He ate half a goose at quarter past three. He talked to his sister. He swam the Rill at four, in the dark, at the flood. He was alive in the sea at a quarter to five. Whatever your chloral did to him, it didn't stop him doing any of that."

She was quite still.

"What did it do?" she said. "Tell me what it did. Exactly. You said you'd be exact."

So I told her.

"It slowed his breathing, so that a detective looking through a gate thought he was dead, and went to find you, and didn't fetch a doctor. And it made him slow and sleepy in the ice house when his father came, so that he laughed at him, which he might not otherwise have done. And it put him down on that floor so deep that if a child hadn't told the cook he'd have died of the cold by three. And it made him so sick and dizzy at the orchard gate at ten to four that he had to sit down with his head between his knees, and while he was sitting there a child walked past him into the sea."

I watched it land. She had been told her husband was dead by a man who had wanted it to be news. I had been afraid that she would look like that. She didn't.

She closed her eyes and breathed in through her nose and let it out, very slowly.

"So I made him sit down," she said.

"Yes."

"For the length of time it took you to get past him."

"Yes."

"And then he got up."

"He got up. He came in after me. With the stuff in him. You couldn't keep him down for five minutes when it mattered. Nobody could."

Celia opened her eyes. They were wet. She had her thumb on her wedding ring, turning it. It was the same ring.

"I only wanted him to stay," she said. "I told Immy that. I wanted him to sleep through the tide so that he couldn't go." She laughed, very low. "He stayed. He never went anywhere. He was on that island the whole night. And in the morning I was on a boat to Holland on his ticket."

"Did you know it was a copy?" Kit said. "The clock. When you sold it."

"No." She did not look at her son. "No, darling. I didn't know anything. I never could tell one from another. I hated every one of them."

* * *

I am not going to give every square. You have the six accounts. You can do it yourselves with a pencil. I will give the ones that they asked about, and the ones that cost.

Toby asked about the mounting block.

He asked it standing up, holding the back of his chair. He had turned down the page of his own book to it, and he put it on the table in front of me.

"*A second injury,*" he said, "*a large contusion over the occiput, separate from the first and sustained somewhat later.* That was me. I've told nobody but my wife. It was me and the barrow. I had the handle."

"It was you," I said.

He nodded. He had known. He had wanted to hear somebody say it.

"I was ten yards away," I said. "I was at the corner of the wall. I saw the barrow go over. I saw you catch the box. I heard his head hit the stone. And then I heard him say *Ow*, and ask who it was, and what you were holding, and say *Well done*. And then he turned himself over on to his side and went to sleep."

"I know. But the doctors said—"

"The doctors said a man can walk about for hours and feel fine. They're right. They can. I asked them too. I gave them the pathologist's report." I picked it up off the table. "*Contusion.* A contusion's a bruise, Toby. It bled because heads bleed. It didn't fracture. The fracture's on the other side, at the front, and his father did that, forty-five minutes before you got there. Both doctors say the same. If he'd gone dizzy from a head injury that night, and he did, it was the one on the left temple. Not yours."

He sat down.

"And there's another thing in the report," I said. "Under *cause of death*. It's not a head injury. It's drowning. He went into the sea of his own will, after a child, and held on to a post for fifty minutes, and talked the whole time, and made jokes, and was perfectly clear in his head. I was there. He was clearer than you are now."

"You were there," Toby said.

"I was on top of the post."

He looked at me. His wife put her hand on his arm.

"And you were there before," I said. "In the ice house. You turned him on to his side. Do you know what the doctor said about that? About a man with that much chloral in him, lying on his back with his head lower than his feet? He said that if he was sick, and he might well have been, he'd have choked. You turned him over. You did it because Hester had told you to do it for the vicar, an hour before, and you remembered. You'd been told the left side, so you did the left side. It was the injured side. It didn't matter. You got him off his back."

"I thought I was being an ass."

"You carried him up twelve steps. On your seat."

"Mrs Pask carried him. I just sort of went first."

"You went first," I said. "Up twelve steps, backwards, with sixteen stone in your lap. Toby. You called your book *Mostly My Fault*. I've been through every square on that wall. You are the only person in this whole history who came on Edmund Vane lying hurt and bent down to see if he was breathing. You and me. Everybody else made up their mind from the door."

He put his face in his hands.

"I had the sock," he said, through them. "In the report. *One sock.* I've thought about the other sock for thirty years."

"It came off in the Rill," I said. "With his shoes. When he went in after me. I'm sorry. It isn't a clue. It's just a sock."

* * *

Hester asked about the voice.

She did not ask until after the goose. She had not eaten much of it. She was sitting at the old man's place, with his exercise books in front of her, and she had been very quiet.

"*I believe that I heard Edmund Vane's voice in the clock chamber forty minutes after I saw him fall,*" she said. She had it by heart. "*When I called his name, he did not answer me.*"

"He didn't answer you because of me," I said. "I was in the weight closet. He'd put me there when he heard his father on the ladder. He put his finger to his lips when you called. He told me why on the way down. He said that if you came up you'd have him in bed with a thermometer, and you'd find me, and there'd be questions, and Mrs Pask would lose her place, and my father would be brought up to the house with every Vane in it looking at him. He said he had one night left to do some things."

"*One night left.*"

"I don't think he meant it like that. I think he meant before morning."

She looked at her hands.

"I didn't climb up," she said. "I was four rungs from the top. I had the lamp. I didn't climb up because I was afraid that I'd see the old man talking to nobody."

"I know."

"If I'd climbed up—"

"You'd have found him with his neck bleeding and a child in a cupboard. You'd have taken him in, and put a dressing on him. You'd have stayed with him. You'd have been sitting on his bed at ten to four, and he'd never have gone down to the gate." I made myself go on. "Hester, I've done that sum. I've done all the sums. Everybody at this table has done the one about themselves. Every one of them comes out to the same thing. If any one of us had done one thing differently he'd be alive, perhaps. And every one of them also comes out the other way. If Celia hadn't dosed him he wouldn't have been in the ice house in the state he was, and if Leo hadn't hit him he'd have been at the Hythe at a quarter to two with the box, and got on Ostler's boat. And if Toby and Mrs Pask hadn't carried him out he'd have died at three. And if you'd climbed up you'd have saved him from the Wade and he'd have been in Holland by Monday."

"Then what's the use of the sum?"

"None. That's what I wanted to tell you. It doesn't come out. It goes round. It's like his clocks. Every one of them is right and none of them agrees."

She was quiet. Then she said, "And the weight."

The Vulliamy was on the table in front of her. Its pendulum was going. The weight hung at the bottom of its line.

"You cleaned it," I said.

"I cleaned it with my handkerchief, and spit, and burned the handkerchief. So that no one would ever know how he came by his death."

"He didn't come by his death that way. He came by a fracture. He lived through it. He climbed a tower with it." I reached out and touched the brass. "Hester, nobody ever asked whose blood was on that weight. The coroner had the fracture and he had the open verdict. If you'd left it, Inspector Brand would have found it and taken Leo away to a place with locked doors, as you said, and asked him every day. And he'd have been asking about a man who wasn't killed by it."

"Then I did a wrong thing for nothing."

"You did a kind thing for an old man who'd have been hurt by the truth. He'd asked you never to lie to him. You told him three lies that night and he knew about all three. He says in his last section that upon consideration he thinks you were right in all three." I put my finger on the place in the exercise book, in her own handwriting, where she had taken it down. "You wrote that. You wrote it down from his mouth. You've had it for thirty years."

She looked at the page. I don't think she had read it since the day she wrote it.

"And the gallery," she said, at last. "You'll tell me that it was Imogen."

"It was Imogen. She has written down every hook she did up."

"I know. I said so. I said I know whose dress lay on the bed." Hester lifted her eyes. "And?"

I had thought about this one more than any other. I had not been able to find anything on the wall that disagreed with her.

"I can't give you a square for it," I said. "I wasn't there. At one o'clock by my watch, which was midnight by yours, I was in the wall, in the dumb waiter, going down, with you standing in the nursery with my drawing in your hand. I heard the clocks strike through the shaft. I didn't see anyone."

"Then you can't tell me."

"I can tell you what the other two wrote, who were there. One of them was wearing the dress." I had Imogen's typescript. I found the place. "She stopped under the portrait on her way down. She says, *for the first time in twenty years Imogen looked into those eyes and did not see a question there.* And then she says she went down the gallery and didn't look to right or left, and that she has been told since that someone was standing on the landing, and she is sorry. She says that she would not, that night, have seen her own father."

Hester listened with her eyes shut.

"And the other one danced with her." I opened Leo's sixth book, in her own hand. "*I do not think that the engine made my wife. I think that it had only to bring her as far as the nearest thing that would hold her.*"

"I took that down," Hester said. "I remember his voice."

"Everything you wrote about that gallery is on my wall," I said. "In black and red. The candle, and the shoes in her hand, and the dress on the bed, and the white hair in the brush. All of it checks. The only thing I can't put in a square is what you saw in her face. Nobody could. Imogen couldn't; she was inside it. Leo was dancing with his eyes shut." I closed the book. "You wrote *I do not say that there are ghosts.* I don't either. I keep the time for a living. But you were the only person who saw her face, Hester, and you were the only one of us who'd spent twenty nights learning that portrait. I'm not going to tell you that you didn't see what you saw."

She put her hand flat on the old man's exercise book. She held it there.

"Thank you," she said.

* * *

Kit asked about the stable.

I told him about the mirror test, and the blanket. I told him what he has already read, that he had not killed anybody. He knew that by then; he had known it since the morning, when Gail told him the man from the insurance had driven off with his mother. What he had not known was what he had been running to protect.

"He wasn't Sears's man," I said. "He'd been hired by your grandfather to find out who was taking the clocks, and then by your mother to keep your father from being thrown off a sea wall. You hit the one man on the island who'd been paid to look after him."

Kit took his hands out of his pockets and looked at them.

"And your father knew," I said. "I told him, in the harness room, on the way up the tower. He said, *Kit did? For me?* He said that you were a young fool, and that he must speak to you, and that he hadn't spoken to you properly since you were ten."

Kit said nothing for a long time.

"He never did," he said.

"No. He ran out of night." I gave him back the handkerchief with the red maple leaves. He had brought it, as I'd asked. "He had this at his neck, up the tower. It's what he was holding when his father told him the train was better than Thorne's. You picked it up off the boards."

He held it.

"I'll keep it, if you don't mind," Kit said. "I've got used to it."

* * *

Saskia asked about the green dress. She was laughing when she asked it. She'd read Mr Cade.

"Five to one," she said. "He sees me going in under the stable arch and Celia at the window at the same time. And he thinks he's going mad."

"You were going to look for Kit's body," I said.

"I was. He'd told me he'd killed a man in the tack room and I went to look, on my own, without a coat, because he was being sick in the gunroom. I called his name under the arch because I thought he might have gone back. I was shaking all over. And the place was empty and the lamp was smashed and there was blood on a blanket." She turned to Celia. "And you were in the window."

"I was in the window," said Celia Vane. "Trying to get up the nerve to go out in the dark. I'm a coward about the dark."

"He saw us both. He'd seen us both at seven in the hall. He even wrote that he knew there were two of us." Saskia shook her head. "Poor sod. He said he'd stood there with a whisky and watched the pair of us. He could have passed an exam on it."

"He'd been hit on the head," I said. "By your boyfriend. With a cricket bat."

"Christ. So he had."

* * *

They had all asked, except one.

Celia had sat through it at Mr Cade's place with her hands on his box. She had asked about her chloral and been told. She had not asked anything else. I had thought that she would ask about the Copy, or about Harwich, or about Sears.

When the others had finished, she said, "There's a thing I want to know. It isn't on your wall."

"Ask it."

"Who did it?" said Celia. "Not the bits. I've had the bits. You've given us all our bits, and very nicely, and I can see Toby's going to sleep tonight for the first time since 1994. But somebody's still got to have done it. A man's dead. Who killed my husband?"

They all looked at me.

I had known that someone would ask. I had known that it would be her.

"I've done the sum," I said. "Every square. I'll tell you what it comes out to. Nobody killed him."

"Nobody."

"The sea killed him. He went into it after a child that shouldn't have been on the road, because she'd broken a promise to her father, and his sister had waved to her. He held on to a post with no ladder for fifty minutes until he couldn't. Nobody pushed him and nobody put him there. He drowned because he went in. He went in because I did."

Celia said, "That's not an answer, darling. That's a confession."

"It's both," I said. "So's everybody's. That's what I've been trying to tell you. You asked who did it. Everybody at this table did it. Nobody did it. Both of those come out of the sum. There's one thing on that wall that nobody did except him, and that's the one thing that I want to tell you about. It's the one thing that isn't in any of the six."

"What's that?"

"What he did on the Wade. In 1974. And what his mother did."

The room went very quiet. I could hear the three clocks on the table, the radio one silent, the kitchen one with its tin tick, and the Vulliamy, slow and deep.

I looked at the empty place at the old man's right hand, with the red diary by the fork.

"He told it twice that night," I said. "Once to his father, in the tower, at ten to two. His father heard it and wouldn't write it down; he said it was Edmund's to tell. And once to me, on the Perch, at about half past four, while he held the post. He gave me a message for two people. I was to tell his sister and my father. I was nine and I was frightened and I didn't tell either of them for thirty years. My father died before I got there. Imogen I told in March, three weeks before she died, at four in the morning, and she said I'd have to tell the rest of you."

I got up. I went to the end of the lining paper, to the column for a quarter to five in the morning. There was nothing in it but a line of blue, in my writing, that says *Perch. E.V. and W.G.*

"I'll tell you what he said," I said. "And then it'll be eleven o'clock, and then it'll be the Hour."

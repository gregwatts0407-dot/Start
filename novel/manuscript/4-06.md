## Chapter Six

He was there.

He was sitting on one of the upturned pots with his elbows on his knees. The lantern stood on the bricks between his boots. The mare was behind him, dozing, with a hind foot tipped.

The gun lay across his knees. It was open at the breech. On the floor beside the lantern there were two short brass-ended cylinders, side by side, standing up like chessmen.

Imogen stopped in the doorway. All the way along the brick path she had been telling herself that he would be there, and that it would mean he had fired into the air. Now she saw his face.

He looked up. He did not get to his feet. In the low light he was grey to the lips, and his eyes had gone back in his head.

"You've been in the house," he said. "You've said your piece?"

"I've been in the house."

"That's good." His voice had no colour in it. "I'm glad you did that. I want you to have done it." He looked down at the gun. "Imogen. There's a thing I've to tell you. You'd best hear it before you come any nearer."

She looked at the two spent cases on the floor.

"You found him," she said.

"I found him."

"Did he tell you? What your father wanted to know?"

"No."

It was one word. She heard everything that was in it. Her heart seemed to stop and go on.

She came in. She shut the door behind her on the wind. She found that she was calm. It was the calm that she had felt once in the studio, when a solvent took hold too fast and she watched an inch of sky lift off a panel under her swab. One did not cry out. One put the swab down and thought very clearly about what could still be saved.

"Tom," she said. "Is he—"

"I'm trying to tell you. I never asked him. I never got to ask. He was up—"

"No."

She had crossed the floor. She was on her knees in the dead leaves in front of him, in her mother's dress, and she had put her hand over his mouth.

He stared at her above it.

"Don't," she said. "Don't tell me. Not now."

He took her wrist and moved her hand away, quite gently.

"You've got to hear it. You'll not want to come with me when you have. I'll not take you over that water on a lie."

"It isn't a lie if I don't ask." She was speaking fast and low. "Listen to me. My mother made a rule. From one o'clock to two, by this house, on this night, nothing that's said is to be held against the one who says it. It's put back with the clocks. I've thought all my life that it was a game. But I went into that hall just now to tell my father the truth, and I found there was something he needed more, and I gave him that instead. I don't know if it was right. I know it's done. And I know this. If you tell me now what you did with that gun, I shall have to know it. I shan't be able to put it back. And I shall not be able to get on that horse."

"That's why you ought to hear it."

"That's why I won't." She took his face between her hands. It was cold. "I hid from you once because I couldn't bear what you'd think of me. I lost twenty years by it. I'm not going to lose the rest because of what I might think of you. Whatever it is. I'll hear it in daylight, on the other side, in your father's kitchen. Not here. Not in this house. Do you understand me? I have waited twenty years to go across that road, and I'm going. I'll not be turned back halfway."

He looked at her. She saw him weigh it. He was a man who did not lie, and she was asking him for something very near it.

"It's not a thing that'll keep," he said. "It's a thing they'll come for me about."

"Then they'll come to Saltcote, and I shall be there."

"You don't know what you're saying."

"No. I don't. That's the point." She let go his face. "Is there anything to be done? Now? Tonight? Anyone to be fetched?"

He was silent a moment. His eyes went to the dark glass, toward the stables.

"No," he said. "There's nothing to be done. I went round and looked. There's nothing there to do anything for."

She shut her eyes. She thought of her brother at eighteen, on the sea wall, with his arms round her, holding a gun to her shoulder. *Squeeze it, Immy. Don't pull. Like shutting your hand on a bird.*

She thought: I will not know this. I refuse it. I'll know it tomorrow.

It was the worst thing she had ever done, and she did it with her eyes open. She has never pretended otherwise.

"Then we go at twenty minutes to three," she said. "By the house. My father's given me the time."

"Your father?"

"He told me while we danced. High water at eight minutes to one. A horse at twenty to three, and not before; three feet in the Rill and a set to the north." She saw his face change. "It's the same as you said. To the minute."

"How'd he know there was a horse?"

"I don't know. He thought I was my mother. He thought he was sending her to Amos." Her voice broke at last. "He said, 'I don't ask you to stay.' He said, 'You've a man waiting with a lantern.' Tom, he knew. I think he's always known. He was giving her leave."

Tom Garrow put the gun down on the bricks. He put it away from him, with the flat of his boot, into the leaves. He bent forward and laid his forehead against hers.

"God help the pair of us," he said. "I come over here to kill a Vane, near enough. And the old man's give me his daughter and his blessing, and never knew I was on the place."

* * *

She could not cross the Wade in grey silk and kid sandals. That was plain. She would be thrown off by the cold before they reached the Rill.

"I must go in again," she said. "For a coat. Boots. Ten minutes."

"I'll saddle up." He had stood. Some of the colour had come back to him, now that there was a thing to do with his hands. "Bring something for your head. And nothing you can't carry in front of you. She'll have the two of us and the sea; I'll not ask her to take a trunk."

"I've nothing to bring. I never have had, from this house."

She went by the kitchen yard. It was the nearest way to the back stairs and her own room. The moon was high, and the wet cobbles shone. From the tower, as she crossed, the great bell struck twice.

She stopped. She listened for the quarters. There had been none. Only the two heavy strokes, out of a silent sky.

Two o'clock. The Hour was over. Whatever was said now would count.

The back door of the kitchen stood open. There was a lamp burning within. She went along the stone passage past the scullery, in her sandals, quickly. Her mind was on a tweed coat and a pair of gumboots.

"That's a Vane walking," said a voice from the kitchen. "I can tell by the feet."

She stopped dead.

She knew the voice. She had known it all her life.

She stood in the scullery passage with her hand on the cold wall. A door closed, somewhere up the kitchen stairs. A chair scraped. Then there was the sound of an uneven step on the flags, and the light from the kitchen doorway was blocked.

Edmund was standing in the scullery passage at twenty past two with a candle, as alive as she had ever seen him, and she said to him the one thing she had promised herself she would never say.

But first, for the space of a breath, she could say nothing.

She had thought him dead. She had knelt on a brick floor not half an hour ago and refused to be told it, and she had believed it all the same. *There's nothing there to do anything for.* She had walked across the yard carrying that, like a stone in her chest.

And here he was. He filled the low doorway. He had his dinner jacket on and his collar open and no tie. His hair was wet and standing in spikes. There was a swelling on his forehead, on the left, as big as an egg and nearly black, and the eye beneath it was half closed. The side of his neck was all over small dark spots, and his collar was soaked red. He held a candle in a kitchen saucer, and in the other hand the leg bone of a goose.

He was grinning at her. One side of his face did not move as well as the other.

"Good God," he said. "It is you. I thought I'd got to the stage of seeing things. Toby said you'd come down in the grey, and I thought he was drunk." His eyes went over the dress, the bare shoulders, her hair. The grin went. "Immy. You look—"

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say like her. You do, but that's not it. You look like yourself. I haven't seen you look like yourself since you were twenty-four."

She found her voice.

"What have you done to your head?"

"Fell over my own feet. As usual." He touched the swelling, lightly, and winced. "And down some steps. And out of a wheelbarrow, if you'll believe it, but that's a long story and reflects no credit on the cook."

"And your neck? That's blood."

"Some idiot letting off at the duck. I was up the tower. I caught the edge of it." He hitched his left shoulder. "It's nothing. A few pellets. I've had worse from Father's gun at a pheasant shoot."

She put her hand on the wall again. She thought her knees would go.

*I never asked him. He was up—*

Tom had fired at a man on the tower. He had seen him fall, and gone round, and found nothing. So he had sat on a pot in the orangery for half an hour believing that he had killed her brother. And she had stopped his mouth, and he was sitting there now, saddling a horse, still believing it.

And her brother was standing in the scullery passage eating goose.

The relief was so great that it came out of her as fury. She had not known that it was there. She had kept it under the floor for twenty years, and all at once the floor was gone.

* * *

"You're alive," she said.

"So I'm told. People keep remarking on it."

"You're standing there. With a bone. Making jokes." Her voice was shaking. "Do you know what tonight has been? Do you know what I've—Papa thought I was Mother. I let him. I danced with him and let him tell me the tide, because he's spent twenty years believing that he gave her the wrong time and killed her. He's built his whole life round it. He was so happy, Edmund. He said, 'I've got it right now.'"

Edmund said nothing. He had put the bone down on the scullery shelf.

"And it was never his clock. Was it?"

"No."

"You know that. You've always known it. And so have I."

He looked at her. The candle shook a little in the saucer.

"I was in the orangery," Imogen said. "That night. I was there from eleven. I was supposed to walk across with her and I hadn't the courage, after what I'd said to you at tea. I sat behind the vine. I saw her lantern go down to the Wade. And I saw yours go after it."

"Yes."

"I saw them meet. Past the Perch. She was nearly over. Amos Garrow was standing at the far end with a light and she was a hundred and fifty yards from him. And you caught her."

"Yes."

"I saw the two of you stand there. An hour. More. I fell asleep watching. And when I woke it was six in the morning and you were walking up the lawn, past the orangery door, as close as you are to me now. You were wet to the neck. You had no lantern."

"I didn't know you were there," he said. It was very low. "I've sometimes thought so. I never knew."

"You went in and changed. You came down to breakfast and said you'd slept in the stable. I sat across the table from you and heard you say it. Then Pask came up from the saltings."

She had to stop. She had not known that she could still see it so clearly. The toast, and the marmalade spoon, and her father looking up.

"I've never said it. Not to Papa. Not to the police. Not to you. For twenty years I've come down here twice a year and sat at that table and passed you things. I let Papa think it was his clock, because if I'd told him it was you, I'd have had to tell him why you went. I'd have had to say that it was I who told you." Her voice rose. "I've hated you for that. Do you understand? Not only for what you did. For what you made of me. You made me a liar to my own father for twenty years, to cover us both. I couldn't see Tom Garrow because of it. I've lived in a cold flat mending other people's pictures because I couldn't bear to look at anything of my own."

"Immy."

"You went after her. You stopped her on the Wade. You kept her there, talking, shouting, I don't know what, a drunk boy who couldn't stand to be left, until the tide came. And then you got yourself out. You got out, and you left her. And you have let that old man wind a hundred and eleven clocks every week for twenty years and tell every one of them that it was his fault."

She was crying with rage. She wiped her face with her bare wrist.

"I said I'd never say it. I promised myself. I thought, if I ever once begin I shall not be able to stop. Well, I've said it. And it's gone two; I heard it strike. It isn't the Hour. It isn't going to be put back. I want it to count. I hope you never have another night's sleep as long as you live."

* * *

The scullery passage was quiet. Somewhere a tap dripped.

Edmund had not moved while she spoke. He stood in the doorway with his big shoulders against the jamb, holding the saucer. He had kept his eyes on her face the whole time. She had expected him to look away. He always had.

"Yes," he said.

She waited.

"Is that all?" she said. "*Yes?*"

"It's all of it that's true. I went after her. I caught her past the Perch. I kept her standing on that road for an hour and a quarter with the tide making, because I couldn't bear it, and I was drunk, and I was thirty-two years old and had never in my life been told no." His voice was quite steady. "That's true. You've had that right for twenty years. I'm sorry you had to keep it. I didn't know that you were."

"And then you saved yourself and left her."

He was silent.

"Didn't you?"

"That's the part you've got wrong," Edmund said. "It's worse than what you think, Immy. And it's better. I don't know which you'd say."

"I don't want to hear it."

"You ought to. You've a right. I told Father, an hour ago, up the tower. I'd have told you first if I'd known what you'd been carrying. I told that little nurse this afternoon that I'd tell the pair of you in the morning. She's got a book of Mother's, did you know? Her diary, for that year. It was in the clock that doesn't go."

"I don't want it."

She heard her own voice. It was hard and thin, like a child's.

"I have carried what I saw for twenty years. I saw two lights, and I saw one man come home. I don't want your account of it. I don't want to be told that it was more complicated, and that you suffered. I'm sure it was. I'm sure you did. I am not going to stand in a scullery at half past two in the morning and take that on as well."

"It isn't that. It's what she did. At the end. You ought to know what she did."

"She drowned," Imogen said. "That's what she did. A hundred and fifty yards from a man who'd waited for her for ten years. I'll not hear another word."

She saw it go home. He shut his eyes for a moment, as he had in the gunroom.

It is the thing that she has had to live with. He was trying to tell her. He had it ready. He had told their father an hour before, and he would have told her then, in two minutes, by a candle. She stopped him. She had refused to hear Tom, for love. She refused to hear her brother, for anger. She has thought since that there was not much to choose between the two.

"All right," Edmund said. He opened his eyes. He did not seem angry. He looked, if anything, relieved, and very tired, and oddly gentle. "All right, Immy. Another day. I've said it once tonight, and it nearly finished me. Father's got it now. He'll tell you. He won't remember that he knows it, but he'll tell you all the same, one morning when you're not expecting it. It'll come out with the tide tables."

He set the saucer down on the shelf beside the bone. He looked at her dress again, at the wet marks on the shoulders.

"You're going," he said. "Aren't you? Tonight. I can see it on you."

"Yes."

"With Garrow."

"Yes."

"He's on the island? How in God's name did he—" He stopped. He began to laugh. It hurt him; he put a hand to his neck. "The duck. Oh, my Lord. Of course. It was Tom Garrow letting off at me from the yard. I shouted at him. I couldn't see who it was; I said something about getting off my land." He leaned his head back against the door frame. "Well. I can't say I didn't have it coming. Is he a good shot?"

"He thinks he killed you."

"Does he? Then I shouldn't tell him otherwise till you're over the water. It'll make him mind the mare." His eyes had lit, the old wicked light, that she had not seen for twenty years. "No. That's unkind. Tell him he missed. Tell him he's a rotten shot, and that I said so, and that he can have the farm."

"What?"

"Saltcote. He can have it. Father's given it to them. He told me so himself, up the tower; he did it in the hall an hour ago, in front of Fenn. Freehold. *To Amos Garrow or his heirs.* So there'll be no caravans. I've been outbid by my own father." He was still smiling. "I find I don't mind. I find I don't mind anything tonight. It's a most peculiar feeling."

She did not know what to say to him. She had come into that passage with twenty years of hatred and had spent it all in three minutes. He had stood and taken it, and said yes. And now he was giving her a farm.

She thought: he has been hit on the head. He is not himself.

She thinks now that he was more himself, in that passage, than he had been since he was a boy.

* * *

"Wait," he said. "Before you go."

He was feeling in the pocket of his waistcoat. His fingers were clumsy. He brought something out and held it on his palm, under the candle.

It was a ring. It was a plain gold band, worn thin, such as every married woman of her mother's age had worn.

Imogen looked at it. She looked at his face.

"That's Mother's."

"Yes."

"She was buried in her ring. Papa said—"

"Papa never looked. He couldn't. Dolly laid her out. Dolly knew it was gone and never said. I've sometimes wondered what she thought." He turned his hand a little, and the gold caught. "She gave it to me. That night. Out there. She took it off her finger and put it in my hand and shut my hand on it. She said, 'Give that to your father.'"

Imogen could not breathe.

"I never did. I've had it in my pocket for twenty years. Every day. I've changed it from one waistcoat to another every morning of my life." His voice had dropped. "I couldn't give it to him without telling him how I came by it. So I kept it. That's what I am, Immy. I couldn't even carry a message."

He held it out.

"Take it. Give that to Father."

"Give it to him yourself. In the morning. You said you were going to tell him the rest."

"I've told him the rest. I forgot this. It was in my pocket and I forgot it; I was looking at his face." He took her hand, the left, and turned it over, and put the ring in her palm, and closed her fingers on it as his own had once been closed. His hand was very cold. "You do it. You'll make a better job of it. And I've an idea I shan't be about in the morning. I never am, after this night. I go to ground. Ask Dolly."

"Edmund—"

"You gave him Mother tonight. I heard. Toby says the old man was dancing. I've not been able to give him anything in twenty years but copies." He let go her hand. "Let him have that from you. Tell him she sent it. It's true."

She stood with the ring shut in her fist. It was warm from his waistcoat.

She should have asked him then. *How did she come to give it you? What did she do, at the end?* He would have told her. He was waiting to.

She was too proud. She had said that she would not hear another word, and she would not go back on it. And there was a man in the orangery, saddling a horse, who thought that he had done murder. And the tide would not wait.

"I must get my coat," she said.

"There's Mother's old tweed on the peg in the gunroom. And her boots. They've been there since. Nobody liked to move them." He stood aside from the door, to let her by. "Take those. She'd laugh."

She went past him. In the doorway she stopped. He was so close that she could smell the whisky and the blood.

She had told him that she hoped he would never sleep again. She wanted to take it back. She did not know how. She had no practice.

"You should have that head seen to," she said. It was all that she could find.

"I will. In the morning." He smiled at her. It was not the easy smile. It was a boy's, crooked, the one he had had before he learned the other. "Go on, Immy. Don't look back. She looked back."

She did not understand him. She thought that he meant their mother had looked back at the house, on the Wade, and wavered, and been lost. She thought that he was telling her not to hesitate.

She did as he said. She went through the kitchen and up the stone passage to the gunroom, with the ring in her hand. She took down the tweed coat from its peg. It still smelled of horse. She put her feet into her mother's boots. The game book lay open on the bench, with its pencil on a string, and she tore a leaf from the back of it and wrote a line, so that Dolly should not have the creeks dragged for her. *Gone across to Saltcote. Don't wait luncheon. I.* She left it under a cartridge.

She did not look back. She has been sorry for it every day of her life.

* * *

Tom was standing at the mare's head when she came into the orangery. He had her saddled and the lantern hung. He had not put the gun in its case. It lay where he had kicked it, in the leaves.

He looked at the old coat, and the boots, and the grey silk showing between. He looked at her face.

"What is it?" he said. "What's happened?"

"I've seen Edmund."

He did not move.

"He's in the kitchen. He's eaten half a goose. He's a lump on his head the size of an egg, and a few of your pellets in his neck, and he says to tell you that you're a rotten shot."

Tom Garrow stood with his hand on the bridle. She watched it reach him. His mouth opened. He put his other hand out behind him, feeling for the stone trough, and sat down on the edge of it as though his legs had been cut.

"He's on his feet," he said.

"He's on his feet and making jokes."

"I saw him go off the back of that tower. I saw him fall, Imogen. With his arms out. Into the fleet."

"I don't know what you saw. I know what I've just spoken to." She came and stood in front of him. "You didn't kill anyone. Do you hear me? You didn't."

He put his face in his hands. His shoulders shook. She thought that he was weeping, and then she saw that it was something else, a kind of long silent shudder, like a horse that has been ridden too hard. She put her arms round his head and held it against her mother's coat.

"I asked him for you," she said. "What your father wanted to know. He said yes. He went after her. He kept her on the Wade an hour and a quarter. That's what he said."

"Then she never turned back of herself."

"He said there was more. He wanted to tell me. I wouldn't hear it." She shut her eyes. "I was too angry. He said, another day."

"Another day." Tom lifted his head. His face was wet, and ten years younger. "I'll go to him another day. I'll go in daylight, by the road, with my hat in my hand and no gun, and ask him civil. I've done enough tonight on my father's account. He'd not have thanked me for the half of it."

"There's something else. Papa's given you the farm."

He stared.

"Saltcote. Freehold. He dictated it to Fenn in the hall, in the Hour. *To Amos Garrow or his heirs.* Edmund told me." She found that she was laughing, and that the tears were running into her mouth. "There'll be no caravans. You came over here with a gun to get your father's ground back, and he gave it to you in the middle of a waltz without knowing you were there."

Tom looked at the gun in the leaves. He looked at it for a long time.

Then he got up. He picked it up, and broke it, and looked through the empty barrels at the lantern. He put it in its canvas case. He buckled the case, slowly, and strapped it under the saddle flap.

"I'll not leave it lying in your father's glasshouse," he said. "But I'll tell you this. It's the last time I carry that anywhere but after rabbits."

He turned to her. He took her left hand. She had forgotten that it was shut. He opened her fingers.

The ring lay on her palm.

"What's this?"

"It was my mother's. Edmund had it. She gave it to him that night, on the Wade, for Papa. He's carried it for twenty years." She closed her hand again. "I'm to give it to my father. I can't now. I'll bring it across to him on Sunday."

He looked at her fist, and at her face.

"That's a queer thing for a woman to do," he said slowly. "That's about to leave a man. To send him back her ring by her son."

"Yes."

"That's not a woman being dragged."

"No."

They looked at each other. Neither of them knew what it meant. It would be thirty years before anyone told them.

Outside, the wind had dropped altogether. The moon stood over the broken roof. From the tower the bell did not ring; there was nobody now to ring the quarters. But Tom looked at the big steel watch on his wrist.

"Twenty-five past three," he said. "By England. That's twenty-five past two by your father."

"Then we've a quarter of an hour."

"We'll walk her down. I want her warm when she meets the water." He untied the halter rope. He stood a moment with the mare's white head against his shoulder. "You're sure? There's no going back over it tonight, once she's in. And I've a damp house and a child you've never seen and a hundred and forty acres of mud."

Imogen put the ring into the pocket of her mother's coat. She buttoned the coat to the neck.

"I held your lamp in May," she said. "I've been waiting since to be asked what for."

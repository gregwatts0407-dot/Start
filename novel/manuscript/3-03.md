## III. The Red Book

It was written in pencil, in the small upright hand that I knew from the tide tables. There was a week to an opening, and for most weeks she had written no more than a few lines. Some were empty. I read it through from January, sitting in her chair, and the light went from the garden while I read.

I am going to copy out some of it. Dr Imrie has asked whether I think I have the right, and I have told him that I do not, but that I cannot make what followed understood without it. I have the book still. I have it by me as I write. I did not make these entries up, though I have sometimes wished that I had.

*Jan. 3. Frost. L. in the Shop from six till midnight with the Copy; he has got as far as the moon. He came up to bed and told me the equation of time for an hour. I told him the equation of marriage, which is that one of us talks and the other is supposed to be asleep. He said, "Quite so," and went on. I do love him. It is like loving a cathedral. One is very proud and it is cold about the feet.*

*Jan. 19. Rode over to Saltcote on the ebb. A. was dipping. He came across the yard with his sleeves rolled and sheep on him and said, "Well, Daisy." Ten years in March. I am fifty-three and when he says that I am nineteen. It is ridiculous. I told him so. He said he'd never claimed to be anything else.*

I stopped there, the first time. I looked at the bundle of tide tables on the desk, with the ringed nights and the letter A. I understood them then. They were the nights when the Wade lay open in the small hours, and a woman might cross and come back before the house was awake.

I thought of the old man on the stairs. *I think that she was going to someone. Was she?*

I went on.

*Feb. 8. Edmund has finished the lunar train for L.'s Copy. Eleven weeks' work, and I never saw anything so fine; it is like lace. He brought it up to show me before he took it down. L. looked at it through his glass for a quarter of an hour and said, "It will serve." E. went out and did not come in to dinner. I said to L. afterwards, could you not have told him it was good? He said, "He knows it is good. I should not have said it would serve if it were not." It is quite useless. He thinks that praise is a sort of inaccuracy.*

*Mar. 2. E. drinking. Celia came to me. I said, he is thirty-one, I can't smack him. She said, he would mind you. I don't think it is true. I think I am the thing he minds about, which is different. I made too much of him when he was small, to make up for L., and now he can't stand unless someone is looking.*

*Apr. 21. Summer time began last month and L. has finally finished putting the clocks on. He does it like a man burying relations. Every year he says the same: "It is a lie, Margot, by Act of Parliament." I said, darling, it's only an hour. He said, there is no such thing as only an hour.*

*May 30. Imogen home. She is twenty-four and looks at pictures all day and thinks I don't see where she goes in the evenings. She goes where I go. Young Tom Garrow is back from the agricultural college, brown as a nut, and can't string three words together, and she comes in with straw in her hair and her face shining. Like mother, like daughter. God help the Vanes, we can't keep off that farm. I ought to speak to her and I've not the face.*

*June 14. A. says, come away. He's said it before. This time he had it worked out. There's a place in Suffolk his cousin wants to give up; Tom can have Saltcote. He said, "I'm fifty-seven, Daisy. I'd like to eat my breakfast with you before I'm past chewing." I laughed. Then I went and sat on the sea wall for an hour and didn't.*

*July 9. Told L. I was tired. He said, "You should rest." He was regulating the gallery clock. I stood behind him for five minutes. If he had turned round and asked me one single question I'd have told him the whole of it and stayed. He said, "She is gaining four seconds a week. I can't account for it."*

There was a good deal more through the summer. I shall not copy it all. She wrote about her garden, and a horse, and a quarrel with Dame Octavia Prowse over a jumble sale. She was very funny. I found that I was smiling as I read, and then that I had stopped. One could see it coming, week by week, in the spaces between the jokes. The entries about her husband grew kinder and shorter.

*Sept. 20. Told A. yes.*

That was the whole of the entry. It had been written hard; the pencil had gone through the paper.

*Sept. 28. It will be the night of the Hour. It must be. I started that nonsense, in '51, to make them talk to each other, and I've stood in that hall every year since and made them say their piece, and never once said mine. I'll say it this year. I'll tell L. to his face, in the Hour, when by my own rule he can't hold it against me, and then I'll go. It is cowardly to use the rule. But I've earned one cowardice. I'll not creep out by the back in the dark like a housemaid.*

*Oct. 11. Imogen knows. She walked in on me sorting my jewel case. She's not a fool. I told her all of it; I thought, she of all people. She went white. She said, "With Tom's father? You?" She seemed to think I'd stolen it from her. I suppose I had. She said, what about Papa. I said Papa would notice in about a week, when the gallery clock wanted winding. That was unkind and not true and she knew it. We both cried. She has promised to say nothing. She says she'll come with me as far as the farm.*

*Oct. 22. A. will be at the Wade head from one, with the lantern and the mare. He says he'll show the light the whole time so I can walk to it. He says he'll come out and meet me. I said no. I said, I'm going to walk off this island on my own feet and you're going to stand there and watch me come. He said, "You always were a hard woman, Daisy." He was crying. Sixty next year, with hands like spades.*

And then the last.

I shall give it entire. It filled the space for the Saturday and ran over onto the Sunday and down the margin. It was dated the twenty-sixth of October. It had plainly been written in more than one sitting, with different pressures of the pencil.

*Sat. Oct. 26. The Thorne says low water twenty-eight minutes past midnight by the sun, and the Wade good until a quarter past two. So by the summer clocks, which is what everyone but L. will be keeping till the Hour, that's low at 1.28 and shut at 3.15.*

*I'll go at one. That's by my watch, and I mean to keep it on my wrist and trust nothing else, because L. has just been in to say he has put all the hall clocks back already, this afternoon, before dinner, "to save a scramble at the Hour." So the house says six now, and the world says seven. He was rather pleased with himself. It'd be like him to drown me by being efficient. But I am not such a fool about water as that, and I shan't look at a clock in this house tonight. One by my wrist. That gives me two hours and a quarter to do a walk of fifteen minutes. I could do it on my knees.*

*I shall tell L. at half past twelve. I've got it by heart. Then up for my coat. I shall go in my stockings; the Wade eats shoes. I'll leave them by the bed where he'll see them. He'll know then that I meant it and didn't fall in a ditch.*

*Later. Edmund has been in. He knows. Imogen told him; they had a row at tea about something else, and it came out, poor child, she was beside herself. He stood in that doorway, the size of a wardrobe, and cried like a boy of six. He said, "You can't. You can't leave me with him." I said, darling, you're thirty-two, you've a wife and a child of your own. He said, "If you go I'll come after you. I'll bring you back. I'll carry you." I said, then you'll get your feet wet.*

*It was the wrong thing to say. I'm always saying the amusing thing and it is always the wrong one. He has gone down to drink himself brave.*

*I'll not put it off. If I put it off for E. I'll put it off for ever; he'll see to that. He must learn to stand up without me watching. Tonight is as good a night to begin as any.*

*It is a quarter to eight by the house and they are waiting dinner. I can hear Leo in the hall, telling someone the time.*

*I'm fifty-four years old and I am going to walk across the bottom of the sea to a man with a lantern. I ought to be ashamed. I've looked for it, and I can't find that I am.*

*One by my wrist.*

There was nothing after that. The pages for November were clean. In the space for the twenty-seventh, the Sunday, someone had begun to write a word, in the same pencil, and had only made the first stroke of a letter.

* * *

I do not know how long I sat there. When I looked up the room was nearly dark.

I am a methodical person and I made myself be methodical. I took a sheet of paper and I wrote down what the book said and what it did not.

It said that Marguerite Vane had not been deceived. Her husband had put the clocks back early, as he believed, and it had killed nobody. She had known of it. She had written it down, with a joke, and had resolved to go by her own watch. She had known the hour of the tide to the minute, from the clock in the library. She had allowed herself two hours and a quarter to cross a causeway that I had walked, with a case, in under twenty minutes.

A woman who sets out at one, to do a quarter of an hour's walk, is not caught by a tide that comes at a quarter past three.

So something had kept her. Something had held her on the Wade, or between the house and the Wade, for two hours. And there was a sentence written in the margin. *If you go I'll come after you. I'll bring you back.*

I thought of him at the morning-room window. *I woke up, and it was getting light, and I walked home.*

I folded the sheet of paper and put it in the red book. I put the red book in the pocket of my dress. I set the little gilt clock back in its place on the desk, with its door shut, and it looked as it had always looked, like a thing that had given up.

Then I went and stood under the portrait.

I had not planned to. I found that I had climbed the stairs and walked the length of the gallery, in the dusk, and that I was standing before her with my hand in my pocket. The cracked clock ticked under the frame. She looked down at me with her mouth a little open.

I said, aloud, "I have read it."

I am not a person who speaks to pictures. I can only say what I did.

I said, "He doesn't know. Your husband. He thinks that it was his clock. He has thought so for twenty years, and he's breaking his heart over it, and he is making a machine in the tower so that he can tell you the time. And your son knows that it wasn't, and has let him. What am I to do?"

I do not say that she answered. The light was going, and I was overwrought, and a painted face will take any expression that one brings to it. But I had looked at that portrait on twenty nights. I knew it as I knew my own hands. And I say that as I stood there the amusement went out of it. I thought that for a moment the woman in the grey gown looked at me as one looks at a servant who has opened a letter.

I turned and went back down the gallery. I did not run. But I felt the whole way that I was followed, and I did not look behind me.

* * *

I kept it for three days. I am not proud of that. I gave Mr Vane his draught and sat with him and walked with him, with the book in my pocket. On the Thursday night he asked me whether his wife had come in. I told him that she was dead. He said, "Yes, the clock, I had put it back," and I said nothing. It was the first lie I had told him. I told it by holding my tongue, and I found that a lie of that kind is quite as heavy as the other.

By the Saturday I knew that I could not go on. It was the twenty-second. The house had been turned upside down since the morning, for the dinner. I had slept late, and badly. When I came down at noon there were strange coats in the hall, and Gail told me that a gentleman had come from the insurance and was going round the clocks, and that Mrs Vane was in a state, and that I was to keep out of the kitchen.

I went to look for Mr Edmund.

He was in the library. It was a little after two. He was standing at the tray with his back to me, pouring from a decanter, and when he turned and saw who it was his face did what it always did. It lightened, briefly, as though someone had opened a shutter. I had seen it do that for no one else. I had been living on it for a fortnight.

"Miss Quaile. You're up early. It's hardly dark."

"I should like to speak to you, Mr Vane."

"That sounds like a notice to quit. Don't say he has bitten you." He saw that I did not smile. He put the decanter down. "What is it?"

I had thought of a great many ways to begin. In the end I took the book out of my pocket and held it where he could see it.

He knew it. That was the first thing. I had expected that I should have to explain what it was. He looked at the red cloth and the gilt figures, and all the colour went out of his heavy face and left it grey.

"Where did you get that?"

"It was in the gilt clock on her desk. The one that doesn't go. The works had been taken out."

"In the *clock*." He made a sound that was nearly a laugh. "Oh, God. Of course it was. The one place in the house my father would never open, because she'd asked him not to. I hunted for that book for a month. I had the floorboards up in her room." He had not taken his eyes off it. "Have you read it?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"To the end."

He sat down. He sat on the arm of a leather chair, quite suddenly, and the whisky slopped over his hand, and he did not notice.

"Then you know more than my father does," he said. "You know more than anyone alive but me."

"I know that she was not mistaken about the time. I know that she meant to leave at one, by her own watch, and that she knew the tide. And I know that you told her you would follow her." My voice was not steady. "I don't know what happened after that. It isn't written."

"No. She'd not have had the chance to write it."

"Mr Vane, I must ask you. Did you follow her?"

He looked up at me. I have thought about that look more than about anything else in my life, more than about Mrs Aske's hand. I had braced myself for anger. I had thought he might deny it, or tell me that it was none of my business, which was true. He looked at me as a man looks who has been carrying a heavy thing up a long stair, and has reached a landing, and found someone standing there.

"Yes," he said.

That was all. He did not go on. I waited, and the fire ticked, and behind me on its table the great clock went quietly under its glass.

"I'm not going to tell you the rest," he said at last. "Not because you've no right. You've as much right as anyone, now. But there are three people who should have heard it before you, and one of them's just been buried, and I'll not make it four by telling a stranger first." He rubbed his face with his dry hand. "What are you going to do with that?"

"It isn't mine. It was his wife's. I think I must give it to him."

"Do you know what it'll do to him?"

"I think it will tell him that he didn't kill her."

"It'll tell him she was leaving him. For Amos Garrow. A tenant. A man he's nodded to across a gate for fifty years. He's half known it, and he has spent twenty years not knowing it, very hard. He's built all this." He moved his hand, at the room, the house. "The hour. The clocks. It's a kind of church. He's the only worshipper, and it's dedicated to the proposition that it was all a mistake about the time. You'd pull it down."

"I should be telling him the truth. He asked me to. He asks me every night."

"He does. And you do. I've heard you." His mouth twisted. "I stood outside his door one night last week and heard you tell my father that my mother was dead. I've never had the nerve to do it in twenty years. I nearly came in and went on my knees to you."

I did not know where to look.

"I can't choose what is true, Mr Vane," I said. "I can only choose whether I say it. And I can't sit beside him another night with this in my pocket."

"No. No, I see that you can't." He was quiet. Then he held out his hand. "Let me look at it. The last page. I shan't keep it."

I gave it to him. He opened it at the ribbon. He read standing, with his head bent, moving his lips slightly, as a child reads. I watched him come to the part in the margin. I saw him stop. He stood for a long time without turning the page, and I looked away, since there are things that one does not watch.

"*Then you'll get your feet wet*," he said. His voice was very low. "Yes. That's what she said. She wrote that at about seven; I'd only just left her. I went down and drank a bottle of my father's port, straight off, in the gunroom." He shut the book. "I've wondered for twenty years what she thought of me, at the last. Whether she'd put it down. And there it is. *He must learn to stand up without me watching.*" He laughed, and it broke in the middle. "Well. I didn't, Ma. I'm sorry. I never got the trick of it."

He held the book out to me.

"I thought you'd want to keep it," I said.

"If I keep it I shall burn it. I've burned enough in this house. You're the archivist. You hold it." He put it into my hand, and for a moment his fingers were round mine, over the red cloth. They were quite cold. "Will you do something for me? I've no claim on you. But will you wait until tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow?"

"Tonight's the twentieth. He's been getting ready for it all the year. I don't know what he thinks is going to happen; he's got some notion, he's been up and down to that tower. Let him have it. Let him open his door and say his piece. And in the morning, when it's over, and they've all gone home, you bring him that book. I'll be there. I'll stand beside you while he reads it. And then I shall tell him the rest. What isn't in it. What happened on the Wade."

"You will tell him?"

"I give you my word. For what that's worth; ask anyone." He tried to smile. "I've been going to tell him every October for twenty years. I get as far as the door. I think perhaps I needed somebody to stand behind me and say, I can't sit beside him another night. I never had a sister who would. Imogen thinks she knows already, and what she thinks is nearly right, and worse than the truth, and she's never asked." He looked down at me. "You're a hard little thing, Miss Quaile. You're like a splinter. One doesn't know it's in till one tries to move."

I ought to have been offended. I felt my eyes fill.

"Tomorrow, then," I said. "After breakfast."

"After breakfast. In the Shop. He's always best down there, among his tools." He picked up his glass and looked at it and put it down again, untasted. I never saw him do that at any other time. "And if for any reason I'm not there—"

"Why should you not be there?"

"No reason. I've some business to see to tonight. A thing I've got to get rid of. It's nothing." His eyes went past me for an instant, to the table in the window, and I saw that same look of dislike that I had seen on him by moonlight. "If I'm not there, you give it to him anyway. And you tell him that Edmund said he was sorry, and that it wasn't the clock. Will you remember that? It wasn't the clock."

"I shall remember."

"Good girl," said Edmund Vane.

I went out. I have gone back to that room many times since, in my mind. He had said that he would tell his father in the morning. He had put his glass down. I thought that I had done a good thing, and that for once in my life I had spoken at the right moment. And I thought that on the next day the old man would know the truth, his son would be free of what he carried, and I should have been the means of it.

I see now what else I had done. I had told a man who had kept a secret for twenty years that by tomorrow he would have no secret. I had set a term to it. I had said, in effect, *You have until morning*, to a person who was known to disappear on that night of the year, and who had business to see to on the water.

I have asked Dr Imrie whether he thinks that a man might choose not to be there in the morning. He says that I cannot know. He says that I am to write down what occurred and leave the reasons. I am doing it. But I heard Edmund Vane say, *If for any reason I'm not there.* I heard that, and I went out of the room and shut the door.

* * *

I must now put down a thing that happened at about four o'clock that afternoon. I had thought of leaving it out. It does not belong with the rest, and I have never been able to make it belong. But I have been told to explain nothing, and so I am not entitled to choose.

I had gone up to the Admiral's room to see that the fire was laid and the tray set for the night. The old gentleman was in the Shop. I was alone on that floor. I had knelt down at the hearth to straighten the fire irons.

I heard a child singing.

It was quite soft and it was close. I thought at first that it was in the room with me. It was a thin, clear, tuneless voice, such as children have when they sing to themselves over a game, without knowing that they do it. I could not make out words. There was a line that went up, and one that came down, and then the first again.

I stayed where I was, on my knees. I looked round the room. There was the bed, with its curtains, and the seven clocks, and the window.

The singing went on. And I found where it was. It was coming down the chimney.

I put my head in under the mantel and listened. It was much plainer there. One could hear that there were words, though not what they were. And it came from above. The flue of that room goes up through the floor over it and joins another, as they do in old houses. I knew what was over it. I had been told on my second day, when I asked what the barred window was on the top landing. It was the old nursery, where Mr Edmund and Miss Imogen had slept as children, and it had been shut up since 1955.

I do not think that I was frightened, at first. I was a nurse. I thought only that there was a child in the house and that nobody had told me. I thought that one of the guests must have brought one.

I went up. There is a narrow stair from the end of the first-floor passage to the top landing, and it is uncarpeted. I went up it quietly. The landing was dim. There are three doors there, and the one at the end has a small square window beside it, set high, with two iron bars.

The singing had stopped. I did not know at what moment it stopped.

I tried the door, and it was locked. I knocked, and I felt foolish doing it. I said, "Is someone there?" There was no answer. I put my ear to the panel. I could hear nothing at all. It was not the quiet of an empty room. I thought it was the quiet of somebody keeping quite still.

There was a keyhole, an old wide one. I stooped and looked.

I could see a strip of bare floor, and the lower half of a window with bars, and grey light. In the middle of the strip of floor there was a rocking horse. It was a big one, dappled, with a red saddle and most of its mane gone. It was facing me.

It was moving. It was only a little, a few inches forward and a few inches back, slowing as I watched. It was moving as a rocking horse moves after someone has got down from it.

I straightened up. My heart was going very hard. I stood with my hand flat on the door, as Mr Vane stood every night at that other door on the floor below.

There was a smell on that landing. I noticed it then. It was faint and sweet and sharp, and for a moment I could not think what it was, it was so out of place. It was oranges. It was the smell that is left on one's fingers when one has peeled a small orange, of the kind that is put into children's stockings at Christmas.

"You'll not get in there, miss."

I turned round so sharply that I struck my elbow on the wall. Mrs Pask was standing at the head of the stairs. She was out of breath, as though she had come up quickly, and she had flour to the elbows. I had never before seen her above the ground floor.

"I heard a child," I said. "Singing. I heard it in Mr Vane's chimney."

"That'll be the wind. That flue sings. It always has."

"There is no wind."

"There's always wind at Compline." She had her eyes on my face. They were small and brown and quite steady. "That room's been shut since Miss Imogen went to school. There's nothing in it but the horse and a few old toys and the damp."

"The horse was rocking. I saw it through the keyhole."

"It's the draught. Them floors are all on the tilt. You open a door downstairs and it'll go for an hour." She came a step nearer. She put herself, I saw, between me and the nursery door, not obviously. "There's no child at Compline, Miss Quaile. There's not been a child sleep in this house since 1955. You've been on your feet too many nights, and it's the day of the dinner, and everyone's on edge. You go and lie down for an hour."

"I smelled oranges."

Something moved in her face. It was gone at once.

"I've a box of satsumas in the kitchen for the table," said Mrs Pask. "It'll have come up the back stairs. Now you go down, there's a good girl, and let me get on. I've a goose."

I went down. I did as I was told, because I was told it by a sensible person, with flour on her arms, in a voice that had no doubt in it. I lay on my bed for an hour and I looked at the ceiling. I told myself that the flue sang, and the floors were on the tilt, and the smell had come up from the kitchen.

I will put down what I know. I know that the kitchen is at the other end of the house. I had been at Compline three weeks and had never once smelled cooking on the top landing. I know that the air that afternoon was as still as glass; the gale did not get up until dark. And I had listened to wind in chimneys all my childhood, on a cliff. It doesn't go up, and come down, and then go up again in the same place, like a child who has only learned the first two lines.

There is no child at Compline. I have that on the word of a woman who had lived there forty years and whom I never knew to say a thing she did not mean.

I heard one. I am not able to write anything else.

* * *

At twenty past five I carried the tray down to the pantry, to have a fresh glass put on it. On the turn of the main stair there was a young man standing in my way. He was very tall, and soaked to the skin, and was holding his shoes. He looked at me with his mouth open.

There was a small crab on his shoulder. It was alive. I told him of it. I do not know what else I said.

I mention him because he will come in again. I remember thinking, as I went on down, that he had a foolish kind face, and that he was the first person I had seen in that house in three weeks who did not seem to be listening for something.

At six I dressed Mr Vane. I got him into the evening clothes that had been laid out, which hung on him, and I tied his tie. He stood quite still for it, with his chin up, like a child.

"It is tonight," he said.

"Yes, Mr Vane."

"The twentieth. You have the count? I lose the count."

"It's twenty years tonight."

"Yes." He looked at himself in the glass. He put the loupe on its cord round his neck and settled it. "I finished the pinion. She is coupled and set. I went across at noon, when they thought I was at the bench, and I looked at her, and she is right." He turned from the glass and took my two hands. His were dry and warm and perfectly steady. "You'll stand by me at one. When I open the door. You are the only one of them who doesn't think me mad, or who has the grace to say so if she does. I shall want a witness."

"I'll stand by you."

"Whatever happens. You'll not leave me."

"No, Mr Vane."

I promised it. I had the red book in my pocket, under my hand. I had promised his son that I would keep it there until the morning. I thought it would be simple to keep both.

He gave me his arm, and we went down.

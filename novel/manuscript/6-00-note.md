# PART SIX {.part}

::: {.parttitle}
A True Relation of the Experiment at Compline

*by Leopold Vane, F.B.H.I.*
:::

## Editor's Note {.note}

Leopold Vane dictated this to Hester Quaile on forty-one evenings between the first week of December 1994 and the end of February 1995. She took it down in longhand in six exercise books, of the same make as the three she later filled herself. He would not have a typewriter in the house; he said that it sounded like a clock with a broken tooth.

She has told me how it was done. He sat in the wing chair in the drawing room, or latterly lay in his bed, and spoke slowly, with his eyes shut, in complete sentences. He never went back to correct one. Where he broke off, she left a line. Where he said a thing twice, she wrote it twice. Where he asked her a question in the middle of a paragraph, such as whether his wife had come in, she put down the question and her answer in the margin, and those margins are a document in themselves. I have not printed them. They are hers.

He meant it as a scientific paper. He had read a great deal, as a young man, of the kind of book in which a gentleman of private means builds an apparatus in his own house and afterwards relates, soberly and in the first person, what came of it; and when he came to set down the most important thing that had happened to him, that was the form he had. He numbered his sections. He stated his method. He was careful, as you will see, to separate what he observed from what he inferred.

I ought to say something about his mind, and I shall say it once. He was eighty-one and he was ill. There were days on which he did not know the year. Miss Quaile's account, which you have read, shows what that was like from the outside. This is what it was like from within, and I would ask you to notice that from within it does not look like confusion. It looks like a man moving, without warning and against his will, between rooms that are all equally real to him. He had a word for it. He called it leakage.

He believed that he had built a machine that returned to him one hour. I have a professional interest in that claim, of a kind that I will explain in its place, and I have been through his reasoning with some care. I can find no error in his arithmetic. His premises are another matter. But I will say this for the old man: the hour that he was waiting for did come back, at the instant he had calculated, to every clock in England. It comes back every year. He was quite right that nearly everyone is asleep.

His times are the house's. He would have been insulted to be told that there were any others.

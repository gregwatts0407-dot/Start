## III. Of the Dinner

§ 24. I shall now relate the events of the evening in their order. I have the first part of it clearly. I am less sure of some of the faces. I shall say where.

It was the twentieth keeping of the night since my wife's death. I had determined that it should be the last, whatever the result. If the experiment succeeded there would be no further need. If it failed I did not think that I should care to stand at that door again.

I was dressed at six. I went down upon Miss Quaile's arm. They were assembled in the hall, as they have been each year, with the fire lit and the candles, and the Font upon its table.

I should explain the Font. It is a pewter basin, of the time of Henry the Eighth, which came out of the ground when my grandfather drained the orchard; it is supposed that the monks used it. Since 1975 I have required every guest who enters this house upon the night to put his watch into it. I do not do this to be disagreeable. I do it because a watch that has been advanced under the Act is an instrument set wrong, and I will not have forty of them going about my hall contradicting the house. There is besides a practical reason, which bore upon the experiment. I did not know how the take-up would behave in the neighbourhood of a large number of escapements keeping the false hour. I wished them all in one place, and still.

I looked into the basin. There were nine or ten. I stirred them. I do not care for wrist watches as a class; the movements are stamped out by the thousand, and jewelled for show. But there was one among them which pleased me. It was a plain pin-pallet lever in a tin case, with a mouse painted upon the dial whose arms served for hands. It was the only article in that basin which made no pretence. I asked whose it was. A tall young man put up his hand, a grandson of my sister Dorothea, with her wrists. I saw at once that he had come to ask me for money and that he had broken something.

I shall say how I knew, since it has a bearing on my method throughout. I do not read faces. I have never been able to. My wife said that I should not notice if a person's nose fell off, provided that he continued to hold his fork correctly. But I have looked at hands for sixty-six years. A hand cannot dissemble; it has not the muscles for it. That young man was holding his right hand flat against his coat pocket, with the fingers a little spread. It is the posture of an apprentice who has snapped a pivot and put the pieces where he hopes the master will not look. I have seen it a hundred times at the bench.

I told him to ask me at one. I did not think that it could be anything of consequence. It jingled.

§ 25. I then made some remarks upon the Act, which I make every year, and which I shall not repeat, having given the substance above.

And I asked whether my wife had come down.

I must be exact about this, since I am told that it distresses people. I knew that she was dead. I had been told so by Miss Quaile at about a quarter past one that morning, as on most mornings, and I had retained it. When I asked whether Margot was down, I was not wandering. I was enquiring, as an experimenter, into the state of the apparatus.

For it had occurred to me, standing at the Font, that if the Reconciler was to return that hour at one o'clock, there might be some sign of it beforehand. A spring that is about to be let go will sometimes tremble. I thought that the house might already be leaning a little toward the other night. And I looked up the stairs, and for an instant I was quite sure that someone was dressing in the room at the end of the gallery, and would be late.

So I asked. It was my daughter who answered. She said, 'She'll be along, Papa.'

I looked at Imogen's hands as she said it. She had them clasped in front of her, the right over the left, very tight. It is how she stood as a child when she had said a thing that she wished were true.

§ 26. We went in to dinner at eight.

I have always had my wife's place laid. I know what is thought of it. I ask the reader to consider it as I do. A place at table is a kind of clock. It says that a person is expected. If I had left hers off, upon the first night after, I should have been saying that she was not; and I was not prepared to say so, and am not now. It is laid at my right hand. The girl serves it. I do not ask that anything be eaten.

I do not see her there. I should like that to be written down plainly. I am not so far gone. I have never, in twenty years, seen my wife at that table, nor heard her voice with my ears.

But I am a man who has lived all his life among clocks, and I will say what I do perceive. If you are sitting in a room, and there is a clock going in the next, with the door shut, you do not hear it. And yet you know that it is going. You would know at once if it stopped. There is a pressure of it, upon the mind, that is not sound. I perceive that the chair at my right hand is occupied in that sense. Something there is going.

And I know what she would say. That is not the same as hearing it. I lived with her for twenty-six years. When the girl with the cropped head announced that she did not eat anything with a face, I knew, as surely as I know the count of a train, that Margot was delighted, and had said, 'Leo, give the child what she wants; she has more sense than the rest of us.' So I said it for her.

I carved. I am told that I do it well. It is only a matter of seeing where the joints are.

§ 27. I must now record the observations that I made at table, since they were the last that I was able to make with a quiet mind.

I had in the house, by Fenn's arrangement, a person whose business it was to discover who had been taking the clocks. I had by that hour entirely forgotten his name and which of the guests he was. I knew only that he was there and that he was to tell me before one. I mention this because I see that it has been thought comic. It did not seem so to me. I have been made to depend, in the last years, upon arrangements that I have set up in my better hours and cannot afterward recall; it is like leaving a clock wound for another man to find.

But I did not rely upon him wholly. I looked for myself, in the only way I have.

I looked at their hands.

My son's wife was at the foot of the table. She has a wedding ring that has grown loose. She turned it, the whole of dinner, with her thumb, round and round. That is not guilt. That is a person winding something up. She was getting ready to do a thing.

My grandson sat with his hands under the table. When he brought them up to take his glass, they were white at the knuckles. He is a pianist, and keeps his hands like a surgeon's. He was frightened; but he kept looking toward his father, and I judged that it was for him.

My daughter held her fork and did not use it. Her eyes went more than once to the west window, where there is nothing to see after dark but the water. She was not thinking of clocks at all.

The young nurse had her hands in her lap. Her right kept going to the pocket of her dress and coming away. She had something there. I did not suppose it mine.

The solicitor had his fingers together.

There was a heavy man opposite my son, whom I did not know, who ate steadily. He had the hands of a policeman, thick, with the nails cut square. I see now that that will have been Fenn's person. At the time I took him for something to do with drains.

And there was Edmund.

He sat upon my right, the second place beyond his mother's. He was drinking. He has drunk for twenty years, and I have watched his hand grow less sure, each October, until I could hardly bear to see it lift a glass. A hand that could feel a thousandth of an inch.

It was steady.

I noted it particularly. He filled his glass four times while I watched, and raised it, and set it down, and there was no tremor in it whatever. I have seen that in a workman once or twice. It comes when a man has been dreading a thing for a long while and learns at last that it is to be that day. The dread goes out of the fingers. He had the hand of someone who has been waiting to be caught, and has just been told the hour.

I knew then. I did not need Fenn's man. I think that I had known since I lifted the cloth in the long cupboard.

§ 28. I rose for the toast before the sweet. We drink it each year. It was my wife's, and she meant it lightly. *To absent time.* She meant the hour that we should not have again until the spring. I have kept it, though for twenty years it has meant to me something that she did not intend.

I had prepared a few words upon the anniversary. When I stood, they had gone. I said so. I have found that it is best to say so at once; people are embarrassed if one hunts.

And then I did a thing that I had not planned.

I said that one of them had been robbing me.

I have considered since why I said it there, at my own table, before strangers. I had meant to wait. I had meant to hear from Fenn's man, and then, in the Hour, to take the person aside. I think that it was the hand. I looked down the table and saw my son's hand lying beside his glass, quite still, that had been the best I ever trained. I thought of it lifting my Tompion out of its case. And I found that I could not sit and eat a syllabub.

I told them what had been done, and with what. I told them that I knew my own work from another man's blindfold, and I pointed, without turning my head, to the two in that room which were mine. I heard somebody draw breath. I do not know who.

I told them that I did not know which of them it was. That was not true. It was the first deliberate untruth that I have told in this house since my wife died, and I told it at her table, with her at my elbow. I knew what she would say of it. She would say, 'Leo, that is the first sensible lie of your life.'

For I did not want to name him. I wanted him to come to me. I had set the Copy in the library as a test of his eye. I set this as a test of something else. I said that I should know by one o'clock, and that then it would be the Hour, and that whoever it was might come and tell me why. I said that I should not want anything more. Not the police, nor the money. Only the reason.

I said, 'I have not got so many hours left that I can afford to waste the odd one.'

I had not meant to say that either. My voice was not steady. I sat down.

I asked what was upon my plate. I am told that I asked my wife whether we had ordered it. I do not recollect that. I recollect only that I looked along the table at Edmund, and that he was not looking at me. He was looking at the heavy man with the policeman's hands, and smiling.

His hand had not moved.

§ 29. The ladies withdrew. The port came the wrong way, through an error of the vicar's, and I put it right.

I did not speak to my son. He went and sat by the heavy man, astride a chair, as he used to sit upon the bench in the Shop when he was fifteen and wished to argue. I could not hear what was said. I saw him laugh, and strike the other upon the shoulder, and go out.

He passed behind my chair as he went. He did not stop. But I felt him check, for the length of one step, as a pendulum checks at the top of its swing.

I thought, he will come to me in the Hour. I was sure of it. I thought, I have left the door open for him as plainly as a man can. I have said before a dozen people that there will be no punishment. He has only to walk across the hall.

I did not then know that he meant to be gone before the Hour began.

§ 30. We went into the drawing room. My grandson played.

He plays the Impromptu of Schubert in G flat. My wife was fond of it. She had Edmund taught it as a boy, and he taught it to Kit, and so it comes down. It is in a slow twelve-eight. I have never been able to hear music as others seem to; I hear it as a going train, and ask myself whether the beat is even. That piece is very even. There is a figure in the left hand that goes on from the first bar to the last without once altering, like an anchor escapement, and over it the other hand does as it likes.

I sat in the wing chair. Miss Quaile was beside me. I shut my eyes and counted the beat.

At twenty minutes past ten the electric light failed.

I record this as a material circumstance. I had not arranged it; it was an accident of the weather. But it was favourable to the experiment and I had not foreseen that it would be.

The mains supply of this country is an alternating current of fifty cycles in the second. It is held to that figure with great strictness by the generating authority, since the cheap electric clocks in people's kitchens count the cycles and would otherwise go wrong. That is to say, every wire in my walls was carrying, all that evening, a pulse regulated to the false time. I had not considered it. I am not an electrician. But when the lamps went out, I felt something go out of the house with them, a kind of hum that I had not known was there. Everything that was left going under my roof was now driven by a spring or a weight and kept the true hour.

I thought, that is better. That is as it was in Thorne's day. Now there is nothing in this house to interfere.

The boy finished the piece in the dark. He has his grandmother's nerve.

§ 31. It was then that I had the third leakage of that day, and the longest.

I heard the music end. I opened my eyes. The room was lit.

It was lit by the old lamps, with the pink shades, that my wife had got rid of in about 1960. There was a different paper on the walls. Edmund was at the piano. He was a boy of thirteen, in a grey flannel suit, with his hair wetted down, and he had just played the Schubert through for the first time without a fault, before company. He was looking across the room at his mother, and not at me.

She was upon the sofa. She had the grey silk on. She was being painted in it that winter. She put down her coffee cup and clapped her hands. She said, 'Oh, well done! Leo, did you hear him? He never dropped a note.'

I was standing by the fire. I was forty-two. I heard myself answer her. I had forgotten that I ever said it. I said, 'The left hand was a little hurried in the middle section.'

I saw his face. I had not seen it at the time; I do not see faces. But I was looking at it now, from the place where I had stood, with eighty-one years behind my eyes, and I saw exactly what went out of it.

And I saw her look at me. It was not anger. She looked at me as she did when I had taken the gallery clock down for the fourth time. Then she got up. She said, 'I'm going up, darlings. I'm going to take my shoes off and read for half an hour, and come down for the Hour.' She put her hand on the boy's head as she passed.

She went out of the door. I watched her go. I saw the grey silk go round the edge of it.

Then the lamps were gone. There was firelight, and my grandson sitting at the piano with his hands between his knees, a young man of twenty-four.

I said, 'Thank you, Kit.' I said it to him as I ought to have said it to his father, thirty-nine years before.

And I said that she had gone up. I am told that it caused some pain. I said that she always went up after the Schubert, and took her shoes off, and came down for the Hour. I asked Imogen whether she had seen her go.

I had seen her go. I had that moment watched her leave the room. I was not confused as to the year. I knew that it was 1994. But I had been in 1955 an instant before, and the door had not finished closing.

I took it for a sign. I thought, the engine is drawing. It is five hours before the stroke, and already the house is letting me through.

Imogen said that she had seen her. She said that she was a little tired.

§ 32. I slept, I think. I am very old and had been upon my feet since six in the morning.

I do not know for how long. I have no record of that interval and shall not invent one. I was aware of people moving. I was aware that Miss Quaile was by me, and then that she was not.

I was woken by a hand.

It was upon my left shoulder. It rested there. It was a large hand, and warm, and it did not press. It lay for the space of perhaps two beats of the mantel clock, and was lifted.

I knew whose it was without opening my eyes. I should know the weight of that hand anywhere. I had it in mine upon the file, to guide it, every day for six years.

I opened them. He was going from me toward the door. I saw his back.

I have said that I do not read faces. But he turned, at the door, to say something to the room; he said that he was going to look at the generator. And for that moment the firelight was upon his face, and I saw it.

I have seen that face twice before. I saw it when he was eleven, and had taken a watch of mine to pieces without leave and could not put it together, and stood outside the Shop for an hour before he came in to say so. And I saw it upon the morning of the twenty-seventh of October, 1974, at the breakfast table, when he came down in dry clothes and said that he had slept in the stable.

It is the face of a man who is going to do a thing that he is ashamed of.

He went out. I heard his step in the passage. He has had that step since he was fourteen and hurt his knee.

I looked at the clock upon the mantel. It was half past eleven.

I thought: he is not going to wait for the Hour.

And I thought: it is tonight that he means to take the Thorne.

§ 33. I got up out of my chair. No one prevented me. Miss Quaile was gone; I did not know where. My daughter had been in the window and was not there. The solicitor was asleep. Dame Octavia Prowse was asleep, with her mouth open, as she has slept in that chair after dinner since about 1950.

I went into the hall.

I am going to describe exactly what I did there, and why, since it has a bearing upon what followed and I have been asked about it.

The regulator stands to the left of the fire. It is by Benjamin Vulliamy, of the year 1810, a plain month-going movement in a mahogany case, with a dead-beat escapement and a mercury pendulum. It is the master clock of the house within doors. I set all the others by it, and it by the tower.

I opened the case. I stopped the pendulum with my hand. I unhooked the weight.

I had two reasons, and I held them both at once, and I cannot now say which came first.

The one was scientific. I had been considering all that day what would occur at the instant of reversal. If the hour was returned, and I passed through it a second time, then every clock in this house would be asked to register the same sixty minutes twice. I did not know what a fine movement would do under those conditions. A turret clock is a coarse thing and would take no harm. But I did not care to have a Vulliamy regulator, upon which the whole house depends, put through an hour that it had already counted. It seemed to me a prudent thing to take the power off the master clock for the period of the transit, and restore it afterward.

The other was older. One stops the clocks in a house where there has been a death. My mother went round and did it when my father died, with a feather, as her mother had. I did it for my wife. The pendulums are held, and the weights are taken off, and they are not let go again until after the burying.

I stood in my hall with fourteen pounds of lead in my hand, upon its line, and I did not know why I should have thought of that.

I have said that I cannot now tell which reason came first. I must be more honest than that. I think that I knew. I think that when I saw my son's face at the drawing-room door, some part of me that keeps the count had already reckoned what was to come of that night, and had sent my hand to the clock, as one sends for the undertaker.

But I had not then reckoned what it would be. I supposed, if I supposed anything, that the death was mine. I am eighty-one. I thought that the experiment might very well kill me.

I did not put the weight down. I ought to have set it in the bottom of the case. I had it by the loop of its line, and I had turned from the clock already, toward the gunroom passage and the side door, and it came with me in my hand. I was thinking of my son. I was thinking that he had gone out to the yard, and that it was dark, and that I must find him before he did the thing.

I carried it out of the house as a man carries his umbrella.

It was twenty minutes to twelve. Every clock in the hall but one said so.

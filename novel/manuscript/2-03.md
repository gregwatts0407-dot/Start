## 3. In Which I Kill the Vicar

A country house without electricity is a very sociable place. Everyone carries a candle, and so one can see nothing of a person but the face, lit from underneath like a pumpkin's, and one is forever meeting these faces round corners.

I met a good many in the next half hour. I was looking for Edmund.

He was not in the drawing room. My great-uncle was, in a wing chair, with the nurse on a stool beside him, and I stood in the doorway for a moment for the pleasure of looking at the top of her head. Dame Octavia was asleep. Kit had finished at the piano and was sitting with his hands hanging between his knees, staring at the carpet. He looked up and saw me. "If you're after my father," he said, "so's everyone." Then he got up and walked straight past me and out, as if he had made up his mind about something.

Edmund was not in the library. There was nobody in the library but a clock under a green cloth.

He was not in the billiard room, or in a sort of gunroom that smelt of oil, or in the downstairs cloakroom, where I knocked. I went along a passage toward the back of the house. Ahead of me, going the same way with a candle, was a green silk dress. It turned a corner and I hurried after it.

"Mrs Vane! I say, Mrs Vane! I'm so sorry to bother you, but have you seen—"

The dress turned round. It had army boots under it.

"Do I *look* forty?" said Saskia.

I had a fit of coughing. It was all I could think of.

"It's all right," she said, when I had finished. "Everyone's doing it. The vicar called me 'dear lady' and asked after the roof. Have you seen Kit?"

"He went out of the drawing room. A minute ago."

"Which way?"

"I didn't notice. He looked a bit—"

"He's been a bit ever since the speech. He's gone all white and won't say. He keeps looking at that insurance bloke like he wants to hit him." She chewed her thumbnail. "I don't get this family. Is it always like this? Nobody says anything. There's that chair. Then the old man stands up and says somebody's been nicking the clocks, and they all just pass the port. In my family, if my nan said that at Christmas, there'd be a fight in the car park."

I said I thought it was a question of how one had been brought up.

"Yeah, well. I'm going to find him. If you see him, tell him I'm in the stables. He goes up that tower when he's upset. He did when he was little." She set off, boots ringing on the flags, and then turned. "Who are *you* looking for?"

"Edmund."

"He went out," said Saskia. "The big side door, about ten minutes ago, when the lights went. He was carrying a box. I held the door for him. He said, 'Thank you, Celia,' and I didn't like to say."

So he had gone to see to something. The generator, I supposed, if there was one. I felt that I could hardly pursue a man into a gale to tell him about a bedside clock. I went back to the drawing room and had a brandy.

* * *

I had two, in fact. I'm not proud of it, but I had had a trying day, and the brandy at Compline was on a tray by the piano with the glasses beside it, and nobody was counting. They were large glasses, the balloon kind that one can get one's whole face into. I mention their size and shape because they come into what follows.

At a little before eleven by the drawing-room clocks, of which there were four, I took my second brandy and a candle and went to have another look for my cousin. I had heard the side door bang.

There was a light in the dining room. I could hear his voice, and a laugh. I was by now in the mood in which one feels that a thing must be got over, and I didn't knock. I was talking over my shoulder as I went in, to the vicar, who was pottering along the passage behind me and had asked me something about my father.

I therefore entered the room backwards. I do this more often than I should like.

When I turned round, my cousin Edmund was standing by the sideboard with his wife. He had his hand on the side of her neck, and she had her cheek leaned against it. They looked, in the light of the one lamp, like a couple in an advertisement for something expensive. I leapt about a foot.

"Oh! I say! Sorry! I didn't—I'll come back."

"Don't be an ass," said Edmund. He took his hand away, without hurry. His hair was wet. "Come in. Have a drink. Have you got a drink? You've got a drink. Have another."

"I really only wanted a word. If it's not a bad moment. It's rather private."

"It's a perfectly good moment. Celia doesn't count. She's heard all my words." He was feeling in his pocket for cigarettes. He had put a glass down on the sideboard to do it, a balloon glass, with brandy in it.

I went over. I'm bound to say I went in a muddle. I had the candle in my left hand and the brandy in my right. I wanted both my hands, because one can't confess properly without them. So I put my glass down too. I put it on the sideboard, by the tray, next to his.

I have been asked about this, many times, by one person in particular. I wish I could be more helpful. I put my glass down beside another glass. They were the same kind of glass, with about the same amount in them, and it was dark. That is all I know.

"It's about the clock," I said.

Edmund had a cigarette in his mouth and a match halfway to it. He stopped.

"What clock?"

"The one Uncle Leo was—at dinner. What he said. That one of us had been—" I swallowed. "It was me."

There was a short silence. My cousin's wife had moved away. She was standing against the wall by a tall clock in the corner, with her hands behind her, quite still. She looked like somebody waiting for a bus that she doesn't expect to come.

Edmund lit his cigarette. He blew out the smoke. Then he began to laugh.

It started somewhere near his knees and worked up. He had to hold on to the sideboard. "You," he said. "*You*. Oh, my God. Dorothea's grandson. Out of a clear sky." He wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"How many have you had?"

"Only the one," I said, bewildered. "Honestly. Just the one."

"Just the one. He says it like a curate with an egg." He clapped me on the back, and my candle went out. "My dear boy. I can't tell you what a relief this is. I've been the black sheep of this family for so long, I'd forgotten there was room for another. What did you get for it?"

"Get for it?"

"Who had it off you? You didn't go to that crook in Camden Passage?"

"Nobody had it off me. It's in my pocket."

That stopped him laughing. He stared at me. I had begun to feel that we were not, as they say, on the same page. I was fumbling for matches, to relight the candle, and at the same time trying to get my other hand into my pocket to produce the exhibit. I would have had it out in another second. And my cousin Edmund, who was a clockmaker's son and had been at the bench from the age of nine, would have looked at it. He'd have said what the man Cade said to me seven hours later in a stable yard, and I should have gone to bed a happy man. The evening would have been perfectly uneventful. I often think of that.

But the vicar came in.

He came in smiling, with his hand out in front of him like a man walking in his sleep. He'd got no candle. He made straight for the lamp, and me. "Ah," he said. "Here we all are."

I was, I hope, properly brought up. An elderly clergyman had entered a room where drinks were going and had not got one. I didn't think. There were two glasses of brandy on the sideboard at my elbow, mine and my cousin's, and I picked one up and put it into his hand.

"How very kind," said the vicar. "I don't mind if I do. Your father always kept a good cellar."

"There you are," said Edmund. He reached past me and took up the other glass. "The Church has spoken. We'll adjourn. Toby, I want to hear this, I want to hear every word of it. But I've got to see a man about a boat." He drank. He stopped and looked into the glass and made a face. "God. What's this? It's corked."

"Brandy can't be corked," I said. I did know that. It's the one thing I do know.

"This is." He drank some more, to make sure. "It's got a taste like a chemist's. That's Father's economy; he'll have had it since the Coronation." He drank about half of it altogether, with the expression of a man doing his duty. Then he set it down, hard, on the sideboard. "No. Life's too short. Find me later. I'll be about; I'm always about on this night, ask anyone. Half past twelve or so. And Toby."

"Yes?"

"Whatever you've done, I'll lay you any money you like it isn't a patch on what I've done. So cheer up."

He kissed his wife on the top of the head as he passed her. She didn't move. He went out, with his limp, and I heard him go along to the drawing room and say something, and my great-uncle answer.

My cousin's wife came away from the wall. She took up the lamp. She didn't look at me, or at the vicar, or at the half-glass on the sideboard. She said, "Good night, Toby," in a queer flat voice, though it was not much after eleven and nobody was going to bed. Then she went out with the lamp and left us in the dark.

* * *

I got my candle going. The vicar and I stood and beamed at one another.

"And how is your dear father?" he said.

"Thriving," I said. I had found by now that this was the quickest way.

"Splendid. Splendid. I buried him, you know. A lovely service." He drank off his brandy in three swallows, with relish, like a child with cocoa. I should say here that the glass I had given him was, on the evidence, my own, and contained nothing but brandy. I've been over it with a pencil. He finished it, and looked into the bottom of it wistfully, and then his eye fell on the sideboard.

There stood my cousin's glass, where he had left it, half full.

"Now that," said the Reverend Aubrey Stiles, "would be a sin."

"I'm sorry?"

"Waste. Waste, my boy. I was at theological college during rationing, and it marks a man." He set down his empty one and took up the other. "If Edmund doesn't care for it."

"He said it was corked."

"I can't hear you, but I'm sure you're right," said the vicar, and drank it.

He didn't make a face. I don't believe he had tasted anything since about 1970. He finished Edmund's brandy to the last drop, patted his waistcoat, and took my arm. He suggested that we should go and sit somewhere with a fire and that I should tell him all about myself.

We went to the library. It was the nearest. I put him in a leather armchair on one side of the hearth and took the other, and I told him all about myself. I remember that I gave him the whole of the Thracian valley, with figures. He nodded. He said "Dear me" in the right places. He was an ideal listener, and I had reached the question of bonded storage, and was drawing a small diagram in the air, when I noticed that he had stopped saying "Dear me."

He had also stopped nodding. His head had gone back against the leather. His mouth was open, and his hands lay palm upward on the arms of the chair.

"Vicar?" I said.

Nothing.

"Mr Stiles? I say."

I got up. I held the candle to his face. He was a very odd colour. I'm aware that candlelight is not kind, but he was the colour of the candle. His eyes were not quite shut. There was a line of white showing under each lid.

I took him by the shoulder and shook him, gently and then not. His head rolled. One arm slid off the chair and hung. I have seen my share of men asleep after dinner, in the trade. They snore. They mutter. If you shake them, they say "What? Quite," and tell you they were resting their eyes. The vicar did none of these things. He just sat there with his mouth open and looked like a man in a waxworks.

I listened for breathing. I heard the fire, and the clock under its cloth in the window. Then I remembered that the clock under the cloth hadn't been ticking the last time I was in that room. It was some other clock, by the door. This is not relevant, but in such a moment the mind goes everywhere but where it's wanted.

And there came into that mind three things, one after another, like coaches of a train.

*It's got a taste like a chemist's.*

*I gave him the glass.*

*One wants to be sure which glass.*

I am not going to pretend that I thought it through. I thought, with great speed and no logic at all, the following. Somebody had put something in the brandy. It had been meant for Edmund. Edmund had enemies; the house was full of the atmosphere of it; there was a detective on the premises. Edmund had detected the taste and stopped. The vicar, who could taste nothing, had not. And the vicar had got it from my hand. I had picked a glass off the sideboard and given it to him with a smile. Then I'd stood by while he drank the second one too, and said nothing at all, because I am a wine merchant and I know that brandy can't be corked.

I had killed the vicar. In my uncle's library, on the same evening that I had broken his clock.

I should like to put on record my first words on realising this. They were: "Oh, I *say*."

* * *

There was one person in that house who would know what to do with a dead clergyman. I knew that without having to think. I left the candle with him, for company. I ran.

In the hall I all but knocked down my cousin Imogen. She was making for the stairs with a candle, quickly, and seemed hardly to see me. She had a key in her hand, an old one, and she went up without a word and turned along the gallery. I had no time for her. I was looking for someone else.

I got her on the next flight. That's twice, on stairs. She had come out of the drawing room and was going up, quickly, with a candlestick, on her own. She had left my great-uncle, which I gathered afterwards she was not supposed to do. She had her head a little on one side, like a person listening to something on the floor above. I came up the flight behind her three steps at a time.

"Miss Quaile! Miss Quaile! Thank God. You're a nurse."

She turned and looked down at me. On a staircase she was the taller, which I think suited us both.

"I think I've killed the vicar," I said.

I will always honour her for the next four seconds. She did not say "What?" She did not say "Don't be absurd." She looked into my face, and whatever she found there, it satisfied her that I was a person to whom such a thing could happen.

"Where?" she said.

"The library."

"What with?"

"Brandy."

She came down past me. She did not run; she never ran. But I had to stretch to keep up.

She set her candle on the mantelpiece and went down on one knee by the armchair. I stood and held my hair. She took his wrist between her fingers and thumb and looked at nothing for a quarter of a minute. She laid the back of her hand against his cheek. She put her thumb on his eyelid and pushed it up, and brought the candle close, and watched. She bent and put her face right down to his open mouth, and I thought for one dreadful moment that she was going to give him the kiss of life, and felt a pang of envy. I'm ashamed of that now. But she was only smelling.

She sat back on her heel.

"He's asleep," she said.

"Asleep? He can't be. Look at him. I shook him. I shook him like a cocktail."

"He's very deeply asleep. His pulse is slow and quite steady and he's breathing, eight or nine to the minute. Look at his waistcoat." I looked. The fourth button was going up and down, about as often as the tide. "He'll not wake till morning. You could fire a gun by his ear." She was frowning, a small straight line between the pale eyebrows. "What has he had to drink?"

"Soup. Claret. Port. I mean, we all did. And then a brandy. Two brandies."

"Where are the glasses?"

"He left them. No, wait." I looked. The old boy had brought the second one with him. It was on the floor by the leg of the chair, on its side. I picked it up and gave it to her.

She put her nose into it.

I was watching her face, for the simple reason that there was nothing else in the room I wished to look at. I saw it change. It didn't change much. She was not a person whose face did. But all the colour that she had, which was none to speak of, went out of it, and her eyes went wide and then narrow. She smelled the glass again. Then she touched the tip of one finger to the dregs in the bottom and put it to her tongue.

"Where did this come from?" she said. She said it very quietly.

"The dining room. Off the sideboard."

"Who poured it?"

"I don't know. It was just there. It was Edmund's. He'd had about half and said it was corked, and left it, and the vicar didn't like to see it go to waste."

"Mr Edmund Vane's glass."

"Yes."

"He drank from this? Himself? You saw him?"

"About half. He made a face." I didn't care for the way she was looking at the glass. "Why? What is it? Is it poison? I *knew* it was. I said brandy can't be corked."

"It's not poison," Miss Quaile said. She said it slowly, as if she were still deciding. "It's a sleeping draught. Something I give. One spoonful in a tumbler of water, at night, for an old gentleman who won't stay in his bed." She looked up at me. She had on her face a look that I couldn't read at all then. I can read it now, and I wish I could not. "It's kept on a tray. In a room with the door shut. There's no way on earth that it should be in a brandy glass on a dining-room sideboard."

"Then how—"

"I don't know."

She stood up. She was still holding the glass. She looked at the vicar, and at the door, and at the ceiling, where there was nothing to see. I thought she was listening again.

"How long ago did Mr Vane drink it?"

"Twenty minutes. Half an hour."

"And where is he now?"

"He went to see a man about a boat."

"On his feet?"

"Well, yes. He limps a bit."

"He's a big man," she said, to herself. "And he's had a great deal of drink, for years, which makes a difference to the liver." She put the glass down on the mantelpiece, beside her candle. I noticed that her hand was not quite steady. "Mr Pargeter. Listen to me, please. The vicar will come to no harm. He's had perhaps half a dose, in spirits, on a full stomach, and at his age he'll sleep twelve hours and wake with a headache. But he mustn't be left sitting up like that with his head back. He could choke. He must be laid down, on his side, somewhere flat, with something under his head, and somebody should look at him every half hour. Can you do that?"

"Yes. Yes, of course. On his side."

"The left."

"The left. Where are you going?"

"I have to look at a tray," said Miss Quaile.

She took her candle. At the door she turned. She was a small pale girl in a dark dress, in a doorway, with a hundred thousand books behind her going up into the dark. I could have looked at her for a week.

"You didn't kill him," she said. "You fetched me. That was right. Most people stand and wring their hands." Then she was gone. I heard her on the stairs, going up, and this time I think she was very nearly running.

I stood on my uncle's hearthrug. I had been told I was right, by her, about something. I looked at the vicar with real affection. I would not have changed places with the Aga Khan.

* * *

The reader who has never tried to move a sleeping clergyman will think that the next part is easy.

He was a small man, and he looked light. I got my arms under his and heaved. It was like trying to lift a sack of wet sand that somebody has taken the trouble to butter. He came up about six inches, and his head fell forward on my shirt front, and his teeth came out. They were on a plate. They dropped into my breast pocket, on top of the handkerchief, and smiled at me.

I let him down again. I went for help. The hall was empty, but through the baize door I could hear voices, and I went down the kitchen stairs. I found the girl Gail sitting on the bottom step, with her shoes off, eating a goose leg.

"Gail," I said. "Would you be very kind and help me carry the vicar?"

She looked at me over the leg.

"Where to?" she said.

It was the same sort of question the nurse had asked. I have noticed this about the women of Essex. They don't waste time on the why.

"Somewhere flat. He's asleep. Miss Quaile says he's to be put on his left side with something under his head."

"Is he drunk?"

"He's been drugged."

"Oh," said Gail. She put the goose leg down on the stair and wiped her hands on her apron. "I thought it'd be something. My auntie says there's always something, on the night. Last year Mr Edmund was in the vicar's car, asleep, with the engine going. Year before, Dame Octavia shot the weathercock."

We carried him between us. I took the top half and Gail the legs. She was a strong girl, used to geese. She said he was heavier than last year's bird and not so well trussed. I led, going backwards, which as I've said is my natural gait, and we got him out of the library, with only one pause, when his foot caught the door.

"Where'll we put him?" Gail panted. "Upstairs?"

"I'm not carrying him upstairs."

"There's the settle. In the hall. It's long enough if you bend him."

So we laid the Reverend Aubrey Stiles on an oak settle in the hall of Compline House, on his left side, facing the fire. Gail fetched a cushion from the drawing room and put it under his head. I restored his teeth. I didn't know how to put them in, so I set them on the arm of the settle by his hand, where he would find them. He looked very peaceful. He looked like a figure on a tomb.

"He's nice, the vicar," said Gail, standing back. "He christened me. He thought I was a boy."

It was about ten to twelve. The hall was all firelight and ticking. As we stood there I happened to look across at the tall dark clock on the left of the fireplace, the discreet one, which I'd had my arm in an hour and a half before.

It had stopped. The pendulum was hanging straight down behind the glass.

I thought nothing of it. Clocks do stop. I may even have thought, with a twinge, that I had done it myself, fumbling about in there. I didn't open the door. If I had, I should have been able to tell you whether the weight was on its line or not, and it seems that somebody would have liked very much to know. But there it is. I looked at a stopped clock at ten to twelve and thought, I hope that's not another one.

Gail went back to her goose leg. I set out, once more, to look for Edmund. It seemed to me that he ought to be told he'd had a sleeping draught, before he tried to do anything on a boat.

* * *

I didn't find him. I should have had to go out, and it was pouring. I looked into every room on the ground floor again and met nobody at all.

I ended, as one does in a strange house, in the kitchen.

It was the only warm place. The range was going, and Mrs Pask was at the table in her apron, setting out little glass plates. She was counting under her breath. There was a clock on the wall, a big round one such as they have in railway stations. I looked at it, having no Mickey. I give it as I saw it: thirteen minutes past twelve.

"Mr Toby," said Mrs Pask, not looking up. "You may make yourself useful. Them glasses on the tray want to go up to the serving pantry for one o'clock, for the champagne, and Gail's got her feet up, and I've only the one pair of hands. You'll find the lift through there. In the larder, at the back. You put the tray in, and haul on the left-hand rope, and it stops itself."

I was glad to be of use to someone. I took the tray, two dozen glasses on a butler's salver, and carried it through a stone passage into a pantry. There were shelves, and a ham. At the back there was a square hatch in the wall with a wooden door. I balanced the tray on my hip and opened it.

There was a shaft, and two ropes hanging. There was no lift. The box part, the thing one puts the tray in, was somewhere up above. I could see the bottom of it, a little way up the shaft, in the light of my candle.

I took hold of the left-hand rope and pulled. Nothing happened. I pulled the right-hand rope. Something creaked. The box came down about a foot, and stuck.

And a voice said, "Hello?"

It was a small voice, quite clear and rather polite. It came from inside the shaft, just above my head.

I stood perfectly still.

"Hello?" it said again. "Is somebody there? Please could you pull the other rope? It's gone crooked. I've been in here for ages."

I want to describe my state of mind exactly. I wasn't frightened. One is frightened by things that may be so. This was simply not so. I had in the course of that evening drunk two whiskies, a sherry, a great deal of the Léoville, some port, and two brandies. One of the brandies I now had some reason to think had stood next to a narcotic. I had killed a vicar and seen him raised. I was in love. A man in that condition, when the furniture speaks to him, does not ask what it wants.

"I'm very sorry," I said to the hatch, quite courteously. "I can't help you. You aren't there."

"I am, though," said the voice.

I shut the door of the hatch. I shut it carefully and put the catch across. I carried the tray of glasses back along the stone passage into the kitchen, and set it down on the table. I didn't break a single one.

"That was quick," said Mrs Pask.

"Mrs Pask," I said. "I wonder if I might ask you something. Does the lift generally speak?"

She had a glass plate in each hand. She put them down.

I shall not forget her face. I had expected one of two things. Either she would laugh, or she would tell me I had had enough. She did neither. She was a square, solid woman of sixty-odd with a face like a cottage loaf, a woman you would have said nothing on earth could startle. And she looked, for one moment, like a person who has been found out.

"What did it say?" said Mrs Pask.

"It said hello. It asked me to pull the other rope. It said it had been in there for ages."

"Did it now." She wiped her hands down her apron, slowly, and I could see her getting her face back, the way one gets a tablecloth straight. "You've had enough, Mr Toby. That's what that is. That's Mr Vane's claret, and the wind in the shaft. That old lift's done that since I was a girl. It moans. You leave my lift be, and I'll see to the glasses myself."

"It didn't moan. It was very well-spoken."

"I'll see to it," she said.

She took a candle and went into the stone passage, and I heard the pantry door shut behind her. Then I heard the bolt.

I sat down at the kitchen table. I helped myself to a piece of cold goose. Through the wall I could hear Mrs Pask in the pantry. She was talking, in a sharp fierce whisper, the kind one uses to a dog that has got on the bed. I caught a word or two. "Told you." "Not a sound, I said." "Wait till I get you home."

It struck me as carrying things rather far, to speak to a dumb waiter like that. I assumed she was humouring me. Country people are full of these old customs.

There was a rumbling in the wall, of ropes. Then silence. She came back, rather red, with her cap crooked, and the tray of glasses was gone.

"There," she said. "All it wanted was a firm hand." She looked up at the clock on the wall, and then at me, and I saw her come to some decision. I think she wanted me out of that kitchen. "Now then. If you've nothing to do but sit there eating my stock, you can run me an errand."

"Anything," I said. "I'm at a loose end. I can't find Edmund."

"Nobody can ever find Mr Edmund, not on the night. He'll turn up; he always does." She took a ring of keys off a nail by the range and began to work one of them round to the front. It had a twist of red wool on it. "It's the ice."

"The ice?"

"The pudding, for the Hour. Mrs Vane's bombe; her that's gone. I've made it every year since 1952, and they have it at one o'clock with the champagne, when the door's opened, and Mr Vane says the same thing every year. He says, 'Ah, Mrs Pask, the ice.' It was in my freezer. And then the electric went. So I had Gail run it out to the ice house, in its box, and bed it down in the ice, where it'll take no harm. And now it wants fetching, and she's got her shoes off, and I can't leave my kitchen."

It was twenty-five past twelve by the kitchen clock when Mrs Pask sent me for the bombe. I looked at the clock as she said it. I have been asked the time of that more often than I have been asked my own age.

"Where's the ice house?"

"Out the back door, across the yard, down the track behind the stables. In the trees. You can't miss it. It's a hump." She put the keys into my hand. "That's the gate. Red wool. It'll likely be on the latch, but if it's shut, it's locked, and you'll want that. Mind them steps, they're greasy, and there's no rail. The box is at the bottom. Wooden. So big." She showed me with her hands. "Heavy, mind, with the ice round it. Here."

She gave me a white saucer with a candle stuck on it in its own wax. She thought, and went to a drawer, and gave me a silver spoon as well. It was a big one, with a crest on the handle.

"What's this for?"

"You lift the lid and you put that in at the edge. Just the edge. If it goes in like butter, it's gone soft, and you come straight back and tell me, and I'll serve them the trifle. If it stands up, bring it." She fixed me with her eye. "Don't taste it."

"I shouldn't dream of it."

"You've the look of one that would."

I went out of the back door into the wind, with a ring of keys, a candle in a saucer, and a spoon. I felt rather like an acolyte going up to the altar. And so I came to my cousin Edmund after all. I had been looking for him for two hours, and was no longer thinking about him at all.

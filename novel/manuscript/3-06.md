## VI. The Tower

The front door of Compline faces east. From the step one looks across eighty yards of gravel to the stable arch, and above the arch the tower, with the clock face and the little roof.

Mr Vane drew the bolt. He took the two leaves of the door in his hands and folded them back against the wall. I stood at his left elbow, as I had promised. The night came in upon us, cold and wet, and the nearest candles lay over and went out.

The rain had stopped and the wind had dropped to nothing. There was a moon. It was low and white, behind a thin cloud, and it lit the gravel like frost. Everything was very clear and still. I could see each stone of the stable arch.

"Come in," the old man said, to the dark. "Come in, then. You're late."

I was not listening to him. I was looking at the tower.

There was a light on it. It was at the top, on the leads, under the roof where the figures stand. It was a lantern, and someone was holding it; it swung from side to side, slowly, in an arc, as a man swings a lamp to signal. It lit the underside of the tiles and the bell. It lit the grey woman on the right hand, all down one side, so that I saw her painted gown and her lifted arm.

And it lit a man.

He was standing to the left of the bell, where the small figure stands that strikes the quarters. I saw a white shirt-front and a black coat. I saw the light slide over a white cuff. He was in evening dress.

I knew that the Jack had been dressed for the night. I had been told so at tea; the young people had gone across with an old dinner jacket of Mr Vane's and a shawl. I knew therefore that there was a wooden figure on that tower in a black coat. I have never pretended that I did not know it.

But a wooden figure does not hold a lantern, and it does not swing one. There was something up there that moved. And as I stared, I saw that the black and white was not one shape but more than one. It shifted. A part of it came forward into the light and another part stayed back. I could not separate them. I can say only that on the left side of that bell, where for two hundred years there had been one small man of oak, there was that night more than he.

Behind me in the hall the last clock finished. There was a silence.

The tower clock began to strike. First came the four quarters on the small bell. I saw an arm rise and fall beside the lantern, four times, stiffly, and the light jerked with it.

Then the grey woman moved.

I had never seen her strike. I had always been in a room, or with my back to that door. Her whole arm came down from the shoulder, slowly, as a person brings down an axe, and the hammer met the great bell, and the note rolled out across the yard and over my head into the house.

It was one o'clock.

On that stroke, in the same instant, there was a flash of red in the darkness under the tower, down in the yard, and a noise like a door slammed in my face. And another.

The lantern leapt. It went up, and sideways, and fell. It fell straight down the front of the tower past the clock face, turning, and went out.

I saw the man go. The lantern was still in the air, and gave me its last light, and by it I saw the man on the left of the bell go backward. He did not crumple. He went all of a piece, slowly, with his arms out from his sides, like someone falling back into deep water. For a moment I saw the white of his shirt, tilted up toward the roof. Then he had gone over the far edge of the leads and there was nothing there.

On the first stroke of one a man in evening dress fell from the clock tower, and the grey woman beside him had her arm raised.

It was raised again. I saw that last, by the moon, when the lantern was out. She had struck, and her arm had gone back up to her shoulder, as it does, to wait for the next hour. She stood alone under the roof with the hammer lifted. She was not looking at the place where he had been. She was looking, as she always looked, across the yard at the house. She was looking at the open door, and at the old man, and at me.

I cried out. I did not know that I was going to. I had both hands at my mouth.

Mr Vane had not started. He stood in his doorway with his arms a little out, as he had held the doors, and his face was lifted to the tower, and he was quite calm.

"Ah," he said. "Yes. I thought there would be something to pay."

* * *

I should have run out. I know it. I had resolved five minutes before that as soon as the door was open I would take a lamp and go. I had now seen a man fall forty feet. I stood on the step and could not move.

It was his hand. He had taken hold of my wrist, without looking at me, and he held it down at his side as one holds a child at a kerb.

"Hush," he said, very low. "Don't tell them. They'd not understand the accounts."

"Mr Vane, there was a man—"

"I saw him. It is the debit. An hour is given back, and something is taken for it; I ought to have foreseen that. One cannot have an entry on one side only." He was nodding. "It is all right, my dear. It is all right now. I have it. I felt it come in at the door. Did you not feel it?"

"Somebody has fallen from the tower!"

I turned round to say it to the hall. I believe that I did say it. But my voice had gone to a thread, and they were all behind us, in the candlelight, and all talking, and not one of them had seen. I looked from face to face. Mr Fenn was saying that it was thunder. Dame Octavia said in her great voice that it was a twelve-bore and that somebody was after the duck. The cropped girl on the stairs said something, and there was a laugh. They had heard two bangs in the night and had explained them to one another before the echo died.

I stood with my back to the night and looked at them. And for a moment I felt the whole thing turn over, as one feels a boat turn.

I thought of the Jack. He was a wooden figure in an old dinner jacket, two hundred years on an iron pin, on the left side of that bell. Somebody below had fired a gun. I had seen black and white in a swinging light, at eighty yards, at night, and I had seen it fall. I had known all along that he was there.

I thought: that is what it was. Thank God. That is all it was.

But the lantern. Someone had held the lantern. A thing of oak does not swing a light to and fro.

I had my mouth open to say so. And through the baize door at the back of the hall, at that moment, the tall young man came in backwards, carrying a box, and said in a loud glad voice:

"Uncle Leo! The ice cometh!"

* * *

It was a wooden box with tin corners. He carried it the length of the hall and set it on the table and cut the cord. There was a grey blanket. He lifted something out of a pillowcase, with a flourish, and said a word in French.

It was a brass clock with a painted moon.

*He had a box in his arms, and in the box was her clock. He had it in a blanket, like something he was going to drown.*

The old man had said that to me an hour ago, by the stopped regulator, before he fainted. He had said that he struck his son, and that the son went backward down the steps with the box. I had half believed and half hoped. I had told myself that he lost pieces of the world but did not make new ones.

And here was the box, and the blanket. The young man said that it had been at the bottom of the steps.

It was true, then. Everything he had told me in the hall was true. Edmund had been in the ice house with that clock in his arms, and his father had come upon him there. And that box had been at the bottom of the steps because it had gone down them with a man.

I looked at Mrs Edmund Vane. She was standing under the tall clock with her hands folded. She was looking at the thing on the table. She did not look surprised. She looked as I have seen relatives look in a hospital corridor, when the doctor comes out and they can tell from the way he shuts the door.

Mr Vane had gone down the hall to it. He was bending over the clock with his glass. He said, "Ah. The Copy. I wondered where I had put it." He remembered nothing. He was pleased to see it. He thanked the young man, and said that we had been promised an ice.

I caught the young man by the sleeve as he went past me with the empty box.

"Mr Pargeter. Where is Mr Edmund?"

He went a deep red. He looked at the box, and at the floor, and at a spot above my head.

"I'm afraid I couldn't say," he said. "I promised." And then, in a rush: "He's all right, though. Honestly. I'll tell you presently. I can't now, I've got to put this away."

He went through the baize door. I stood where he had left me.

*He's all right.* He had been to the ice house. He had brought the box from the bottom of the steps. If there had been a dead man lying beside it he would not have come into the hall making a joke about ice. So Edmund had not been there. Or he had been there, and was not dead, and had got up.

Then where was he?

I looked out through the open door, at the tower. The grey woman stood alone under the roof.

* * *

I shall pass quickly over the next quarter of an hour. I was held in that hall by an old man's hand and by my own promise, and I was thinking of one thing only. But I must say what Mr Vane said, since I have heard that it was reported afterward, and that some people thought it showed his mind was gone. I thought it was the sanest thing I heard in that house.

He sat down in the chair by the door, with the rug over his knees, and I stood by him. He told his family that he had asked a question at dinner and no longer wanted the answer. He said that a clock was for telling a man how much time he had got, and that he owned a hundred and eleven, and they had only ever told him how much he had had. He said that if anything had been taken from him, it had no doubt been wanted more than he wanted it, and that he had been a father to whom a person could not come and ask. He would put it all on the Hour's account.

Then he called Mr Fenn to him and dictated a paper. I did not attend to it. I caught the name of the farm across the water. I heard him say, "I can't pay it to the man. I let him die without. I'll pay it to his name." I did not know what was owed, or to whom. I understood only that he was settling something, and that he did it like a man going through his pockets before a journey.

When that was done he shut his eyes, and I thought that I might go. I had drawn my wrist very gently out of his fingers. I had taken one step from the chair.

"Kit," he said, without opening them. "Wind the gramophone. I should like the Lehár."

The boy went to the corner. There was the sound of a handle turning, and a hiss, and then music, a slow waltz with violins. It was old, and sweet, and it filled the hall, and under it one could still hear the clocks.

I heard the girl on the stairs say her lover's name, in a gasp. I looked up.

She was coming down the main staircase.

She had one hand on the rail. She came a step at a time, in the candlelight, with the grey silk moving round her and her shoulders bare and her dark head up. She had put her shoes on. Her face was white, and wet, and there was a smile on it, a small steady one, as of someone who has made up her mind to be brave about a thing that is going to hurt.

I had seen her go down the back stair to the garden an hour before. I had stood in her cold room and looked at a black dress on the counterpane. I had told myself everything that a sensible woman tells herself. I had it all arranged in my head, the key and the hanger and the white hair in the brush. I knew as well as I knew my own name that the person on those stairs was Miss Imogen Vane, in a borrowed gown.

I am ashamed to say that it made no difference. My knees gave. I went back against the wall beside the door, and slid down it, and sat on the stone floor with my hands pressed flat on either side of me.

I saw Dame Octavia Prowse get up out of her chair, without help, which she had not done all night. I heard her say, "Oh, Daisy." I saw the boy at the gramophone with his hand out, and his mouth moving. I saw Mr Fenn bow his head, as a man does in church. Those were people who had known Marguerite Vane alive. They had dined with her, and quarrelled with her, and followed her coffin. They looked up at that staircase, and I saw in their faces that they did not doubt who was coming down.

Mr Vane had risen. He stood by his chair. The rug had fallen.

"You came back," he said. "Margot. You came back."

She crossed the floor to him. She put out her hand.

"Yes, Papa," she said. Then, lower, and I think that only he and I were near enough to hear it, "Yes, Leo. I came back."

They danced.

I have been asked by Dr Imrie what I felt, sitting on that floor. I have told him that I do not know the word. It was not fear, by then. The fear had been in the gallery. This was a thing that I had no right to be present at.

He held her as if she were made of glass. He had his eyes shut and his face lifted. He, who an hour before had lain on that same floor with his lips blue, led her round his hall, among his clocks, without a fault. And he talked to her. I heard some of it, each time they came by the door. He was telling her the tide.

"High water was at eight minutes to one," he said. "I have it from the Thorne. The Wade will not carry you on foot before half past four. But a horse may go at twenty minutes to three, and not a minute before, and he must swim at the Rill. Do you hear me, Margot? I had it wrong for you. The last time. I put the hall clock back and I never told you. I've got it right now. I have had twenty years to get it right."

"I hear you, Leo."

"You'll not go on foot."

"No."

"You'll wait for the horse."

"I'll wait."

"Then you will get across," he said. "You'll get across, my dear. That is all I wanted. I don't ask you to stay."

Her face was over his shoulder. I saw it when he said that. I saw what it did. I do not think that Miss Imogen Vane, by herself, could have looked like that at her father. There were two women in that face, and one of them was hearing a thing that she had waited twenty years to hear.

The record ended. The needle ran on in the groove. He stood still, holding her.

"There," he said. "There, my dear. You see. It's quite all right. We had the time after all."

She kissed him on the forehead. She took his face between her hands for a moment and looked at it. Then she turned, and went away quickly toward the garden passage. As she passed the place where I sat she looked down at me.

They were grey eyes, and they were full of tears. She knew me. She gave me a little nod, such as one gives to a nurse in a sickroom on the way out, that means *I leave him with you.*

I do not know to this day which of them it was that nodded.

* * *

I got up. I took the old man to his chair. He went like a child that has been promised something and been given it. He was smiling, and his eyes were closing.

I stood beside him and I looked through the door at the tower. There was nothing there. I knew what I had to do, and I was waiting only until he should be asleep.

It was then that the young man came across the hall to me. I have read what he has written of it, since. He was kind enough to send me the pages. He has put down what he said and what I answered, and I cannot find that he has changed a word. I shall therefore not go through it again. I shall say only what it was like from where I stood.

He told me that Edmund had been found at the bottom of the ice-house steps, drugged, with a wound on his head. He said that he and the cook had carried him to the stables and put him in the end loose box, under the tower, and that he had been left there asleep, at a quarter to one.

"Asleep," I said. "You left him asleep."

"Snoring like a grampus."

I looked at the tower. I had the whole of it then. It came together in my mind as quickly as a thing falls.

His father had struck him at ten minutes to twelve. He had lain at the bottom of those steps for half an hour. He had been lifted out, alive, and carried to the stable, and laid in straw at the foot of the tower stair. That was at a quarter to one.

And at one o'clock, fifteen minutes later, I had seen someone at the top of that tower with a lantern. I had seen a man in a black coat go backward over the edge.

"Then he woke," I said. "He woke, and he went up."

The young man said that it was impossible, with so much of the drug in him. He reminded me that I had said the vicar would be out for twelve hours. He was quite right. I had said so. It is what the dose would do, in an old man of nine stone.

I had sat by Edmund Vane at dinner. I had watched him drink for three weeks. I knew what he could carry, and how he could stand upright and speak clearly when another man would be on the floor. I thought: he woke in the dark, in the straw, with his head broken and that stuff in his blood. He did not know where he was or how he had come there. He saw the stair and climbed it, as he had climbed it when he was a boy and was unhappy. The girl had said so; I had heard her. *He goes up that tower when he's upset.* He took the lantern that hung in the harness room. He came out upon the leads, beside the grey woman, at the moment that she struck.

Dame Octavia had said that it was the Jack that fell. Perhaps the Jack did fall. I have told you that there was more than one shape on that side of the bell. But the Jack was not the only man in evening dress on that tower, and one of them had held the light.

The young man was still speaking. I heard him as one hears a person through a wall. He was saying that he would look after me. He said that whatever had happened that night, the glass, and the tray, and what I had seen, I need never say a word of it to anyone. He would say nothing himself. He asked me whether I would.

I looked at him. He had a round, anxious, honest face, and his hands were going. He had carried a hurt man up twelve steps because he thought it right. He had fetched me to the vicar. I thought that he was the only one in that house, that night, who had done nothing but try to help.

And he was offering me silence. I thought that was what he offered. The tray had been in my charge. The old man had been in my charge. I had failed in both, and a man was dead of it, and here was someone who knew a part and guessed more, telling me that it should go no further.

I said yes.

I have been told that he meant something else. He told me so himself, in a tea shop, with a ring in a box, and I have never been so sorry for anything. I had not heard what he asked. I had heard only that there was a way to stop thinking, and I took it.

* * *

Mr Vane woke. He spoke to the young man, and wrote something for him, and got up. He said that there was a clock stopped, and went across the hall to the regulator and opened the long door.

I followed a few steps. The heavy man from the insurers had come in from the back. He was wet to the thighs and there was blood dried on his face. He went and stood by Mr Vane, and they spoke together in low voices. I saw the old man touch the weight with his fingers. I saw him turn his wrist over and look at his cuff.

The young man said something to me about a clock that he had trodden on. I did not understand him.

When I looked again, Mr Vane had gone. The front door stood open. The heavy man was by himself in front of the regulator.

I went to him. I asked whether he had seen Mr Vane. He said that he had been there a moment since, looking at the clock.

I looked at the clock. The case was open. The pendulum hung straight. At the bottom of its line was the weight that I had lifted onto its hook two hours before.

There were fifty candles in the hall now. When I had held that thing in my hands I had had one lamp, standing on the floor behind me. I had felt that it was wet, and wiped my fingers.

It was not wet now. It had dried. Along one side of the brass, near the lower rim, there was a smear, as long as my finger, dull and dark, like varnish. In the smear there were hairs. There were four or five, stuck fast. They were short and dark, and one was grey.

I do not know what was in my face. The heavy man was watching it. He was the kind who does.

I shut the door of the case. I pressed it until the catch took. I said, "Thank you," and I walked away from him to the stairs.

I went up to my room and put on my outdoor shoes and my cloak. I took the lamp. I had finished with promises. The old man had gone out of the house again, alone, and this time I knew where.

* * *

I crossed the yard at about twenty minutes to two.

The moon was higher and very bright. The tide had turned. I could hear it going, behind the stables, a long soft sucking sound. I did not go to the tower door first. I went round the end of the block, by the track, to the back, where the ground falls to the creek they call the fleet.

It was full, and it was running out. It was black, and quite smooth, and it slid along under the wall of the stables with a speed that made me giddy to look at. The tower stood straight up from its edge. I held my lamp out over the water. I looked at the foot of the wall, and up to the leads, forty feet above. I looked down the length of the fleet to the brick arch where it goes out under the sea wall.

There was nothing in it. There was a little straw, turning, and some foam.

Anything that had gone into that water at one o'clock would have been carried out through that arch long since, and into the sea.

I stood on the bank for I do not know how long. Then I went back round to the yard.

The end loose box had its half-door open. I went in with the lamp. There was deep straw, and in the straw a hollow, such as I had found an hour and a half before in a child's bed. It was a big one. There was a horse rug thrown back from it, and another rolled up for a pillow. On the rolled one there was a stain.

I put my hand in the hollow. It was cold.

So he had lain there, and he had got up. The young man had told the truth.

The door of the harness room was beside it. I opened it. There was a smell of paraffin and spirits, and a broken lamp on the floor. The ladder-stair went up in the corner into the dark. Overhead I could hear the great clock, slow and heavy.

And I could hear voices.

They came down through the trap from the clock chamber. There were two. One was Mr Vane's. I knew it at once, thin and exact. I could not hear what he said. The other was lower. It was a man's voice, deep, and very tired, and it spoke for a long time together without stopping, as a person speaks who is telling something from the beginning.

I put my foot on the stair. I climbed four rungs. I stood with my head just beneath the level of the floor above. I could see nothing; there was no light up there at all.

The low voice had stopped. There was a silence. Then the old man spoke, and I heard him plainly.

"Then she didn't leave me," he said. "She was coming back."

The low voice said something short.

"She was coming back," said Mr Vane again. "Then she did not leave me."

He said it a third time, and a fourth. He said it as I have heard patients say a thing over when they have been given good news and dare not stop saying it in case it should cease to be true.

I should have gone up. I was four rungs from the top. I had only to climb, and lift the lamp.

I did not. I am going to try to tell the truth about why. I knew that voice. I had listened for it for three weeks. I had last heard it in the library that afternoon, saying, *Good girl.* And I had seen a man fall from the top of this tower forty minutes before, backward, with his arms out, into water that went to the sea. I had just stood on the bank and looked.

I thought: he is up there with his father. The old man has gone up to his machine, as he said he would. He has taken his hour back, and gone through it again, and he has found his son.

I thought that if I climbed the last four rungs and held up my lamp, I should see Mr Vane in that chamber, talking, and nobody else.

I had borne a good deal that night. I had found a warm bed with no child in it. I had seen a woman pass me in a gallery who had been twenty years in her grave. I could not have borne that as well.

So I called. I stayed where I was, and I called up through the trap.

"Mr Vane! Mr Vane, it's Hester Quaile. You must come down. You'll take your death."

The voices stopped. I waited. Nothing moved above. I could hear the pendulum.

Then I heard his thin shoes on the boards, and his legs came through the trap, and he climbed down to me, slowly, feeling for each rung. I went down ahead and held the lamp.

He reached the floor. He turned round. I have never seen a human face so happy.

"He is alive," he said. "I've been through it twice. It was all just as I calculated. I went to the ice house the second time and there was nobody there, and I came here, and he was sitting against the frame. I did not strike him. I put my hand on his face. He is quite warm."

"Who is up there, Mr Vane?"

"Edmund. I've told you. He has been telling me about his mother." He took my arm. He was trembling all over with joy and cold. "She did not leave me. She had gone, and she turned back. Did you know that? He was there. He saw. She turned back on the Wade."

I looked up at the black square of the trap.

"Mr Edmund," I said. I said it quite loud. "Mr Edmund, are you there?"

There was no answer. There was no light, and no step. I listened until my ears sang. I could hear the great clock cutting the time into pieces, and that was all.

I have gone over that silence a thousand times. If there was a living man in that chamber, why did he not speak? He had only to say, "Yes, Miss Quaile." He knew my voice. He had talked with me that afternoon as he had talked with no one for twenty years.

I have told myself that he was hurt, and had no strength. I have told myself that he did not wish to be seen. Mr Pargeter has written that he met him afterward in the kitchen, eating, and laughing. Miss Vane says in her letter that she spoke to him. I have never asked either of them to their face whether they were sure. I am afraid of how they would answer. I think that on that night more than one person in that house saw what they most wanted to see.

I only know that I stood at the foot of the stair and called his name into the dark, and that nothing answered me. I did not climb up to look.

The old man was pulling at my arm. His teeth were chattering. I took him out into the yard.

We had got halfway across the gravel when the clock struck over our heads. I stopped and looked up.

The grey woman brought her arm down. The great bell sounded. She raised her arm, and brought it down again.

It was two o'clock. There had been no quarters. The small bell had not rung. All that night, and every night for two hundred years, there had been four chimes before the hour. She had struck without them. There was nobody now on the left side of the bell to give them.

"The Hour is over," said Mr Vane. He sighed. "I should have liked it longer. But it was enough. One must not be greedy."

* * *

I come now to the thing that I did.

I did not take him in through the hall. I took him by the gunroom door and up the back stair, which is shorter, and put him to bed. There was no draught to give him. He did not need it. He lay down on his back, with his hands folded, and was asleep before I had drawn the curtains of the bed. He was smiling. I turned the key on him from outside and put it in my pocket.

Then I went down. It was about half past two.

The hall was nearly dark. The candles had burned low and most had gone out. One leaf of the front door had been shut. Dame Octavia was asleep in her chair with her mouth open, and the vicar on his settle. On the other settle, across the hearth, the tall young man lay with his knees drawn up and one shoe off, sleeping like a child. On the table the brass clock stood on its blanket.

Nobody stirred. I could have been the only person awake in that house.

I went to the regulator. I opened the long door.

I had no plan when I came down the stairs. I do not think that I had one when I put my hand on the catch. I looked at the weight, hanging there, with its dark smear and the hairs. I thought of the old man overhead, on his back, smiling. He had looked a hundred years old when I laid him down. He had looked like something that would break if it were lifted wrongly.

I thought of what would come. The sea gives things back. I am from the coast, and I know. In a day, or two, or three, there would be a man found on a beach, with a wound in his head. There would be a policeman in this hall. He would ask what had been done, and with what, and by whom. He would go round the house, and he would open this case. And he would see what I was looking at.

Then they would take the old man. They would not hang him; he was eighty-one and out of his mind. But they would take him out of this house and put him in a place with locked doors. They would ask him, every day, quite kindly, whether he remembered striking his son. And every day he would hear it new.

I had told him the truth for three weeks because he asked me to, and because I thought that a person has a right to it. I still think so. But I had learned something at twenty to one, kneeling on that floor. There are truths that a person has a right not to be told.

I took out my handkerchief. I wet it with my tongue, as my mother used to do for my face. I took the weight in my left hand, and I cleaned it.

It did not come off easily. I had to rub. I did it thoroughly, as I have been taught to do everything, turning the cylinder on its line, working round the rim and into the join where the brass meets the lead. I picked off each hair with my nails. When I had done, I held the lamp to it. It shone. It was a clean brass weight, such as might hang in any clock.

I went to the fire and put the handkerchief on the coals. It was a small linen one, with an H in the corner, that my mother had worked. I watched until there was nothing.

Then I came back and shut the case.

I have thought about that every day since. I told Mr Pargeter so, in the tea shop. I did not tell him what it was.

I had been a nurse, and I had resigned because I failed to watch. I had come to this house to sit awake by an old man and do no harm. And I ended that night by rubbing a man's blood and hair off a piece of brass with spit, so that nobody should ever know how he had come by his death. It was a man I had loved, in my fashion.

I did it for the father. I say that, and it is true. I have asked myself whether I did it also for myself, so that no one should ask where the nurse had been between half past eleven and ten past twelve, and I cannot swear that I did not.

I went up. I let myself into the Admiral's room and locked the door on the inside. He had not moved. I sat down in the low chair by the fire. I had the red book in my pocket, and a child's drawing, folded small.

I had promised to bring the book to the Shop after breakfast. He had said that he would stand beside me.

I sat and waited for it to be morning.

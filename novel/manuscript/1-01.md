## 1. The Wade

Lionel Fenn kept his office over a building society in Colchester High Street, up a flight of stairs that smelled of floor polish and other people's wills. He was sixty-seven and looked as if he'd been sixty-seven since the Coronation. He had a wing collar, near enough, and a desk you could have landed a helicopter on, and he sat behind it with his fingers together and looked at me the way a man looks at a plumber he has been told is good.

"You were recommended, Mr Cade," he said.

"That's nice. Who by?"

"Mr Rudge, at Rudge and Maitland. He said that you were discreet and that you had been a policeman."

"He's half right. I'm still discreet."

It didn't get a smile. I hadn't expected one. It was a Wednesday morning in the third week of October, there was rain on the window behind his head, and I had driven up from Leigh with a hangover and forty pounds in the world to hear about what I assumed would be a divorce. In my line it's usually a divorce. When it isn't a divorce it's a man on the sick with a bad back who's been seen on a roof. Either way you sit in a car.

Fenn opened a drawer and took out a letter and laid it on the blotter and turned it round with one finger so that I could read it. He didn't push it across. I had to lean.

It was written with a proper pen on thick paper in a hand like an engraving. No address at the top, only a word, *Compline*, and a date three weeks gone. It said:

*Dear Fenn, Someone is taking my clocks and leaving me my own copies in their place. I made the copies. I should know them. I have counted eleven and there will be more that I have not found, since whoever it is has the sense to take the ones I do not often open. I do not want the police, who will tramp, and I do not want it spoken of in the house. Find me a man who can look at people. I will pay him. I want to know which of them it is before the Hour. Yrs, L. Vane.*

Under that, in the same hand but hurried, a line that had been added afterwards: *I may not remember having written this. Act on it regardless.*

I read it twice. It's a habit. The second time you read the man instead of the words.

"Who's L. Vane?"

"Leopold Vane. You won't know the name unless you are interested in clocks."

"I own one."

"Mr Vane owns a hundred and eleven. He restored a good many of them himself and he made, in his working life, exact copies of some dozens of the finest, as studies, the way a painter will copy an Old Master. He is, or he was, quite possibly the best restorer of early English clockwork alive. He is eighty-one years of age."

"And what's the last line about?"

Fenn looked at the rain for a while. "His memory is not what it was," he said. "There are good days. That letter was written on a very good day. I telephoned him the following week to say that I was making enquiries on his behalf, and he asked me what enquiries, and was rather short with me."

"So I'd be working for a client who doesn't know he's hired me."

"You would be working for me, Mr Cade, on the instruction of a client who foresaw precisely that difficulty and provided for it in writing. I have known Leo Vane for forty years. If he says there are eleven copies standing where eleven clocks should be, I should be astonished to find there were ten."

"What are eleven clocks worth?"

"These eleven? I have had the schedule out." He had it out now, typed, with a paper clip. "Something over four hundred thousand pounds. Insured for rather less, the valuation being elderly."

I didn't whistle. I'd given that up along with the warrant card. But I looked at the schedule with more respect than I'd given the letter.

"*Which of them*," I said. "He says *which of them*. He's got a list."

"He has a family, which comes to the same thing." Fenn took off his glasses and polished them, and did the family for me while he did it, flat, like a man reading the weather for shipping. "The son, Edmund. Fifty-two. He has managed the estate since his father stopped troubling with it, by which I mean since about 1975. I should not say that he has managed it well. The son's wife, Celia. The grandson, Christopher, who is at one of the music colleges, and who has I believe a young woman. The daughter, Imogen, who lives in Edinburgh and comes down perhaps twice in a year. There is a cook who comes in from the village and a girl who helps her. And there is a young person, lately engaged, a sort of nurse, who sits up with Mr Vane at night, since he walks about."

"When was she engaged?"

"The first of October."

"After the letter."

"A few days after." He put the glasses back on. "I engaged her myself, Mr Cade. From an advertisement, with references. I think we may leave Miss Quaile out of it."

I've never left anybody out of it in my life, but I let him have that one.

"Any of them short of money?"

There was a pause that a less careful man wouldn't have left.

"I act for the family," Fenn said. "All of the family. You will appreciate that there are matters I am not at liberty to discuss with you, and that what I am asking you to find out I should a good deal prefer not to know."

"That's a yes."

"That is a statement of my position."

"All right." I sat back. The chair was the kind they give clients so they won't stay. "How do I get in? I don't see myself as a long-lost cousin."

"On Saturday they give a dinner. They give it every year on that night. It is a family occasion, somewhat peculiar, I shall explain it. There will be a few guests from outside; I shall be one. And as it happens the insurers have been pressing for some time for a fresh valuation of the collection. Pemberton's. I have told Edmund that their man is coming on Saturday to make a preliminary inspection, that it was the only day he could manage, and that as he must come over on the morning tide and cannot get back before the night one he has been asked to dine and stay. Edmund was not pleased. But he could hardly refuse the insurers."

"He might ring them."

"He might. I have a friend at Pemberton's."

I looked at him again. Wing collar and all. You never know with solicitors. Some of the best liars I ever met had their names on brass plates.

"I don't know anything about clocks, Mr Fenn."

"You are not required to. You are required to look as though you do, for one afternoon, with a notebook. I shall give you the insurers' photographs. Mr Vane's copies are extremely good, but he was a craftsman and not a forger, and he made no attempt to deceive. Each original has some mark of the maker's which the copy lacks. You will find a note of them on the back of each print." He slid a fat buff envelope across. This one he did push. "Your real business is the people."

"Tide," I said. "You said tide."

So he explained about the tide. Then he explained about the time, and I asked him to explain it again, slower.

"In 1974," Fenn said, "Mr Vane's wife was drowned on the causeway. It was on the night of this same dinner; the night, as it happens, that the clocks were put back for the winter. I shan't go into the circumstances. It was held to be an accident. She had misjudged the hour of the tide. Mr Vane took the view that she had been deceived as to the hour by the changing of the clocks, and he has not permitted a clock in that house to be changed since. Not forward in the spring. Not back in the autumn. Compline keeps Greenwich time the year round."

"So in the summer they're an hour out."

"In the summer, Mr Vane would say, the rest of the country is an hour out. From the end of March to the end of October, when it is one o'clock at Compline it is two o'clock here. You become accustomed. One learns to ask. And on the night the clocks go back, the difference ceases, and the house and the world agree again until the spring. That is what the dinner is for. At one o'clock in the morning by the house, which is two by your watch, Mr Vane opens his front door. To let the world back in, as he puts it."

"And then everyone goes to bed."

"Then," Fenn said, "there is an hour during which, by a custom of the late Mrs Vane's, the family are permitted to say anything they please to one another without consequences. It is called the Odd Hour. I have sat through nineteen of them. I do not recommend the experience." He squared the schedule on the blotter. "There is one further thing. You will be asked to give up your watch at the door. All the guests are. It is put into a basin in the hall and returned in the morning. You would be wise not to make difficulties."

"I never make difficulties."

"Mr Rudge said that you would say that."

He offered me a hundred and fifty a day and expenses. I'd have done it for eighty. I asked for two hundred, because it was that kind of desk, and he gave it to me without blinking, and I went down his stairs with a cheque for the first day and the envelope under my arm, thinking it was the softest thing I'd been handed in a year. A dinner. A bed. A rich old man's family, one of them with sticky fingers, and all I had to do was watch them eat.

I used to be good at knowing when a job was wrong. I'd lost that too.

* * *

Saturday came up grey and windy with a sky like a dirty sheet. I had the suit cleaned. I bought a notebook and a jeweller's loupe from a man in Southend who asked no questions because he never does. I spent the Friday evening with Fenn's photographs spread on the kitchen table and a bottle of Bell's to hold them down, learning eleven clocks I had never seen and the names of men three hundred years dead who had made them: Tompion, Knibb, Quare, East. On the back of each print somebody at the insurers had written in pencil where to look. *Signature on backplate, lower edge. Punch mark inside the door. Initials scratched under the seatboard.* It was like being given the answers to a crossword in a language you don't speak.

The twelfth photograph was different. It was bigger, and there were six views instead of two, and Fenn had put a slip of paper round it with a figure written on it. I looked at the figure and then I put my glass down and looked at it again.

One million two hundred thousand pounds. For a clock.

It didn't look like much. A brass thing about two feet high under a glass shade, with more dials on its face than seemed decent: a big one and five small, and a little painted moon. *The Thorne Sidereal*, the caption said. *Isaac Thorne, Colchester, 1771. Mean and sidereal time, equation, lunation, and the tide at Compline Bar. The only known example.* And on the back, in the pencil: *Maker's mark a sprig of thorn, scratched. (1) Rear of moon disc. (2) Underside of dial foot, left.*

It wasn't on Leo Vane's list of the eleven. I noticed that. I also noticed that a man who had lifted four hundred thousand pounds' worth of clocks a piece at a time, starting with the ones nobody opened, was a man working his way up to something. I'm slow, but I get there.

I drove out early, with the lights on. Fenn had been particular about it. The road to the place was open first thing and then not again till the evening, and Pemberton's man was to go over on the morning tide and make a day of it. It's forty miles from Leigh as the map has it and a good deal further than that in every other way. After Maldon the road gets narrow and then it gets lonely. The fields go flat and the hedges give up and the sky comes down to meet you, and there's nothing standing higher than a pylon between you and Holland. I had the Granada's heater on and the wipers going. Wind kept taking the car by the shoulder.

Thorney Staithe was a street, a church, a pub called the Plough and Sail, and a smell of mud. I didn't stop. Past the last house the road turned to concrete and ran out along the top of a sea wall, and from there I could see where I was going, and I pulled over and had a look at it, because I like to know the ways out of a place before I go in.

The tide was down, though it was on its way back. What I was looking at was about half a mile of nothing: grey-brown mud, shining, cut up by creeks, with birds picking over it. Across the nothing ran a road. It came down off the wall on a ramp and went out dead straight on a low bank of stone and shingle, with a line of poles either side like a guard of honour that had been stood there too long, and about halfway over there was a sort of dip, and a little flat bridge, and beside the bridge a thing I couldn't make out, a black box on legs, like a hide for shooting from.

At the far end of the road the ground rose about as high as a bus. There were trees on it, bent one way. In the trees there was a house, long, a lot of chimneys, and off to one side of it a tower with a clock face. That was the island. That was all of it. I've seen bigger car parks.

There was a board at the top of the ramp. White paint, black letters, done by somebody who took trouble.

VANE ESTATE. THE WADE. PRIVATE ROAD.
DO NOT CROSS WITHIN THREE HOURS OF HIGH WATER.
THE SEA COMES IN FASTER THAN YOU CAN WALK.

Under that was a frame with a glass front and a printed card in it, the week's tides, a column for each day. I got out to read it. The wind tried to shut the door on my leg. Saturday, it said. High water 12.27 p.m.; road closed from 8.40 a.m. until 4.12 p.m. Then high water 12.52 a.m.; road closed from 9.05 p.m. until 4.35 a.m.

I looked at my watch. It was twenty past nine. By the card that road had been shut for forty minutes. I looked at the road. It was lying there in plain view with nothing on it but a few puddles. Then I saw that there was a line along the bottom of the card, small, in red:

*All times are Greenwich Mean Time.*

I stood and did the sum. I did it with my lips moving. An hour on, all of it. I had twenty minutes, then, not forty less than none. And tonight it would shut at five past ten by any clock a sane person owned, and not open till gone half past five in the morning. I thought about how many people must have stood where I was standing in July, and read that card with a watch on their wrist, and thought they had an hour more than they did. I thought, somebody ought to put that in bigger letters. Then I remembered who the somebody was and what had happened to his wife.

There was a farm just there on the landward side, down off the wall: a low house, tarred barns, a yard. A man was standing in the yard with a horse. It was a big grey horse and he had one of its feet up between his knees and was doing something to it, and when my door banged he looked round without letting go. He was fifty yards off. I couldn't tell you his face. Lean, a hat, a canvas coat. He looked at me and at the car, and then at the island, and then back at me, and he took his time over each. I've been looked at by experts. That was one. Then he went back to the horse.

I filed him. I file everybody. Mostly it's a waste of the cabinet.

I got in and took the Granada down the ramp and out onto the Wade.

It's a queer thing, driving on the bottom of the sea. The road was wet and ribbed and had weed on it. Water stood either side in the low places, and the poles went by, each one with a rag of something green at about the height of my head, and it took me half the distance to work out that the green was a tidemark. The sea had come a good way in while I stood reading. It was in the creeks on both sides now, creeping. At the dip the surface dropped away and the mud came up close, glistening. The little bridge was stone piers with planks across, no rail. The creek under it was only a few yards wide but it was cut deep and the water in it was moving fast, inland, with bits of foam on it. And the box on legs was a box on legs. Tarred timber, a ladder up one post, a door. I knew what it was without anyone telling me. It was where you went if you had read the card wrong.

Somebody should have looked at that ladder. I'm not a carpenter, and I could see daylight through the bottom rungs from a moving car.

* * *

The woman who opened the door to me was not the cook.

She was forty-something and she'd decided some time ago that it was nobody's business how much. Fair hair, the colour that's called ash on the bottle, cut to the jaw. Slacks, a man's cashmere jersey with the sleeves pushed up, no jewellery except a wedding ring she turned with her thumb. A good face that had been a beautiful one and knew to the day when the change had happened. She had the sort of grey-green eyes that seem to be thinking about something a little way behind you.

"You're the clock man," she said. She had a low voice, dry. A voice that had been taught.

"Cade. From Pemberton's."

"Celia Vane. We're all at sixes and sevens, I'm afraid, there's a goose. Come in out of that."

I came in out of that. She shut the door on the wind and the first thing I noticed was the noise.

I'd been ready for clocks. A hundred and eleven of them, Fenn had said. I had thought of it as a number. I had not thought what it would be to stand in a stone hall with a dozen of them round the walls, tall ones, in their cases like men in sentry boxes, every one going. It wasn't loud. That was the trouble with it. It was a sort of whisper from all sides, never quite together, a crowd muttering in another room. You felt talked about.

"You stop hearing it," Celia Vane said. "After about a year. Then one of them stops, and you wake up at three in the morning wondering what's wrong."

"Which one's right?"

"Oh, they're all right. That's the idea. They're all right, and it's the rest of us." She held out her hand, palm up. "I'm afraid I have to ask for your watch."

There was a table in the middle of the hall, and on it was a grey metal basin about the size of a washing-up bowl, old, with a rim that had been hammered. There were two watches in it already. One was a lady's and one was something flat and gold on a crocodile strap that had cost more than my car.

"Mr Fenn mentioned it."

"It's my father-in-law's rule. Compline time or none. You get it back at breakfast." The hand stayed out. "We call it the Font. You can make the obvious joke if you like, everybody does."

"I don't wear one," I said.

Her eyes came down to my left wrist. I had the cuff shot, but I'd worn that watch for eleven years and there's a mark. She looked at the mark. She looked at it for about as long as it takes to strike a match. Then she looked at my face, and something happened at the corner of her mouth that I wouldn't call a smile but that lived in the same street.

"How sensible," she said. "Neither does the goose."

She dropped her hand and turned and walked ahead of me across the hall, and that was the first conversation I had with Celia Vane. She'd caught me in a lie inside a minute and a half and put it in her pocket for later. I had a Tissot down my left sock, pressing on the ankle bone, and I could feel it ticking against me all the way across that floor, as if it wanted to be found.

I'd like to tell you I wasn't watching her walk. I told the truth to a disciplinary board once, and look where that got me, but I'll tell it here. I was watching her walk.

* * *

She took me down some stone steps at the back of the hall to what she called the Shop.

It had been a chapel once, or the cellar of one. The ceiling was arches, low, whitewashed, and under them ran forty feet of bench, with lathes on it small enough to put in your pocket and a rack of files in order of size, hundreds of them, and trays of wheels laid out on green baize like a jeweller's window. It was the cleanest room I had ever seen that wasn't in a hospital. There were more clocks down here, but most of them were in pieces. And at the far end, under the one good window, an old man sat on a stool with a brass plate in his left hand and a thing like a dentist's pick in his right, and didn't look up.

"Leo," she said. "The man from the insurance."

"Put him down somewhere."

"He isn't a parcel, darling."

"Then he may sit." He still hadn't looked up. "Not on the bench."

Celia Vane gave me a glance that said *all yours*, and went back up the steps. I heard her shoes on the stone and then I didn't. I found a chair with a seat made of string and I sat on it, as told, and I watched Leopold Vane work.

He was a long man folded small. White hair, a lot of it, standing up off a high forehead. A big bony nose with a pair of spectacles on it and a second lens, a watchmaker's glass, screwed into his right eye and hung on a black cord. An old cardigan with leather at the elbows. I've seen a good many men of eighty-one, mostly in the kind of trouble where somebody has taken their savings, and they all had the hands: the spots, the shake, the skin like tissue. He had the spots and the skin. He hadn't the shake. The pick in his fingers moved about a sixteenth of an inch at a time and stopped dead exactly where he put it.

It went on for four or five minutes. That's a long time to sit on string. Then he set the plate down on a square of chamois, let the glass drop out of his eye onto his chest, and turned round on the stool and looked at me. His eyes were pale blue and very clear and they went over me the way they'd gone over the brass.

"Pemberton's sent a boy last time," he said. "With a camera. He touched a dial with his thumb."

"I'll keep my hands in my pockets."

"You're not from Pemberton's."

I didn't say anything. In that position you don't. You let them tell you what they know.

"You have no glass," Leo Vane said. "A man who came to look at clocks would have a glass in his top pocket and the mark of it round his eye. You have a policeman's shoes. Fenn sent you. I wrote to Fenn." He stopped. Something moved behind the blue, like a fish under ice. "Did I write to Fenn?"

"You did, sir. Three weeks ago."

"What did I say?"

"That someone in this house is taking your clocks and leaving you your own copies."

"Yes," he said. "Yes. That is so. Eleven." He nodded slowly. He was pleased, and I saw what it was that pleased him. Not that I'd come. That his own letter had arrived, like a message a man leaves himself on the hall table. "I counted them in the summer. I wrote them down, and I hid the paper, and I've not the least idea where. It's a great nuisance, Mr—"

"Cade."

"A great nuisance, Mr Cade, to be robbed by somebody who need only wait. He knows I shall forget that I know. I suppose he's very patient. Or she."

"Do you know who it is, Mr Vane?"

"If I knew, I shouldn't want you."

"Do you have an idea?"

He picked up the brass plate again and turned it to the window. I thought he wasn't going to answer.

"I made forty-three copies in my life," he said. "It's how I learned. You don't understand how a man thought until you have tried to make the same cut with the same tool and found out why he held it as he did. I never passed one off. I never signed one with another man's name. A copy is not a lie, Mr Cade. It is a copy, until somebody stands it in the place of the original and says nothing. Then it's a lie, and it is his, and not mine. I resent that. I resent having my own work used to lie to me. I think that I mind that more than the money. You'll think that an old man's vanity."

"I think it's a reason. I've known people killed for less."

"Have you?" He was interested. "I suppose you have." Then: "It must be someone who knows which they are. That's the difficulty. I kept the copies in the long cupboard, there, and a ledger. It wants knowledge to match the one to the other and carry it up and put it in the case and set it to time so that I walk past it for a year. Anyone can steal a clock. To steal one from me, in my own house, that wants an education." His mouth went thin. "I gave the education to one person only."

"Your son."

"I had him at this bench from the time he was nine years old. He had better hands than mine. He had the best hands I ever saw on a boy." He put the plate down. "He stopped. When his mother died, he stopped, he never touched a tool again. I've never known why. I don't say it is Edmund. Do you understand me? I say it wants an education. Imogen has eyes, she restores pictures, she'd know a copy from across a room, and she could be taught the rest by a man who had the rest. Edmund's wife is a clever woman and is not happy. The boy is a musician, and has good fingers, and no money. There's a girl who sits with me at night; I can't at present recall her name, but she looks at everything." He spread the long hands, the first helpless thing I'd seen him do. "You see how it is. I don't want it to be any of them. So I'm of no use. I should convict the cat."

I got the notebook out and wrote some of it down, not because I needed it but because it steadies people. He watched the pencil.

"Fenn's letter said you wanted to know before the Hour," I said. "Why before?"

"Because of the rule. What's said in the Hour is not held against the one who says it. If I know, I can ask. In the Hour I can ask him to his face, and he may answer me, and it need go no further; and if he gives them back, it need never have happened." He looked at me with the clear eyes. "It was my wife's rule. She understood us. She said that a family is a clock with too many wheels, and that once in a year you must let it down and take the power off, or something will break. She used to say that the hour was given to us, and that it was bad manners not to use a present."

"She sounds like a sensible woman."

"She is in the long gallery," Leo Vane said. "You should go and look at her. In the grey. She'll be down directly; she's dressing." He frowned, and the fish went under the ice again, and when he looked at me next the eyes were still clear but they had nothing behind them that knew me.

"Are you the man about the Dent?" he said. "It isn't ready. You may tell them it isn't ready. I'll not be hurried over a remontoire by a railway company."

I said I'd tell them. I put the notebook away. At the foot of the steps I looked back, and he had the glass in his eye again and the pick in his hand, and it was as steady as a surveyor's mark. Whatever had gone out of the rest of him, it hadn't gone out of the hands. I've thought about that since. I'd been given a description that afternoon of the hands that had robbed him, by the man who trained them. I wrote it down in my book and then I spent the night looking at everything else.

* * *

I did what I was paid to pretend to do. I went round the house with my photographs.

It took most of the day and I'll spare you most of it. Somebody sent me up a plate of cold beef at one. There were clocks in every room and some rooms that seemed to be there only to hold them. I'd open a case, feeling like a man going through a stranger's coat, and find the place the pencil said, and either there'd be a name cut in the brass or a little punched mark or a set of scratched initials, or there wouldn't. I found five of Leo Vane's eleven before four o'clock. Five times I looked where a dead man had signed his work and there was nothing but clean metal. The clocks ticked on at me, all the same. They kept perfect time. That was the insult of it. He was right about that.

Nobody bothered me. A big girl of seventeen or so with a tray passed me twice on the back stairs and said "Sorry" both times. From the kitchen came a woman's voice raised over a clatter of pans, giving orders, and once, when I was on the top landing by a door with bars on the little window beside it, I thought I heard that same woman's voice much nearer than it should have been, low, saying something sharp, and then nothing. I tried the door. It was locked or stuck. I stood there a moment. An old nursery, by the bars. It was no business of Pemberton's.

In the long gallery I met the late Mrs Vane.

She was six feet high in a gold frame at the end, where the light came in. Painted in the fifties, when they still did it properly. A woman in her thirties in a grey silk evening dress, standing, one hand on the back of a chair, and looking out of the picture with grey eyes at somebody she was about to say something funny to. Not a beauty. Better than that. I stood in front of her a while. I've looked at a lot of photographs of dead women in the course of work, and it's a rule that you can't tell from the picture. But I'd have liked to hear what she was about to say.

On the wall under her a clock was going. There was always a clock going.

I left the big one till last.

The library was at the end of the ground floor, a long brown room with a fire in it and books to the ceiling that nobody had opened since the old king died. In the bay of the window stood a mahogany table, and on the table a square glass case with a brass frame, and inside the glass, with the grey light coming through it, the thing in Fenn's twelfth photograph. One million two hundred thousand pounds. It was smaller than I'd thought. It was going. The big hand moved while I watched, and the five small dials went about their own affairs, and the little painted moon, three-quarters of a face, looked at me sideways out of its slot.

There was a green baize cover folded on the table beside it, like the cloth off a parrot's cage.

I checked the door. I got the print out, and the loupe. The case had a lock but the key was in it. I'm not proud of how careful I was. I opened the glass an inch at a time and I didn't breathe on anything, and I found the left foot of the dial, underneath, where the pencil said, and I got down on my knees on Leo Vane's carpet with the glass in my eye like a real one and looked for a sprig of thorn.

Brass. Clean, yellow, with the fine lines the file had left. Nothing scratched on it at all.

I shifted. I looked again. I got the angle from the photograph, held the print up beside the clock, and matched them, and in the photograph you could see it, a tiny thing like a bird's footprint. On the clock I couldn't. The light was bad. It was coming four o'clock on an October afternoon with rain on the window, and I was a retired copper on his knees with a three-pound loupe he'd owned for two days, looking for a scratch the size of a grass seed, on the wrong side of a dial, in the shadow of its own foot. I couldn't get at the back of the moon without taking things apart, and I'd promised him I'd keep my hands in my pockets.

I told myself it was the angle.

I want that on the record, because I've had a long time to think about it since. I told myself it was the angle, and the light, and that the old man had said eleven and this wasn't one of the eleven, and that a clock worth more than the street I live in would be the last one anyone dared touch, not the twelfth. I shut the glass. I turned the key. I've been wrong about bigger things, but not many, and never on my knees.

"Find any woodworm?"

I got up without hurrying. That's the main thing, not to hurry.

The man in the doorway filled it. Six foot two and wide with it, in a tweed jacket that had been made for him when he was a stone lighter, with thick dark hair going grey in a handsome way and a face that had been good-looking so long that it hadn't noticed it was starting to blur. He had a tumbler in his hand, half full, the colour of the stuff I drink myself, and it was not his first. A heavy gold ring on the little finger. He came into the room with a hitch in his walk, the left leg, and stood looking at me, and at the loupe in my hand, and at the clock, in that order. He was smiling. I've been smiled at like that across an interview table.

"Edmund Vane," he said. "You're the insurance. My wife says you don't wear a watch. I thought that was rather good, for a man who's come to count the clocks."

"Harry Cade. I find I'm never short of the time in this house."

"No. God, no. One isn't." He drank. "You'll have seen the lot, then. What's the damage?"

"I've only looked at the cases, Mr Vane. A proper valuation takes a specialist a week."

"Then what's the point of you?"

"I count them. I see they're here, and where they're kept, and what the locks are like. Then I go home and write that the locks are no good."

"The locks are no good," he agreed. He strolled to the table. He looked down at the Thorne clock through its glass, and for a second there was something in his face that I couldn't read and that didn't go with the rest: a kind of flat distaste, the way you'd look at a dog that had bitten somebody you knew. Then he took the baize off the table and dropped it over the case, and the clock was gone. It ticked on underneath.

"Father covers it at night," he said. "Like a budgerigar. He thinks the light fades the silvering." He turned round, and he was close, and bigger than me, and he let me notice both. "Fenn fixed this up. Fenn's an old woman. I'd have told Pemberton's to send their man on a weekday like anyone else and not come sniffing round on the one night of the year when the family's got its hair down. But here you are. So I'll tell you how it goes. You'll eat your dinner. You'll drink my father's claret, which is the only thing in this house that's what it says on the label. You will not go poking about after dark, because this is an old house, with a lot of stairs in it, and we've lost the electricity three times this autumn. And tomorrow morning when the road dries you'll drive back to wherever you come from and write that the locks are no good." He smiled again. "I shouldn't like you to have an accident. Think of the paperwork."

"I'm obliged for the warning."

"It wasn't a warning. It was hospitality." He finished the tumbler. "Drinks at six in the hall. That's six by us. Seven to you, if you had a watch."

He went out. The hitch in the walk was worse going than coming. I stood where I was and listened to him cross the hall, and I thought, there goes a man who's frightened of something, and it isn't me.

Then I went to the window, because I'd heard an engine.

You could see the Wade from there, the whole grey length of it. It had been under the sea since ten that morning and it was only now coming up again, a bit at a time, black and wet, as the tide drew off. Most of it was clear. The dip in the middle still had water lying across it that the wind was pushing along. And right in the dip, in the water, a small red sports car was sitting at an angle with its bonnet up. A tall young man in a yellow pullover was walking round and round it. And from the island end a tractor was grinding out along the causeway, with a thin dark boy at the wheel and a girl with next to no hair standing up behind him, hanging on to his shoulders, and laughing so that I could see her teeth from the library.

Under the tractor's back axle, where it had been slung for the ride, a black dinner jacket was flapping from a hook like something they were going to bury.

I didn't know then what it was for. I thought it was a funny thing to take to a breakdown. I found out later that they'd been across to the stable that afternoon, the boy and the girl, dressing a pair of wooden figures on the clock tower for the party, the way the family did every year, and that there'd been a jacket over, and I thought no more about it. That's the way with the things that matter. They go past the window on a tractor and you're looking at the girl.

I counted on my fingers. The Wade would close at five past ten, real time. From then until half past five the next morning there'd be six feet of North Sea on that road, and whoever had been stripping Leo Vane's walls for a year would be shut on ninety acres of mud with a hundred clocks, a frightened husband, a wife who'd read me like a bus timetable, an old man who couldn't trust his own head, and me.

I liked that fine. It's how I'd have arranged it myself. Nobody slips out for a quiet word with a fence when the fence would have to swim.

It took me until a quarter past one in the morning to see that it cuts both ways. When the door's shut, it's shut on you too. And I had it wrong about the swimming.

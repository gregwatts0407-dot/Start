## Chapter One

The taxi would not take her across.

"Not in the dark, love," the driver said. "Not for a tenner, nor yet for twenty. I've a wife."

"The tide's well out. Look at it. It's been out for two hours."

"That's as may be. I had a mate drove a wedding party over there in 'eighty-six and they found the car in March." He had already switched off the meter. "There's a pub. They'll ring across for you."

"There's no telephone at the house that works."

"Then I should stop the night at the pub," said the driver, and held out his hand for the fare.

So Imogen Vane came home to Compline as she had left it twenty years before, on foot, with a bag in her hand and the wind in her face.

She stood at the top of the ramp until the tail lights had gone. It was the Friday evening, a little after seven. Behind her were the few lit windows of Thorney Staithe. In front of her was the dark. It had been a clear cold day and the last of it still showed in the west, a band of green light low over the flats, and against that she could see the line of withies going out, each one thinner than the last, and at the end of them the low black shape of the Ness with one lamp burning.

That would be the lamp in the porch. Her father had it lit at dusk every night of the year. He said that a house with a tide in front of it owed the traveller that much.

She had not meant to feel anything. She had sat in the train from Edinburgh for six hours with a book open on her knee, telling herself that she was a woman of forty-four going to visit an old father, as she did twice a year. But she had never before come in October. She had managed that for twenty autumns. There had always been an exhibition to hang, or a panel in the studio that could not be left, or a cold. Celia had stopped asking. And then Fenn had written, in his dry hand. *Your father has said that this will be the last time he keeps the night. I think he means it. I think you should be there.*

The wind smelled of mud and salt and rotting weed. It was the smell of her whole childhood. She had forgotten that a smell could do that, come straight in under the ribs without asking. She was nine years old, in gumboots, with a shrimping net. Then she was twenty-four, running.

She picked up her bag and went down the ramp.

To her left, as she came off the wall, there were the buildings of the farm. She had told herself that she would not look. She looked. Saltcote lay under the bank as it had always lain, long and low, tarred black, with its barns round it. There was a light in the kitchen window. There was smoke going up straight from the chimney in the cold. And in the yard, tied to the ring by the door, a big grey horse was standing with a rug on.

Her heart did something foolish. She stood still on the shingle with her bag.

It was only a horse. Amos Garrow had always had greys; he had bred them. He would be sitting in that kitchen now with his boots off, an old man of seventy-seven, and he had never spoken to a Vane since the day they buried her mother. He had stood at the back of the church, by himself, with his hat in his hands. When they came out he was gone.

His son had been gone longer. She did not let herself say the name, even now, even here. She had found, over the years, that it was like a bad tooth. One could live with it perfectly well if one kept one's tongue away.

She walked out onto the Wade.

* * *

There was a moon rising behind her, nearly full. By the time she was a quarter of the way over it had cleared the sea wall and laid her shadow down on the stones in front of her, long and thin, with a bag. The mud on either side shone like pewter. Somewhere out on it a curlew called, and another answered, a long way off.

She had walked this road ten thousand times. Her feet knew it. They knew where the surface was sound and where the winter had got under it, and she let them go and did not look down.

She was thinking, as she always did here, of her mother.

It was not grief, exactly, after so long. Grief wore itself smooth. It was more like a room in the mind that one walked through every day and no longer saw. But tonight every piece of it was sharp-edged again. It was the moon that did it. There had been a moon that night too, in 'seventy-four, though later, and smaller. She remembered it standing over the orangery roof.

She had been twenty-four, and she had known everything.

She knew that her mother was leaving. She had known for a fortnight, since she walked into the Grey Room without knocking and found the jewel case open on the bed. She knew for whom. And that had been the part that she could not forgive, though she had cried in her mother's arms and promised to say nothing. It was not that her mother loved another man. Imogen was not a child; she had seen how it was between her parents, the kindness and the distance. It was *which* man. It was the farm. Her mother had been crossing this road at night for ten years to the same yard that Imogen herself had been crossing to since May, and neither of them had known it of the other.

She had thought she had found something that was hers alone. A boy with brown arms, who could not put three words together, and who looked at her as though she had hung the moon. She had gone to him through the dusk with her heart in her mouth, the squire's daughter, feeling wicked and free. And all that time she had only been walking in her mother's footprints.

*With Tom's father? You?*

She had said it like an accusation. She could hear her own voice now, shrill, twenty-four years old. And her mother had laughed, and then stopped laughing, and said, "Oh, my darling. I'm so sorry. I ought to have told you. I hadn't the face."

They had made it up. They had made a plan. On the night of the Hour her mother would say what she had to say to Papa, and then go, on foot, at one. Imogen would walk with her as far as the farm. Amos would be waiting at the Wade head with a lantern. And Tom would be there. The four of them would stand in that kitchen. After that, her mother had said, with the old amusement back in her eyes, the Vanes and the Garrows might as well put up the banns in pairs and save the vicar a journey.

It should have happened. It had been so nearly managed.

Imogen stopped walking. She had come to the dip.

The Rill lay in front of her, black, with the moon in it. The planks of the Clapper were silver. And beside the bridge, on its four legs, stood the Perch.

She had not been as near to it as this since she was a girl. On her visits she had always crossed by car, and looked at the far withies. It was smaller than she remembered. A tarred box, a ladder, a little door. Her brother had been going to mend that ladder for as long as she could remember.

They had found her mother a hundred yards from here. On the seaward side. The ebb had left her on the mud, on her back, with her grey eyes open, and her stockinged feet. Imogen had not seen it. She had been told. Dolly Pask had told her, in the kitchen, holding both her hands. She had said, "She looked surprised, Miss Immy. That's all. She just looked surprised."

Imogen put her bag down on the planks. She stood with her hand on one of the posts of the Perch. The tar was cold and a little sticky.

"I should have come with you," she said aloud. "I said I would. I'm sorry."

Nobody answered. The curlew called again. The water went by under the bridge, very quietly, toward the sea.

* * *

Dolly Pask opened the kitchen door to her and said, "Well. So you've come," and then put her arms round her.

It was the only embrace Imogen had had in that house in twenty years. Dolly smelled as she had always smelled, of flour and coal smoke. She was sixty-three now and as broad as a door. She held on for a moment longer than was needed, and then stood back and looked her up and down.

"You're thin. They don't feed you up there. You've walked it? In them shoes? Sit down. Your father's in the Shop and Mr Edmund's out and madam's lying down with her head. There's only me and the nurse."

"The nurse?"

"Miss Quaile. For the old gentleman's nights. Mr Fenn got her. She's a queer little thing, white as a candle, but she's good with him. She tells him the truth." Dolly said it as one might say of a new dog that it bit. "He asks is your mother come in, and she tells him. Straight out. I thought he'd go for her. He thanked her."

Imogen sat down at the long table. She had sat there as a child to shell peas. "How is he?"

"He's eighty-one. Some days he's sharp as a tack. Some days he calls me Nanny." She was cutting bread. "He's been up to something this month. Out to the tower at all hours, with his tools. I asked what he was doing and he said he was getting something back that he'd lent. I don't ask twice."

"And Edmund?"

Dolly's knife paused, for the space of a breath, and went on.

"Mr Edmund's as he is," she said. "It's October."

It was all she would say. It was all that anyone at Compline ever said. Imogen ate bread and cheese and drank tea out of a blue-striped mug that she had drunk from at seven years old. She looked round the kitchen. Nothing had moved. The same clock on the wall, eight minutes fast. The same nail by the range, with the keys.

"There's a horse at Saltcote," she said. She had not meant to say it. "In the yard. A grey."

"That'll be Mab. The old man's mare."

"I thought he'd given up keeping them. He must be nearly eighty."

Dolly put the knife down.

She turned round. She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, looking at Imogen with small brown eyes that had seen her through measles and her first communion.

"You've not heard, then," she said. "No. Who'd tell you? Amos Garrow's dead, Miss Immy. He went a fortnight Tuesday. It was his chest. They buried him yesterday at St Botolph's. I was there. There was a good few; he was respected."

Imogen sat quite still. She had not known the old man, not to speak to. He had been a figure at the edge of things. But he had loved her mother. He had waited at the Wade head with a lantern for a woman who never came, and had gone home alone, and lived twenty years with it.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm very sorry. Was he—was anyone with him?"

"I was. I've neighboured that farm thirty year." Dolly was still looking at her. "And his son come. He got there the day before. They'd sent to him."

The kitchen clock ticked. It seemed very loud.

"His son," Imogen said.

"Tom. From Canada. He's been out on the prairies all this while, with cattle. He come in at the door in a big hat like the pictures, and I didn't know him. He's gone grey at the sides. He's as thin as a rail." Dolly's voice was carefully flat. "He sat with his father the last night and held his hand. I don't know what was said. He carried the coffin, Thursday, at the head end. And he stood at the grave after, on his own, a long time, and looked over here."

Imogen found that she was holding the blue mug with both hands. It was empty. She set it down, very precisely, in the ring it had left on the table.

"Is he staying?"

"I couldn't tell you. He don't say much; he never did. There's the stock to see to." Dolly hesitated. "There's a notice come to the farm. From Mr Fenn. Mr Edmund's giving them notice to quit, at Lady Day. He's a buyer for the land, I've heard. For caravans. That come the morning of the funeral."

"Edmund sent a notice to quit on the day they buried Amos Garrow?"

"I don't say he knew what day it was. Mr Edmund don't always." But her mouth was hard. "A hundred and eighty year the Garrows have had that ground. Tom read it in my kitchen, standing up. He read it twice, and folded it small, and put it in his shirt. He never said a word. I'd sooner he'd shouted."

Imogen got up. She went to the window over the sink and looked out. There was nothing to see. There was the dark yard, and beyond it the black line of the sea wall.

She had spent twenty years keeping her tongue from a tooth. She had done it by living four hundred miles away, in a tall cold flat in the New Town, among other people's pictures. She had been engaged once, for five months, to a decent man who collected Scottish watercolours. She had given him back his ring one evening without being able to tell him why.

And he was there. Half a mile from where she stood, across the water, in a kitchen with a lit window. He had gone grey at the sides.

"He's a child with him," said Dolly behind her.

Imogen turned round.

"A little girl. Nine or ten. Dark, like a gypsy. He was married out there, and the wife died, two year back. Cancer." Dolly had gone back to her bread. She spoke without looking up. "She's a sharp little thing. Don't miss nothing. She sat in my kitchen all Thursday afternoon while they was at the church, drawing, and never spoke, and when I give her her tea she said, 'Thank you, Mrs Pask,' as nice as you like, in that voice they have. And then she said, 'Is that the house where the lady drowned?' She'd been looking at it out the window."

"What did you tell her?"

"I said it was. I don't hold with lying to children." Dolly set a plate down, rather hard. "I'm minding her tomorrow. He's to go to Colchester, to see a man about the notice. So if you see a little dark thing about my cottage, that's who."

Imogen said that she would remember. She said that she was tired, and would go up. She took her bag.

At the foot of the back stairs she stopped, with her hand on the rail.

"Dolly. Did he ask about us? About any of us?"

"He asked was your father living. I said he was. He asked was Mr Edmund still at the house. I said he was." Dolly's back was toward her. "He never asked for you by name, Miss Immy. But he looked at me. When I said that Miss Imogen was in Scotland and never come home this time of year. He looked at me as if I'd took something off him that he'd been carrying."

* * *

She did not go to her own room. She went along the gallery.

There was a lamp burning at the far end, on the table beneath the portrait. It was always left. She walked the length of the sunk floor toward it, past the dark windows, with the clocks going on either hand. Under the frame the old cracked one was ticking. It had gained four seconds a week for as long as she could remember, and her father had never found out why.

She looked up at her mother.

She had been six years old when it was painted. She remembered the man, who had a beard and smelled of turpentine and had let her squeeze white out of a tube. She remembered her mother standing for him in the morning room, hour after hour, in the grey silk, with one hand on a chair. Imogen had sat on the floor and watched the dress come on the canvas, a stroke at a time. She thought now that it was where she had first learned what paint could do. She had been cleaning pictures for twenty years. She had never had her hands on a better piece of silk than that.

"I'm home," she said. "Papa says it's the last one. So I've come."

The grey eyes looked down, amused, as they had always looked. *Have you, darling? And what do you mean to do, now that you are here?*

She did not know. She put her hand in her coat pocket and felt the key.

It had been her mother's own. It was a long iron key with a loop of ribbon through it, gone the colour of weak tea. Dolly had given it to her on the day of the funeral. *You'd best have this. Your father's is in his waistcoat, and he'll lose it.* She had carried it for twenty years, in one bag after another. She had never once used it.

The door was six feet from where she stood.

She told herself that she was only going to look. She was a grown woman, and it was a room. There was nothing in it but furniture.

The lock turned as though it had been oiled yesterday. She pushed the door and went in, with the lamp from the gallery table in her hand.

The cold met her like a wall. And with it, so faint that she thought for a moment she was imagining it, there was a scent. Lavender. Camphor. And under those, something that stopped her breath. It was the stuff her mother had worn, that came in a square bottle from a shop in Jermyn Street. Imogen had not smelled it since she was twenty-four.

She stood in the doorway and let her eyes go round.

Nothing had been touched. Her father had seen to that. The white counterpane. The triple glass, with the silver brushes in their row. The book by the bed with a marker in it; she went and looked, and it was *Persuasion*. The clock on the mantel, stopped. And on the floor by the bed, side by side, with their toes to the wall, the grey satin shoes.

She stood over them. She had not been ready for the shoes.

*I shall go in my stockings,* her mother had said, that last afternoon, laughing, sitting on this bed. *The Wade eats shoes. I've lost three pairs to it in ten years. I shall leave them just here, where your father will find them, and he'll know then that I meant it and haven't simply fallen in a ditch.*

She had meant it. She had taken them off and set them down, neatly, as she did everything. Then she had gone down the stairs in her stockinged feet and out of the garden door.

Imogen had been waiting for her in the orangery.

That was the arrangement. They were to meet there at one, among the vines, and go together. Imogen had gone out early. She had not been able to bear the hall, and the clocks, and her father's face. She had quarrelled with Edmund at tea, about nothing, about a horse, and it had come out of her mouth before she knew. *Mother's going to him tonight. To Amos Garrow. She's leaving. Ask her yourself if you don't believe me.* She had seen his face go. She would see it until she died. She had run from the room.

So she had sat on an upturned pot in the orangery, in the dark, from eleven o'clock, sick with what she had done. She had heard the house clocks strike. And when her mother came through the garden door at one, in her tweed skirt and her stockings, with a lantern, and called softly, "Immy? Are you there? It's time," Imogen had not answered.

She had sat there in the dark behind the vine. She had held her breath.

She could not have said why, then. She was not sure that she could now. She had told Edmund. She had broken her word, and he would be coming, and there would be a scene, and it was her doing. She could not face her mother. She could not face Tom, who would see it in her. She was twenty-four years old and she hid in a greenhouse like a child who has broken a vase.

Her mother had waited. She had called once more. Then she had said, quite gently, to the dark, as though she knew perfectly well who was in it:

"Never mind, my darling. Come over in the morning."

And she had gone.

Imogen had watched the lantern cross the lawn and go down to the Wade. She had watched it for a long time, getting smaller. It had gone out along the road, steadily, a little yellow star. It had got more than halfway. She was sure of that. It had been well past the Perch.

Then a second light had left the house. It moved fast and unevenly. It was a man with a lantern, running.

She had known who it was. She had sat on that pot with her knuckles in her mouth and watched her brother go after her mother across the sea.

She had seen the two lights come together, far out. She had seen them stand. They stood for a long time, and she grew cold, and the moon moved over the roof. At some point she must have slept. She was twenty-four and had cried herself stupid. When she woke the glass was grey. There were no lights on the Wade. There was water where the Wade had been.

And across the lawn, from the direction of the sea, a man was walking toward the house. He was walking slowly. He was soaked to the neck, his hair flat on his head, his good suit black with wet. He went past the orangery not ten yards from her and did not turn his head.

It was Edmund. It was six o'clock in the morning. He had been out on the Wade with their mother at one, and he had come back, and she had not.

He went in by the garden door. An hour later he came down to breakfast in dry clothes and said that he had slept in the stable. Imogen had heard him say it. She had sat at the table with the toast going cold and said nothing at all. And at nine Dolly's husband had come up from the saltings at a run.

She had never told anyone. Not her father, who believed from that day that his clock had done it. Not the police, who asked a few questions and went away. She had never told Edmund that she knew. She had looked at him across this house for a week, across the coffin, and he had looked back, and it had lain between them like a blade.

She had refused to see Tom. He had come to the kitchen door three times that week. The third time he had stood in the yard for two hours in the rain, with his cap in his hands, looking up at her window. She had watched him from behind the curtain. She had thought: I told Edmund. I sat in the dark and let her go alone. If I had walked with her, she'd have crossed in twenty minutes, and we should all four be sitting in his father's kitchen. How can I ever go into that kitchen now?

In November she heard that he had gone abroad.

Imogen knelt down, slowly, on the cold carpet. She touched one of the satin shoes with the tip of her finger. It was stiff, and a little dusty.

"I didn't come over in the morning," she said. "I never came at all."

* * *

When she got to her feet her knees ached. She was forty-four; one's knees did. She took up the lamp.

She would go now. She had looked. She had seen what there was. She would lock the door and go to her own room and sleep, and tomorrow she would put on her black dress and sit at her father's table and get through it.

The lamp, as she turned, sent her shadow across the wardrobe. It was the big mahogany one with the long glass. She saw herself in it: a thin dark woman in a travelling coat, with a white streak in her hair and a careful face.

She did not decide to open it. Her hand did.

The dresses hung in a row, each in its cotton bag, as Dolly had put them away. Imogen knew what was in every one. The blue that her mother wore to church. The tweeds. The black velvet for funerals. She ran her hand along them, and the bags whispered.

At the end of the rail, by itself, on a padded hanger, there was one that was longer than the rest.

She lifted the cotton. She knew before she saw.

It came out of the bag like water. It was heavier than she expected, and cold, and when the lamplight touched it the whole length of it woke. Grey silk. Not the dull grey of a mouse or a pavement. It was the grey of a pigeon's breast, with rose in it, and blue. She had spent her life looking at how painters got that colour. She knew that it could not be got. One could only suggest it, with white dragged over a warm ground.

Her mother had worn it every year, for the Hour. It was the Hour dress. She had been painted in it, and she said that she must go on wearing it so that people could tell her from the picture. She had worn it that last night, at dinner. Imogen could see her at the foot of the table, with the candles on her shoulders, saying something that made even Papa smile.

And at half past twelve she had come up here and taken it off. She had hung it on its hanger and put on a tweed skirt and a jersey, to cross the sea to a farm.

She had taken off this dress to go to Amos Garrow. And she had never got there.

Imogen stood holding it. The silk slid over her arm.

It was mad, the thing that had come into her head. She was not a person who did such things. She mended what other people had made. She wore black, and grey flannel, and a watch she never looked at—no, not even a watch. She had not worn a colour since she was twenty-four.

She held the dress against herself and looked in the long glass.

A woman looked back at her with her mother's eyes. She had forgotten that she had them. One did not see one's own eyes. The grey silk lay under her chin and gave them back their colour. She saw the white streak at her temple. Her mother had never had that. But the rest—the brow, the set of the head, the mouth that she kept so carefully shut—

*And what do you mean to do, darling, now that you are here?*

"Nothing," Imogen said to the glass. "I don't mean to do anything. He's got a child. He never asked for me by name."

She put the dress back in its bag. Her hands were not quite steady. She hung it at the end of the rail and shut the wardrobe door.

She locked the room behind her. She set the lamp back on its table under the portrait and did not look up.

But when she lay in her own narrow bed that night, in the room she had slept in as a girl, she could not get warm. She lay awake and listened to the clocks tell eleven, and midnight, and one. She thought of a light in a kitchen window across the water.

*He looked at me as if I'd took something off him that he'd been carrying.*

Somewhere toward two, by the house, she got up and went to the window. Her room was on the west side. The moon was high. The Wade was gone. The tide had been over it for hours, and there was only water, white and still under the moon, from the garden wall to the far shore.

On the far shore, under the sea wall, the lamp in the farmhouse was still burning.

He was awake, too.

She stood there in her nightdress with her forehead against the cold glass. She stood until the light across the water went out.

## V. The Reversal

§ 44. At five minutes to one I took my place at the door.

I state my condition at that moment, as the experimenter should. I was weak; I had been upon the floor, they said, and my legs were not sure. But my head was as clear as it has been in ten years. I knew the date and the hour. I knew what I had built in the tower and to what I had set it. I knew that my wife had been dead twenty years, since I had been told so that morning.

I did not know that I had been out of the house. I did not know that there was a man lying in the ice house with his head broken. I record that with emphasis. Whatever else may be said of what followed, I went into it with no knowledge of the thing that I afterward found undone.

Miss Quaile stood at my left hand. I had asked it of her. The family were behind us in the hall, with the candles. I did not turn to count them.

I put my hand upon the bolt.

§ 45. The clocks struck one. I listened to each. I have listened to them every hour for sixty years and I know their voices as a shepherd knows his ewes. The Knibb upon the stairs is always first; she runs a little fast in damp weather. The Tompion by the door is last, and takes his time.

They were two short, I noticed. The Knibb did not speak, nor the Tompion. I had forgotten that those were among the eleven. What stood in their cases were of my own making, and my copies have never had the tone.

When they had done, I said, 'Now.' I drew the bolt and opened both leaves.

I have described the sensation that I have had at that door upon twenty nights: that something passed, outside, in the dark, going by.

It did not pass.

I am going to describe what I felt as plainly as I can, and I shall not dress it. The night came in, and the wind, and laid the candles over. That was the same. But there was besides a kind of pressure. It came across the gravel from the tower, and it came to the step, and it stopped there. I felt it upon my face and my chest, as one feels the heat of a fire that has been opened. It was not heat. It was not anything that I have a name for. It was large, and it had been travelling, and it had arrived.

I said, 'Come in, then. You are late. You have been late since the last Sunday in March.'

I spoke to it as to a person. I did not know how else.

And it came in. I felt it go by me into the house, on either side, as a tide comes round a post. I felt the hall fill. Behind me a hundred clocks were ticking, and I had the distinct impression that for the space of two or three beats they hesitated, every one, and then went on.

§ 46. I looked at the tower. I had meant to watch the Jacks; they are coupled to the going train, and I wished to see whether the striking would be affected.

There was a light upon the leads. Someone was up there with a lantern. I was not surprised. It seemed to me only reasonable that at such a moment there should be a light at the top of the engine.

The quarters rang. Then Jenny brought down her arm, and the great bell struck one.

Upon the stroke there were two reports, close together, from the foot of the tower. I took them for the take-up. A fusee that is wound suddenly hard will sometimes crack like a whip. I thought, it has caught. It has taken the load.

The lantern fell. And something fell after it.

It was the figure of a man. He was upon the left of the bell. He went backward off the leads, stiffly, with his arms spread, and was gone behind the roof.

I saw it quite clearly. Miss Quaile saw it also; she cried out.

I did not cry out. I had expected something of the kind. I had not known what form it would take; but I have said already that I do not believe a thing can be had for nothing. An hour was being given to this house which the house had not lent. It stood to reason that something must be taken in exchange. I had wondered all the autumn what it would be.

I said, 'Ah. Yes. I thought there would be something to pay.'

I took it at the time for the Jack. He stands upon that side, and he has been loose upon his pin for fifty years. I thought, the engine has taken the Jack. It is a fair price. He is two hundred years old and has done his work, and he never kept good time.

I told Miss Quaile to say nothing to the others. I did not think that they would understand the accounts.

I have had to revise that entry since. I shall come to it.

§ 47. I turned from the door. I had the hour. I had felt it come in. It remained to see what it would do.

There now occurred a thing which I did not at the time understand, and which I have since come to regard as the first piece of positive evidence.

A young man came into the hall from the kitchen passage. It was Dorothea's grandson. He was carrying a wooden box with tin corners. He said something that I did not catch; he seemed to think that it was the ice. He set it upon the table and cut the cord, and took out, from a blanket, a clock.

It was the Copy. I knew it across the room. I went and put my glass upon it. There was no device beneath the dial foot. The moon work had been jarred off its cock, as it will be if the movement is laid upon its side, and it had stopped.

I said, 'Ah. The Copy. I wondered where I had put it.'

That was the truth. I did wonder. I had set that clock under the glass in the library window, in September, with my own hands. I could not conceive how it came to be in a box, in a blanket, in the arms of a young man who thought that it was a pudding.

I asked him no questions. It did not then seem important. I thanked him, and said that we had been promised an ice.

I ask the reader to mark it now. I return to it in § 61.

§ 48. I sat down in the chair that they had put for me by the door. And I found that I wished to speak.

I had not expected it. I have never been a man who wished to speak. But the hour was in the house; I could feel it standing in the hall, among us, like a guest who has come a long way and is waiting to be told why he was sent for. It was my wife's hour. She had made it for the purpose of saying what was true. I had wasted twenty-three of them in her lifetime and twenty since.

So I spoke. I am told that I was lucid. Fenn has said that it was the clearest that he has heard me in three years. I do not find that remarkable. I have explained that the hour which I had lived had been taken off me, upon the floor of the hall, and I was the lighter for it.

I said that I had asked a question at dinner and no longer wished to have it answered.

That was so. I had sat at that table and looked at my son's hand, and I had known. But I found, with the hour standing at my elbow, that I did not care to be told. I had said that I wanted the reason. I saw now that I knew the reason, and had known it for forty years, and that it was I.

I said what a clock is for. I said that it is for telling a man how much time he has got, and that I had a hundred and eleven and they had only ever told me how much I had had. It is the truest thing that I know about my trade. I had not known it until I said it.

I said that if anything had been taken from me it had no doubt been wanted more than I wanted it. I said that I had not been a father to whom a person could come and ask. I said that their mother had told me so.

And I said that the matter of the clocks was closed, and to go upon the Hour's account. I told Fenn to note it.

I looked for Edmund as I said it. I wished him to hear. I had said it for him.

He was not in the hall. I thought, he has gone out; he will have it from the others. I was not uneasy. I record that also.

§ 49. There was one thing more that I had to do in that hour, and I had been putting it off for thirty years.

I sent Fenn for his pen.

I must here speak of Amos Garrow, who held the farm of Saltcote from me, and from my father before me, and who died a fortnight before the night of which I write.

I knew him all my life. He was four years my junior. We were boys upon the same marsh and never, I suppose, exchanged a thousand words. He came to the estate office upon the quarter days and paid his rent. I would see him across the Wade, with his sheep. He was a silent man, and so am I, and we nodded.

He farmed that ground better than it has ever been farmed. I am not a countryman, but I can see when a thing is kept in order. His gates hung true. He had a way with a horse that I have never had with any living creature.

My wife rode over to Saltcote. She had done so from about the year 1964. She said that it was for the exercise, and to see the mares.

I did not ask. I have said that already and I am tired of saying it. I regulated the gallery clock.

I have told my son, in the ice house, that I knew of it from about 1980. That is when I allowed myself to say it. But I think that I knew before she died. There was a look that she had, coming in from the Wade of a morning, with the salt on her boots. I had seen it upon her face in 1948. I had not seen it since about 1950.

After she was drowned, Garrow did not come to the office. He sent his rent by post, in notes, for a year. Then he came again. He stood in front of Fenn's desk with his cap in his hand. I was there once. He looked at me. I looked at him. We had each of us lost the same thing, and I was his landlord.

I might have spoken. I had only to say, 'Garrow, I know where she was going. I do not hold it against you. I am sorry for your loss.' It would have taken ten seconds. I have had the words ready for him as I had them for her.

I never said them. He died in his chair, a fortnight since, with the lamp in his window. Mrs Pask told me. She said that it had burned there every night for twenty years.

And upon the morning of his funeral my son's solicitor, who is also mine, sent a notice to quit to that farm. I did not know of it until dinner. I heard Edmund say it across the table. *That farm's out at Lady Day.*

I thought of that lamp. I thought: I let that man pay me rent for twenty years, for the ground from which he watched my wife drown. I took his notes. And now my family is turning his son off, upon the day that he was put into the earth beside her.

So I told Fenn to write. I said that there was something that I owed across the water, that had been owing a long time. I said, 'I can't pay it to the man. I let him die without. I'll pay it to his name.' I gave Saltcote, the house and buildings and the hundred and forty acres, freehold, to Amos Garrow or his heirs, for ever.

Fenn asked for what consideration. I said, 'For the hour I owe.'

He wrote it. Octavia Prowse witnessed it, and the vicar, when he had been woken.

I do not put that forward as part of the experiment. It was a debt of the ordinary kind. But I should like it understood that it was paid in that hour because I do not think that I could have paid it in any other. I had not the means, in the other eight thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine, of saying such a thing aloud.

§ 50. I closed my eyes. I had done what I could with the hour, as a man. It remained to see what the engine would do with it.

I was listening. I thought that I should hear her upon the stairs. I had the nineteen words ready. *Margot, the clock is wrong. I put it back before dinner.*

I waited. I heard nothing but the clocks, and my family breathing.

Then I thought, she does not come down until there is music. She never did. She waited for the waltz.

I asked my grandson to wind the gramophone.

It is a Lehár. 'Gold and Silver.' I first danced it with my wife at the Hunt Ball of 1947, having been taught the steps the week before by a professional in Colchester, to whom I paid three guineas. She said that I waltzed like a man pacing out a boundary. But she had it played at every Hour afterward, and I learned to do it with my eyes shut. I found that if I did not look, I could feel the beat through her hand.

The record began. I sat with my eyes closed and counted.

At the ninth bar I heard the girl upon the stairs say something, sharply. I heard Octavia get up out of her chair. She said a name. It was the name that only she and one other person ever used.

I opened my eyes.

§ 51. I shall be exact. It is the result of the experiment and I shall not have it said that I recorded it loosely.

She was upon the fourth stair from the bottom. It is where she always paused. She had one hand upon the rail.

She was in the grey silk. I chose that silk. I went with her to the shop in 1955; it was in Bond Street; and I picked it from forty bolts, since it was the colour of Jenny's gown upon the tower, and she laughed at me. I know the fall of it. I know how it takes a light. I could not be deceived in that stuff any more than in the silvering of a dial.

Her hair was up. Her shoulders were bare. She was looking at me.

I noted her face. I do not read faces, as I have said. But I know my wife's. It was pale, and it was wet, and she was smiling a little, as she smiled when she had made up her mind to something difficult and meant to go through with it.

I rose. I said, 'You came back. Margot. You came back.'

She crossed the hall. She held out her hand. She said, 'Yes, Papa.'

I did not understand that. Then she said, 'Yes, Leo. I came back.'

It was her voice. It was lower than I recollected, and there was something in it of the north; but twenty years is a long time, and I do not know where she had been.

I took her hand. It was cold. I felt her fingers, and the bones of them, and I knew them. I put my other hand at her back, where the silk is cut low. And I danced with her.

§ 52. I must now deal with an objection, and I shall do so before I go further, since I am aware that it has been in the reader's mind for some pages.

I have a daughter. Her name is Imogen. She is forty-four years of age. She has her mother's height and build and colouring, and in particular her mother's eyes; I have often remarked upon it. She was in the house upon that night. She has a key to her mother's room.

I am told that it was Imogen who came down the stairs. I am told so by Miss Quaile, by Mrs Pask, by my grandson, and by Imogen herself, who came to me upon the following evening and said that she hoped that she had not done wrong.

I have listened to them. I am a man of science, or have tried to be, and I do not dismiss testimony because it is unwelcome. I set down their account, here, in full: that my daughter, for reasons of her own, put on her mother's gown upon the twentieth anniversary of her death and came down to me in it; and that I, being old, and expecting my wife, took her for her.

Now I shall set down mine.

I had my hand upon her back. I had her hand in mine. I led her through forty-eight bars of a waltz that I learned in 1947 for one woman and have danced with no one else. I am not a man of feeling. I do not go by impressions. I go by measurement; and there is a kind of measurement that the hands make, which is finer than any gauge. I have told the reader that my son could feel a thousandth of an inch. I taught him.

I know the weight of my wife upon my arm in the turn. I know by how much she anticipated the beat; she was always a shade before it, and I a shade behind, and that was how we went. I know the place at the end of the second strain where she would press my hand, very slightly, to remind me that the reverse was coming, since I never could remember it.

She pressed my hand at the end of the second strain.

My daughter has never danced that waltz with me. She was a child in her nightgown upon the stairs.

I do not say that the two accounts cannot both stand. I have told the reader that a regulator and a sundial may disagree by a quarter of an hour and both be right. It may be that Imogen put on the gown. I think it very likely. She is a good girl, and braver than I knew. But I say that a gown must be put on by somebody before it can be worn, and that an hour must come in at a door before it can be spent; and that when the hour that I had sent for was let out into my hall, along the line that I had laid for it, it found there a woman of the right build, in the right silk, at the foot of the right stair.

I do not think that the engine made my wife. I think that it had only to bring her as far as the nearest thing that would hold her. And I think that my daughter knew, when she said 'Leo,' that she was not speaking altogether for herself.

That is my observation. I leave it beside the other.

§ 53. I had the nineteen words. I began upon them. And I found that they were wrong.

I had prepared them for the hall in 1974. *It is three, not two. You have a quarter of an hour.* But I was not in 1974. I had her in my arms, in the Odd Hour, and the regulator was stopped, and it was this night's tide that was running outside. If I told her the tide of twenty years ago she would walk out upon the wrong water.

It was fortunate that I had been to the library. I had gone in at three that afternoon, from habit, to read the Thorne. I had forgotten that it was the Copy that stood there; but the Copy is exact, and keeps the same tide. I had the figures.

So I gave them to her. I said that I had had it wrong for her, the last time. I told her what I had done with the hall clocks and that I had never told her. I said it all. It took a good deal more than nineteen words.

And then I gave her the hour. High water at eight minutes to one. No crossing on foot before half past four. A horse at twenty minutes to three, and he must swim at the Rill, with three feet upon it and a set to the north.

I do not know why I said a horse. I had not thought of one. It came with the figures. The Garrows' horses always could.

She said that she heard me. She said that she would wait for the horse.

I said, 'Then you will get across. That is all I wanted. I don't ask you to stay.'

I had not known that I should say that. But it was the thing. I see now that it was what I ought to have said to her in July of 1974, when she stood behind me at the gallery clock for five minutes and I did not turn round. She was waiting to be asked whether she was happy. I did not ask. I should not have known what to do with the answer.

I could not ask it now. It was twenty years too late for a question. But I could tell her that I knew the answer, and that she might go.

She wept. I felt it. She said something to me, low, as we turned beneath the Tompion.

She said, 'It wasn't the clock, Leo. It wasn't your doing. I knew the time. I swear to you, it was never the clock.'

I did not understand her. I thought that she was being kind. She was always kind to me at the last; it was how I knew that she had gone. I told her so.

But I wish the reader to mark those words. *It was never the clock.* I had heard them once already that night, an hour and a half before, in another voice, upon the landing of the ice house, and had answered them with a piece of lead. I did not then recollect that I had. It had been taken from me. I heard them from her as if for the first time.

I have since had them a third time. I shall say where.

§ 54. The record ended. I said to her that it was quite all right, and that we had had the time after all.

She kissed me. She held my face. I said to her, very low, that she was not to keep him waiting. I knew that there was a man with a lantern. There always had been.

She went from me toward the garden passage. I watched the grey silk go round the edge of the curtain, as I had watched it go round a door some hours before, in another year.

I found my chair.

My wife came back across the hall in her grey silk and I gave her the tide correctly. She did not drown.

I state that as the first result. I have verified it since by enquiry. There was a woman in grey silk upon the Wade that night, upon a horse, at twenty minutes to three. She reached the further shore. My daughter tells me that it was she. I have given my view of that.

No one was drowned upon the Wade in that hour who had been given the tide by me. I had waited twenty years to be able to write that sentence.

§ 55. I sat for some time. I was very tired and entirely content. Dorothea's grandson came and asked me for five thousand pounds, for wine, and I gave it to him, since he told me the truth about the wine. I dated the cheque with the hour. It seemed to me that it was the only date that signified.

And then, as I sat there, it came back.

I had been told that I had gone to the ice house. I had asked what for. Now I knew.

It returned to me whole, as a dial comes up under the cleaning rag. The lamp upon the landing. The box. His face. *It will serve.* The weight going out upon its line. The twelve steps.

I looked at my hands. They were quite steady. I looked at my right cuff. There was a stain upon it, brown, that I had taken for port.

I have asked myself why it was given back to me at that moment and not before. I believe that the reason is mechanical. So long as I was in the hour for the second time, and it was still running along the same line as the first, I could not be let to remember the first; I have explained that. But my wife had come down the stairs. She had not gone out by the garden door; she had come to me, and been told the tide. The second passage had parted from the first. It was upon a different line now. And there was no longer any harm in my knowing what had happened upon the other.

I sat with it. I was not afraid. I ask the reader to understand that. I had killed my son, at ten minutes to twelve, and I recollected every particular. But I had also just held my wife, who had been dead twenty years, and sent her safely over the water.

I thought: if she is not drowned, he is not struck.

At one o'clock the hour was returned to me, as I had calculated, and I went through it a second time.

I got up out of my chair. I went across the hall to the regulator and opened the case. The weight hung at the bottom of its line where the girl had put it. I touched it with my fingers.

There was a man standing behind me. He was wet to the thighs, and had been bleeding from the head. He had the hands of a policeman. He asked me a question, and I answered him. I told him that I had killed my son, and that now I had not. I told him that he should write it down. He seemed a capable person and I am sorry that I cannot recall his name.

I told him that the Jack had stopped. I had been listening for the quarter and it had not come.

Then I went out of my front door, alone, to see about Edmund.

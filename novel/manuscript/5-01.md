## 1. The Burying

The man came back to that country on a Monday in the fall of the year, with a child, in a hired car.

He had been gone twenty years. He drove out from the railway town through the flat land in the afternoon with the rain coming in off the sea. It was a small land after where he had been. There were hedges. The fields were the size of a horse pasture and every one of them had a ditch around it with water standing. He had forgotten how wet it was. He had forgotten that the sky came down so low a man could feel it on his hat.

The child sat beside him with her face to the glass. She was nine years old and had not seen the sea. She had asked him on the airplane what it looked like and he had said it looked like the prairie only it moved. She had thought about that for some time. Then she had got out her notebook and written something down.

Is this where you were a kid, she said.

Near enough.

It's all one colour.

It is today.

They came through the village. It had not changed. There was the church and the public house and the row of cottages with their doors on the street. An old woman was standing in a doorway and she looked at the car as it went by and he saw her know him. He lifted one finger off the wheel. She did not lift anything.

Past the last house the road went up onto the sea wall. He stopped the car on top of it. He had not meant to.

The tide was out. The mud ran away grey and shining to the edge of the sky. Across it lay the Wade with its two lines of stakes, and at the far end the island, low and black, with the trees leaning. He could see the roof of the house. He could see the tower.

He sat with his hands on the wheel. The engine ran. The wipers went across and back.

What is that, said the child.

That's the Ness.

Who lives there?

People called Vane.

Do we know them?

He put the car in gear.

No, he said. We don't know them.

* * *

The farm lay under the wall on the landward side. It was a long low house of tarred boards with a tile roof gone green, and barns to the north and east of it built the same, and a yard between. There had been Garrows on it since his great-great-grandfather came home from the French wars with one arm and took the lease. None of them had ever owned a foot of it. They paid the rent on Lady Day and Michaelmas, in the estate office, with their hats off.

He drove down into the yard. There was nobody about. The stock was out on the saltings to the north; he could see the sheep, small and dirty white, strung along a creek. In the home pasture by the barn a grey mare was standing with her tail to the rain.

He got out. The child got out on her side and stood looking.

The mare had lifted her head. She was old. She had gone white all over, the way greys do, white as a bone. She looked at him across forty yards of wet grass. She did not move for as long as it would take to count twenty. Then she walked to the fence, slowly, and put her head over it.

He went to her. He stood with his hand on her face.

He had pulled her out of her mother with his own arm, on the saltings, by a lantern. He had been twenty-three. A girl had held the light.

Hello, old lady, he said.

The mare breathed on his shirt.

Is she yours, the child said. She had come up beside him. She was a horseman's daughter and she stood where the mare could see her.

She's my dad's.

What's her name?

Mab.

That's a funny name.

It's out of a poem.

You don't know any poems.

No, he said. I was give it.

A woman had come out of the house. She stood on the step with her arms wrapped in her apron. She was broad and grey and he did not know her at first, and then he did. It was Dolly Pask from the cottage. She had been a young married woman when he left, with a tongue like a rasp. She cooked at the big house.

She looked at the hat. He had not thought about the hat. He had worn it for twenty years and it was only what went on his head.

Well, she said. Tom Garrow. You'd best come in. He's been asking the time since Thursday.

* * *

The old man was in the kitchen in his chair.

It was the chair he had always sat in, by the range, with the high back. They had put a pillow behind him and a blanket over his knees. He had been a big man, wide, with hands that could span a ewe's back. The hands lay on the blanket now like a pair of gloves someone had left there. His face had gone in. His breath came and went with a sound in it like a wet rope running through a block.

His eyes were open. They were the same.

The man took off his hat. He stood in the door with the rain running off his coat onto the bricks.

The old man looked at him. He looked at him for a long time. He looked at the grey in his hair and the lines on his face and the coat, which was a stockman's coat of oiled canvas with a cape to the shoulders. The clock on the wall ticked.

You've took your time, the old man said.

I came when they sent.

I never told them to send.

No. Dolly did.

She would. The old man's eyes moved. Who's that?

The child had come in behind. She stood by the man's leg and looked at the old man the way she looked at everything.

That's Wren. That's your granddaughter.

She's dark.

Her mother was dark.

Where's her mother?

She died. Two years back. I wrote you.

Did you? The old man was quiet. I don't recollect. I don't read them. I can't see to. He looked at the child again. Come here, girl.

She went. She stood in front of the chair. The old man lifted one of his hands off the blanket, an inch, and let it fall.

She's a look of your mother, he said. About the jaw. Can she ride?

She can ride, the man said.

Can you ride, girl?

Yes sir.

Sir, the old man said. He made a sound that might once have been a laugh. They've learned her manners out there. That's more than I ever done for you.

He shut his eyes. He did not speak again that afternoon.

Dolly Pask gave them tea. She said the doctor had been at noon and had said it would be the night, or the next. She said the old man had not been to his bed in a week. He would not lie down. He said if he lay down he would not get up and he had a thing to do first. She did not know what thing.

She took the child home with her to the cottage across the yard to sleep. The child went without a word. At the door she looked back.

Is he going to die, she said.

Yes.

Like Mom.

Not like that. He's old. It's different.

How is it different?

He's ready, the man said. He did not know that it was true. He found out in the night.

* * *

He sat with his father.

He built up the range and pulled a chair to the other side of it. He took off his boots. The rain stopped about ten and the wind went down, and through the wall he could hear the tide come in over the flats. He had not heard it in twenty years. In the place where he had been living the nearest salt water was six hundred miles off and the only sound at night was the wind in the grass and sometimes coyotes. He had lain awake his first year there listening for the sea.

The old man slept and woke and slept. His breath caught and went on. The man watched the blanket rise and fall.

He thought about the last time he had sat in that kitchen. It was the fourteenth of November in the year 1974. He had a cardboard suitcase and a ticket. His father had been sitting where he sat now, with a face like a shut gate. He had not looked up from the fire.

I'm going, then, the boy had said.

Go.

I'll write.

Do as you like.

Dad. It's no good my stopping. She'll not see me. There's nothing here for me.

There's a hundred and forty acre and thirty score of sheep.

That's not what I mean.

I know what you mean, his father had said. You mean a Vane. Well, I had one. You've seen what come of it. You go on to Canada and find a girl that'll have you and forget you ever looked at that house. I wish to God I had.

The boy had stood there. He was twenty-three and he had lost the girl and he did not know why. He had found out only the week before, from the talk in the village, what his father and Mrs Vane had been to each other. He had learned it standing in the public bar with a glass in his hand. He had said nothing to anyone. He had come home and looked at his father across the table and his father had looked back and neither of them had spoken of it.

You might have told me, he said. He had not meant to say it. All that summer. You knew where I was going nights. You might have said.

What was I to say?

That you was doing the same.

His father had turned his head at that.

It wasn't the same, he said. Don't you ever say that. You was a boy after a girl. I waited ten year for that woman. I'd the lamp lit for her. She was a hundred and fifty yard from that door. He stopped. His jaw moved. Get out, Tom. Go and catch your boat.

So he went. He walked up onto the wall with his suitcase. At the top he had turned and looked across at the house on the island, at one window. Then he walked the four miles to the bus.

He had written at Christmas for twenty years. He had sent a photograph of the wedding and one of the child. He had never had a line back. Dolly Pask wrote, twice a year, in pencil. *Your father keeps well. He has the mare still. He don't say much.*

The clock on the kitchen wall said three.

Tom, the old man said.

* * *

His eyes were open. They were clear. He had hold of the arm of the chair.

I'm here.

I know you're here. I'm not daft. Give me your hand.

The man got up and went to him. He knelt on the bricks. He put his hand on the blanket and the old man's fingers came down over his wrist and closed.

There was no strength in the rest of him. A child could have lifted him. But the man could not have broken that grip without breaking the bone.

I've a thing to tell you, his father said. I've held on for it. I'd have told that woman from the cottage if you hadn't come, but she cooks for them, and it's not for them to hear.

Tell it.

You'll let me say it through. I've not the wind to say it twice.

I'll let you.

The old man shut his eyes. When he spoke again his voice was low and even, with the wet sound under it. He might have been telling the points of a horse.

It was the night they put the clocks back. You'll remember. You was here. You was sat in this kitchen with your good boots on, waiting, same as me.

I remember.

She'd said one o'clock. She'd said she'd tell him to his face at half past twelve and leave at one, and I wasn't to come to the house nor yet to meet her. She said, I'll walk off that island on my own two feet, Amos, and you'll stand on your wall and watch me come. She was a proud woman. I liked that in her. I'd liked it for ten year.

He breathed. The man waited.

I went out at half past twelve. I'd the big lantern. I'd the bay mare saddled, this one's dam, in case she was tired. I stood on the top of the ramp. It was a clear night with a bit of moon and the tide was right out. I could see the house. And at one o'clock, to the minute by the church, I see a light come out of the garden door.

It was her. I knew the way it moved. She always walked quick, like she was late for something. It come down across the grass and through the orchard and out onto the Wade. And it come on.

I never took my eyes off it. I thought, that's her. That's Daisy. She's done it. In a quarter of an hour she'll be stood here, and I'll never have to watch her go back across that road again.

The old man's fingers tightened.

She got past the Rill. She got past the Perch. I could see her shape. I could near enough see the colour of her coat. I put my hand up to shout to her.

And there come another light.

It come out of the house like something let off a chain. It was running. It come down onto the Wade after her, and it was going up and down, up and down, and I knew what that was. There's only ever been one on the Ness that runs like that. He'd broke his knee at football when he was a boy and it never set right.

Edmund Vane, the man said.

Young Edmund. He'd a skinful. I could hear him before I could see him. He was bawling her name. Not her name. He was shouting Ma. A man of thirty-two.

He caught her. A hundred and fifty yard off from me. I could have thrown a stone. And they stood.

* * *

The range ticked. A coal fell.

I started down the ramp, the old man said. I'd took three steps. And I stopped.

Why?

She'd told me. You stand and watch me come. I thought, if I go out there, I'm the tenant, and he's the squire's son, and drunk, and there'll be blows. And she'll not thank me for it. She'd deal with him. She'd dealt with him all his life. I thought, she'll send the boy home with a flea in his ear and come on.

So I stood. I held the lantern up over my head, so she'd see where I was.

I stood there an hour.

I could hear them. I couldn't hear words. The wind was off the land. I could hear him, loud, and then her, low, and then him again. It went on and on. Once I see his light go down, like he'd sat on the ground. Then it come up.

And the tide turned. You know how you feel it. I felt it in my feet. I looked down the creek and it was running in.

I shouted then. I shouted, Daisy. I shouted, the water. I shouted till I'd no voice. I don't know if she heard. I'd say she did. I see her light move. It come toward me. It come ten yard. Twenty.

Then it stopped. And it turned round.

The old man opened his eyes. He was not looking at his son. He was looking at the window, where the lamp stood on the sill.

It went back. Toward the house. Away from me. The other one was ahead of it, going the same way, all over the road. I watched her light go back along the Wade as far as the Rill. And there it went out.

The water come over the road about ten minutes after. I stood on the ramp till it was at my knees. I'd have gone in. I've thought since that I should have. But I'd no boat and I can't swim, and the mare wouldn't face it.

They found her on the mud at nine.

The man knelt on the bricks. His wrist had gone numb in the grip. He did not try to move it.

I've lain awake on that for twenty year, his father said. Every night. I've lain in that room up there and gone over it. Why'd she turn?

She'd got by him. She was coming to me. She'd twenty yard on him and he was too drunk to run. She'd only to keep on. I'd have had her up on the bay and off that ramp before the water was over her shoes.

And she turned back.

So I've asked myself, was she turned? Did he get hold of her? Did that drunken young devil lay hands on his mother and drag her back toward that house, to stop her, and the tide caught the pair of them, and he got out and she never? I've thought that. God forgive me, Tom, I've hoped it. I've prayed it was that.

Because the other is this. That she stood out there an hour with her son crying at her. And she looked at my light on the one hand and that house on the other. And at the last, with the water coming, she thought better of it. She chose them. She'd picked up her feet to come to me and she put them down again the other way.

His voice had gone to almost nothing.

I never knew which. That's all. I never knew if she was coming or going when it took her. I've farmed this ground twenty year since and paid my rent to that family, and looked at that house every morning. I've never once been able to ask.

You could have asked him.

A Garrow, ask a Vane? Go up to that door with my cap in my hand and say, Mr Edmund, sir, did you kill your mother or did she change her mind? He'd have had me off the farm by the quarter day. And I'd not leave it. Not while she was in that churchyard. I'd my reasons to stop.

He turned his head on the pillow. He looked at his son. It was the first time he had looked straight at him since the hat.

You'll ask him.

Dad.

You've nothing to lose by it. You've a place out there. You've no rent to pay. He's nothing to you. You'll go over to that house when I'm in the ground, and you'll stand in front of Edmund Vane, and you'll say, which was it. And you'll make him tell you.

How do I make him?

That's your affair. You've been twenty year among cattlemen. I'd think you'd have learned something. The old man's mouth moved. There's my gun in the corner. He always minded a gun. He was gun-shy as a boy; I've seen him flinch at a rook-scarer.

And when he's told me?

Then you'll know. One of us will. He shut his eyes. That's all I've stopped for. I'd like one Garrow to know if she was coming.

* * *

He did not speak again that had sense in it. Toward five he said a name, twice. It was not the man's name or his mother's. It was Daisy.

He went about six. It was getting grey in the window. The man was still on his knees. He felt the grip on his wrist go, a finger at a time, like a knot working loose. He stayed where he was a while after.

Then he got up. His legs had set. He went to the window and turned down the lamp on the sill. It had burned there all night. He supposed it had burned there every night for twenty years, though the old man had never said so, and Dolly had never written it.

He looked out. The tide was in. It lay from the wall to the island, flat and grey, with the first light on it. The house stood on the other side with its chimneys.

He stood and looked at it.

He had a thing now that he had not had the day before. He had carried one question out of that country in 1974, and it was why a girl had stood behind a curtain. He had thought he knew the answer. He had thought it was his father. He had gone to the other side of the world to get away from it.

Now he had another, and it was not his own. It had been put on him. He could feel it on his wrist where the fingers had been.

He was not a man who had ever looked for trouble. He had worked for wages on other men's ranges. He had stood a good deal from foremen. He had seen men in that country settle things with their hands and once with a rifle, across a fence line, over water, and he had thought them fools.

But he looked at the house and he thought about a lantern held up for an hour. He thought about a man of thirty-two bawling for his mother on a road with the tide making. He thought about twenty years of rent.

Somebody came into the kitchen behind him. It was the child. She was in her night things, with a coat over, and her feet bare. She stood and looked at the chair.

He's dead, she said.

Yes.

She came and stood by the man at the window. She did not cry. She had done her crying two years back and she was careful with it.

Is that the house where the lady drowned, she said.

Who told you that?

Mrs Pask. I asked her. She said a lady went out on that road and the sea got her. She said it was a long time ago and I wasn't to go on it by myself.

That's right. You're not to.

Did you know her? The lady?

To look at.

Did Grandpa?

The man put his hand on the child's head. Her hair was thick and black and it had not been brushed.

Yes, he said. He knew her.

* * *

They buried him on the Thursday.

It was a clear day with a cold wind. There were forty people in the church. They were farm people and village people, in their dark clothes, and they looked at the man sideways when he came in. He had no suit. He wore what he had come in, and he had the hat in his hand. The child walked beside him in a blue coat that Dolly Pask had found.

He carried the coffin at the head end. It weighed nothing. The other three were men he had been at school with. One of them said, at the lych gate, low, You're back, then, Tom. He said, For this. The other said, You'll have heard about the farm. He said he had heard nothing.

He found out what the man meant when he got home.

They put the old man down on the north side, under the wall. The vicar read the words. He was a soft pink deaf old man and he read them well. The man stood with his hat against his chest. He was looking at the next grave but two.

It was a plain stone, grey, with moss in the letters. *Marguerite Vane. 1920 – 1974.* There was nothing else on it. Somebody had put flowers there, not lately. They had gone brown in the jar.

His father had bought the plot he lay in the year after. Dolly had told him so. He had gone to the vicar and paid for it in notes. He had wanted to be near.

When it was done the people went away down the path. A few shook his hand. The child had gone ahead with Dolly. He stayed.

He stood at the foot of the two graves. The churchyard wall was low there, and beyond it the ground fell to the marsh. He could see across the flats. The tide was out and the Wade lay bare and brown. He could see the island.

He looked at the house. He stood with his hat on now, since the wind was cold, and his hands in the pockets of his coat. He looked for a long time. He was looking at one window on the upper floor, on the west side. It had a curtain. He knew that it was foolish. She was four hundred miles off. Dolly had said so.

There was somebody on the sea wall of the island.

He saw it after a while. It was a figure, small with the distance, standing on the bank at the place where the Wade came ashore. It was a man. He was big. He stood with his feet apart and one hand up to his face, and something winked in the hand. He had a glass.

The man in the churchyard did not move. He stood by his father's grave and let himself be looked at.

They stood so, the two of them, with half a mile of mud between. Neither lifted a hand. Then the figure on the wall lowered the glass. It turned, and went along the top of the bank toward the trees. It walked with a hitch.

The man watched it out of sight.

He went back to Dolly Pask's cottage, where the child was. She had the kettle on. There was a letter on her kitchen table, a long blue envelope with a printed name in the corner. It had come to the farm by the second post while they were at the church, and she had carried it across.

He opened it standing. He read it. It was one page. It was from a firm of solicitors in the county town, acting for the Vane Estate. It regretted to inform the personal representatives of the late Mr A. Garrow that the tenancy of Saltcote Farm, being an annual tenancy, would not be renewed. It required vacant possession of the house, buildings and land on the twenty-fifth day of March next. It was signed by a man called Fenn.

He read it a second time. The child was watching him from the hearth.

What is it, Dad?

A paper.

What does it say?

It says we're to get off.

Off where?

Off this ground.

But it's Grandpa's.

No, the man said. It never was.

He folded the paper in three, and in three again. He had an oilcloth packet inside his shirt, where he kept a thing of his own. He put the paper in with it.

He did not say anything else. Dolly Pask was looking at him from the range, with the teapot in her hand. She had the look of a woman who would rather he had shouted.

He went out and saw to the mare.

## I. Of Time, and of the Act

§ 1. I, Leopold Vane, of Compline House in the County of Essex, horologist, being in my eighty-second year, and of sound hands if not always of sound recollection, desire to set down a true relation of an experiment which I conducted in my own house on the night of the twenty-second of October last, and of its result.

I do so for three reasons. The first is that the result was positive. The second is that I am aware that it will not be believed, and I should wish there to be a record made by the experimenter himself, while the particulars are fresh, against the day when some better-furnished person may repeat it. The third reason I shall give at the end, if I have not by then forgotten it.

I am dictating this to Miss Quaile, who attended me on the night in question and was a witness to the greater part. I have asked her to write down what I say and nothing else. She is a truthful young woman. She does not believe that the experiment succeeded. I have told her that she is not required to believe it, only to spell it correctly.

§ 2. I should begin by stating what I am, since the reader is entitled to know what kind of instrument has made the observations.

I was born in this house in the year 1913. My father kept the clocks here, as his father had, and at the age of fifteen I was sent to Clerkenwell, and afterward to Le Locle in Switzerland, to learn the trade properly. I have worked at the bench for sixty-six years. I have restored, I suppose, some four hundred clocks and watches of the first rank, among them nine by Tompion and the Thorne Sidereal, of which I shall have more to say. I have made forty-three of my own, all copies, since I had never any gift for invention and knew it. I am a Fellow of the British Horological Institute and was for some years on its council, until I found that I could no longer follow the minutes.

I mention these things without vanity. I mention them in order that it may be understood that I am not a philosopher, nor a mathematician, nor any sort of visionary. I am a mechanic. I have spent my life persuading small pieces of brass to do the same thing at the same rate for as long as possible. If I now put forward a claim that sounds extravagant, I should like it to be remembered that it is put forward by a man whose whole training has been against extravagance, and who would not accept a quarter-second's error in a going train without knowing the cause.

§ 3. I must also state a defect.

My memory is no longer continuous. I have been told that this is a disease of the vessels of the brain, and I have no reason to doubt it. I do not experience it as a disease. I experience it as follows. I shall be in a room, engaged upon some ordinary matter, and without any interval I shall be in another room, or the same room in another year. I am not remembering that year. I am in it. The light is as it was. I can read the date on a newspaper. Persons who have been dead for a long time come in and speak to me about the business of that day, and I answer them, and find that I know what to say.

Then I am back. Miss Quaile will be looking at me. She tells me that I have been silent for a minute or two, or that I have called her by another name.

I call this leakage. I use the word in its workshop sense. In a clock, power is meant to pass from the spring through the train to the escapement, by one path, tooth by tooth. Where a pivot is worn, or a wheel is out of depth, some of the power escapes sideways and is lost. The clock goes on. But it no longer keeps its place in time. I am an old movement, and my pivots are worn. Something in me which ought to hold each hour in its proper order, one tooth after the next, has begun to let them slip past one another.

I state this for two reasons. It will explain certain gaps in my relation, which I shall mark honestly where they fall. And it has a bearing upon the experiment itself, as will appear. For it was in consequence of this infirmity that I first came to consider whether the order of the hours is so fixed a thing as I had been brought up to think.

§ 4. I now come to the question of what time is. I shall be brief, since I do not understand it, and nor does anyone.

A clockmaker has a view of the matter which differs from the common one. The common person supposes that there is a thing called the time, which flows of itself, evenly, like a river, and that a clock is a kind of window through which one looks at it. We in the trade know better. A clock does not show the time. A clock makes it. There is a spring, or a weight, which would run down all at once in a rush if it were let. There is an escapement, which stops it; and lets it go by one tooth; and stops it again. Tick. And tock. Each of those is a small quantity of power, released and arrested. The hands go round because they are pushed. What you read upon the dial is not a view of anything outside the case. It is a count of how many times the thing inside has been let go.

It follows that there is not one time. There are as many as there are escapements. I have a hundred and eleven in this house, and I can say upon my honour, having regulated every one, that no two have ever agreed for an entire day together. I have had them within a second. I have never had them the same.

Which, then, is right? The question was put to me by Mrs Edmund Vane on the afternoon before the experiment, in my hall. I am told that I answered, They are all right. I do not recollect it. But it was a good answer. They are each of them a faithful record of their own letting-go.

§ 5. There is, however, one clock that we did not make.

The earth turns. The sun comes to the meridian of a place, and that is noon at that place. It is the oldest escapement and the only one that no man can regulate. All our brass is an attempt to keep step with it. A good clock is one that says twelve when the sun stands due south over the roof under which that clock is going, and says it again after one turn of the earth, and so on.

That is the true time. I say so without apology. It is the time of the place. It differs from Greenwich to Bristol by ten minutes, and from my bench to the church at Thorney by about two seconds, and in the days before the railways every town in England kept its own and was right to. I do not say that it is convenient. I say that it is true, in the plain sense that it corresponds to something which is the case.

The sun, I should add for completeness, is not himself an even timekeeper. He runs fast in some months and slow in others, by reason of the shape of the earth's path and the tilt of her axis, so that the noon of the sundial and the noon of a well-regulated clock may differ by as much as sixteen minutes. That difference is called the equation of time. It is tabulated for every day of the year. The Thorne Sidereal shows it upon a subsidiary dial, by a kidney-shaped cam which the maker cut by hand, and which I spent the winter of 1957 learning to copy. I mention it here because I shall want it later. The reader should hold in his mind that a regulator and a sundial may stand side by side, and disagree by a quarter of an hour, and both be perfectly correct.

§ 6. I come to the Act.

In the year 1916, when I was three years old, Parliament passed a measure by which every clock in the kingdom was to be advanced one hour upon a certain night in spring, and put back one hour upon a certain night in autumn. It had been urged for some years by a Mr Willett, a builder, of Chislehurst, who was fond of riding before breakfast and was grieved to see the blinds drawn in the houses that he passed. He desired that his fellow countrymen should have more of the morning. As they would not rise earlier of their own will, he proposed that they should be deceived into it.

I wish to be fair to Mr Willett. He was, I believe, a kindly man, and he died before his scheme was adopted. But I ask the reader to consider what it is that he did.

He did not add one minute of light to the day. No Act can. The sun rose and set exactly as before. What was changed was only the number that men agreed to call it. From the last Sunday of March, forty million people look at the sun standing at eleven and say with one voice that it is noon. They know that it is not. There is not a clock in the country that agrees with the sky. And they have consented to this, year upon year, so readily that most of them have forgotten that it is being done.

I have been called obstinate upon this point. My wife called me so. I do not think it is obstinacy to decline to say a thing that one knows to be false, merely because one's neighbours have agreed to say it. But I did not always decline. I should set that down. For fifty-eight years I kept the Act in this house, as a good citizen. I changed the clocks, all of them, twice a year, with my own hand. My wife made a festival of the autumn change, of which I shall speak. I held my tongue and moved the hands.

§ 7. I ask the reader now to attend closely, since it is from this that the whole experiment proceeds.

When the clocks are advanced in spring, an hour is passed over. The hands go from one to two. The hour between is not lived. No one in England has an appointment in it, or is born in it, or dies. If you look in a parish register you will find no entry against it. It is left out.

When the clocks are put back in autumn, an hour is had twice. The hands go from one to two, and are returned to one, and go to two again. A child may be born at half past one and his twin brother forty minutes later at ten minutes past. It has happened. The registrars have a form for it.

Now I am a mechanic, and I was brought up to believe that nothing is had for nothing. If I take a tooth off a wheel, the train loses by exactly that tooth. If I let down a spring, the power that was in it has gone somewhere. I cannot make a movement that gives out more than was put in. Nobody can.

So I asked myself a question. It took me, I am ashamed to say, until I was sixty-one years of age and had a reason to ask it. *Where is the hour kept?*

It is taken in March. It is given back in October. For seven months it is not upon any dial. But it is given back; that is not in dispute; every clock in the kingdom receives it. A thing cannot be returned which has ceased to exist. It follows that between March and October the hour is somewhere. It is held over. It is, if I may use a term of the counting-house, on deposit.

I put this to my solicitor, Mr Fenn, some years ago. He said that it was a manner of speaking. He said that an hour was not a thing, and that one could not owe it or bank it; that it was only a convention about numbers.

I asked him whether he would say the same of a pound.

He did not reply. But I have thought since that it was a fair question. A pound is also a convention about numbers. One cannot hold it in the hand, nor say where it is when it is in a ledger. Nevertheless it may be lent, and owed, and held over, and paid back, and a man may be ruined for want of it. I do not see that an hour is less real than a pound. I have found it a good deal more so.

§ 8. I must now speak of a private matter. I do so with reluctance and only because the experiment cannot be understood without it.

My wife's name was Marguerite. I called her Margot. We were married in the spring of 1948, and I had the keeping of her for twenty-six years, and did not keep her well.

She was a person of great life. I am not. I have often thought that it was like housing a bird in a clock case. She was patient of it. She made the house laugh. She had a way of teasing me about my accuracy which I pretended to mind. I have learned since her death that she used to move the hands of the gallery clock forward a little every Sunday while I was at church, in order to watch me try to account for the gain. I spent eleven years upon that movement. I took it down four times. I am told that she would stand in the door of the Shop with her hand over her mouth.

I should give a great deal to be so deceived again.

It was she who made the Hour. I must explain that. On the night of the autumn change, when the hour came round the second time, she held that it was a gift, and that it was bad manners not to use a present. She said that the first time through was for the world and the second was ours. She gathered us in the hall, the children when they were old enough, and whoever was dining. She would have me open the front door upon the stroke, to let it in. And then she laid down a rule, which was that for that hour anyone might say to anyone else whatever was true, and it should not be held against them afterward. It was to be put back with the clocks.

I thought it a game. I thought that she did it for the children. I see now that she did it for me. She had married a man who could not be told things. She made one hour in the year when I might be, and stood in the middle of my hall and waited. I never once asked her anything in it. I danced with her. I was pleased with the precision of the arrangement.

§ 9. In the year 1974 the change fell upon the night of the twenty-sixth of October.

I shall set down what I did that day without excuse. It was a labour to alter the clocks; there were by then a hundred and four in the house. It had been my custom to do it at the Hour itself, with the family helping, each taking a room. That year I was tired. I had a movement on the bench which I wished to finish. I thought that I would get the business over. And so in the afternoon, between four and six, I went through the house by myself and put every clock back.

I told my wife. I recollect it. I went into her room, where she was dressing, and said that I had done the clocks already, to save a scramble. She looked at me in her glass. She said, 'Oh, Leo. You would.' I took it for one of her jokes.

So that from six o'clock that evening this house stood upon winter time, one hour behind the rest of England, which would not change until two in the morning.

At some time in the night my wife left the house and went out upon the Wade. I did not see her go. I had opened the door at the hour and had waited for her to come down the stairs, as she always did, and she did not come. I supposed that she was tired. I went to my bed.

They found her on the mud at nine in the morning.

The road had closed that night at a quarter past three, by the summer clocks that the tide tables are printed in. That is a quarter past two by the clocks of this house as I had set them. She was found a hundred yards from the refuge. She had been upon the road when the water came.

I worked it out. I am a mechanic; I work things out. She had looked at the regulator in the hall, which I had put back at four in the afternoon. It said two. She had thought, I have an hour and a quarter. She had no more than a quarter of an hour. She went out to cross a causeway of half a mile with what she took to be ample time, and the hour that she was relying upon was one that I had taken from her that afternoon with my own fingers, to save myself trouble.

I did not then ask why she was crossing. I did not ask for a long time. That is another defect and I shall come to it.

§ 10. I stopped the clock in her room. I did it with my hand, at twenty-five minutes to four in the morning of the twenty-eighth, when they had brought her home and I had seen her. I have not started it since.

And I made a resolution, which I have kept. I resolved that no clock in this house should ever again be altered for the Act. The house had been upon winter time when she died. It should stay there. In the spring, when the rest of England put its hands forward, Compline would not. In the autumn, when they came back, they would find us where they had left us.

I was told that it was madness. Edmund said so. The tradesmen could not understand our appointments. For seven months of each year my family have been obliged to add an hour in their heads to every dial under this roof, and guests give up their watches at the door. I am aware that it is a great inconvenience. I meant it to be.

For I had found that I could not bear to hear a clock in this house tell the false time. Every one of them, if it had been put forward in March, would have been saying, for seven months, the thing that killed her. I preferred that they should say what was true, and that I should be reminded each time I looked at one of what I had done. A hundred and eleven of them say it now. I have not been out of the sound of it for twenty years.

§ 11. It was some while before I perceived what followed from this.

The house had not taken the hour in the spring of 1975. It has not taken it in any spring since. It followed that the house was not owed it in the autumn. Each October, upon the night, the hour that had been held over since March was paid back to every clock in England; and it was not paid to Compline, since Compline had lent nothing.

I thought of that, at first, only as a just consequence. We had stood out of the transaction and had no claim upon it.

But I continued to keep the night. I did so for my wife's sake. Each year I opened the door at one o'clock by this house, which was two by the world; and stood there; and each year I had the same sensation. I do not ask the reader to give weight to a sensation. I record it. It was that something went past. One feels it upon a railway platform when an express goes through without stopping: the air moves, and there is a sound, and one's coat is pulled. I felt that at my own front door, twenty times, upon the stroke of one, with my family behind me in the hall. Something very large went by in the dark outside, going toward the rest of England, and did not stop for us.

It was in the fourteenth or fifteenth year that I understood what it was. It was the hour. It was being delivered.

And I thought: it passes my door. It comes as near as that. For the space of one stroke of the bell it is neither in March, where it was taken, nor in the houses where it is to be spent. It is in transit. And the world's clocks at that instant stand at two, and mine at one, and in the next instant they will stand together. There is a moment at which the difference between the false time and the true is exactly one hour, and is in the very act of being closed.

I am a maker of escapements. I know what it is to catch a thing in the instant of its letting-go. It is the whole of my art.

I began to ask whether a movement could be made which should take up that hour as it went by.

§ 12. I should say what I wanted it for. A man does not spend five years upon a piece of apparatus without an object.

I did not want an hour in general. I am eighty-one, and one hour more or less of the ordinary kind is of no consequence to me. I wanted that one. I wanted the hour between one and two upon the night of the twenty-sixth of October, 1974, as it stood upon the clocks of this house. It was the hour in which my wife went down the stairs in her stockings and looked at my regulator.

I wanted to be standing in the hall when she did. I had the words ready. I have had them ready for twenty years; I say them over at night. *Margot, the clock is wrong. I put it back before dinner. It is three, not two. You have a quarter of an hour. Do not go on foot.*

That is all. It is nineteen words. It would take me perhaps ten seconds to say them. I did not ask to have her back for good. I knew by then, though I did not allow myself to know it, that she had been going to another man, and I had no right to prevent her. I asked only to be let to tell her the time correctly. It was the one thing in the world that I was fitted to do for her, and on the night that it mattered I had done it wrong.

I state the object plainly so that the result may be judged against it. The experiment was designed to return to me one particular hour in order that I might give my wife the true state of the tide.

§ 13. I must here record a leakage, since it bears upon what follows, and since I have undertaken to mark them.

It occurred in September last. I was in the long gallery, at about four in the afternoon, regulating the clock beneath my wife's portrait. I had the hood off and my glass in my eye.

Without interval I was in the hall. It was night. The door stood open upon the gravel and the wind was coming in. All the candles were lit. I was wearing evening clothes, which I had not had on a moment before, and I was a much heavier man; I felt it in my shoulders. My son's wife was at the foot of the stairs in a yellow frock that I had forgotten. She was twenty-eight. My son was by the fire with a glass in his hand, flushed, and he was not looking at the stairs. He was looking toward the garden passage.

I knew the night at once. I looked at the regulator. It said five minutes past one.

I thought, I am here. It has happened of itself. I have been let back.

I went to the foot of the stairs. I looked up. I said, I believe aloud, 'Margot!' I had the nineteen words in my mouth.

She was not there. The stairs were empty. And I recollected, standing in that hall in 1974, what I had not known in 1974: that she had already gone. She had not come down to the hall that year at all. She had left by the garden door while I stood at the front with my hand on the bolt.

I had been returned to the night, and I had been put in the wrong room.

Then I was in the gallery, with the hood of the clock in my hand, and Miss Quaile—no. It was before she came. It was Mrs Pask, who had brought up my tea. She said that I had been standing so for some minutes and had shouted.

I set this down carefully because of what I concluded from it. I concluded two things. First, that the thing could be done; for I had been there, in the flesh, in the hour, and had felt the draught from the door. I do not accept that it was a dream. I know the difference. I have dreamed of my wife on most nights for twenty years, and in dreams I am never able to read a dial. I had read the regulator. It was five past one, and the seconds hand was moving.

And second, that it was of no use to be carried back by leakage. A leak has no direction. It had put me down in the hall at five past one, when I wanted to be at the garden door at one precisely. If the thing was to be done, it must be done by a proper train, with a known ratio, so that I should arrive where I meant and at the minute I meant, and in possession of my faculties.

I went down to the Shop that evening and began to draw.

It was on the same afternoon, or the next, that I opened the long cupboard to look for a pattern, and found that eleven of my copies were not in it.

## 7. The Quarters

I told it as he told it to me. I have given it to you already, on the Perch, and I shall not give it again. I told them about the bottle of port in the gunroom, and the two lights on the Wade. I told them that he caught her past the Perch and begged her, and that she kissed him and walked on toward a lantern held up on a wall. I told them that he sat down on the stones like a child who won't walk, and then got up and went the other way to make her sorry. And that the Rill had filled behind them while they talked, and took him.

I told them that she heard him, thirty yards from Amos Garrow's ramp, and turned round.

I told them about the ladder, which was sound then, every rung, and how she put her shoulder under her son and pushed him up it from the sea. I told them about her hand coming up out of the water with the ring in it. *Give that to your father. Tell him I'm sorry.* And that she let go of the post.

Then I gave them the nine words, as I had been asked to, thirty years late.

"*She turned back for me. Not from Amos Garrow.*"

Nobody spoke.

Then Toby said, quite softly, "*Don't look back. She looked back.*"

"He said that to Imogen in the passage," I said. "She thought he meant that their mother had looked back at the house and wavered, and been caught. He didn't. He meant that she'd looked back at him. And that it had cost her everything, and he didn't want it to cost his sister. He said it to me too, on the post. He said, *people in this family look back, and it never does them any good. You're not one of us. You walk straight on.*"

"Did you?" said Celia.

"I walked to the lights. I didn't look for him."

She nodded. She looked at the empty place at the head of the table, at the red diary by the fork.

"She didn't leave Leo," she said slowly. "Not in the end. And she didn't change her mind about Amos. She did neither. She was on her way, and she heard her boy, and she went back into the sea." She was turning her ring. "And he let them all think it was something else. Leo thought it was his clock. Immy thought he dragged her. Amos thought she'd chosen the house. Twenty years. All because he couldn't bear anyone to know that she'd got clear and come back for him."

"Because he'd sat down," said Hester. "On the road. And wouldn't walk."

"Yes."

"And then he stood in the same water, thirty years later." Hester's voice was very low. "For a child who'd come after her father. And held the post. And let go."

I had not put it like that. I had never let myself.

"Yes," I said.

"So he did what she did," said Hester Quaile. "In the same place. On the same night. I said that the Wade was owed one. I took that down from Leo, and I thought it was the worst thing he ever said." She looked at the Vulliamy, going on the table in front of her. "It wasn't a debt. He paid it back. He'd been carrying it for twenty years in a waistcoat pocket and he found somebody to give it to."

* * *

It was half past eleven by the radio clock. The others had gone through to the hall. Gail had built up the fire. I had lit candles, fifty of them, on the clock hoods and the stairs, as I had seen them through the hatch at nine years old, and the stopped clocks stood round the walls in the moving light with their pendulums hanging.

I had one more thing to show them, and I had kept it till last because I was afraid of it.

I had carried it through from the car that afternoon in a box with tin corners. I am not above that sort of thing. I put it on the hall table beside the Font and took off the lid.

There was a grey blanket. There was a pillowcase.

Toby made a sound.

"No," he said. "No, you haven't. Not the—"

I drew the pillowcase off upward, in one sweep.

There it stood. The big white dial, and the five small ones, and the painted moon looking out of its slot. It was not going. It had not gone since about 1999, I think.

It had taken me most of the summer. Ronnie Sears died in 2008, in a hospice in Westcliff, and his effects went to a saleroom in Southend in two hundred lots. Lot 117 was described as *an English table regulator in the eighteenth-century manner, probably 1960s, craft revival, with lunar and tidal indication, not working.* It fetched four hundred and twenty pounds. The dealer who bought it sold it to a man in Kent, who kept it in his conservatory, where the moon work rusted. I found him through the trade, in July. He was glad to see the back of it. He said it had always given him the creeps; he said it seemed to know something.

"The Copy," said Kit.

"The Copy."

Celia had come to stand at the table. She was holding the edge of it with one hand.

"That's the one I sold," she said. "In the car park. To Ronnie Sears. For sixty thousand pounds in a bag." She looked at it for a long time. "I thought it was worth a million. Harry thought so too. Leo told us both it was a copy and we neither of us believed him."

"It's worth more than the Thorne," I said. "To this table."

"Why?"

I took the key out of my pocket.

I had kept it wrapped in the notebook page since November 1994. It is about three inches long, of steel, with a bow pierced in the shape of a sprig of thorn. Leopold Vane had given it to his son in the tower at five to two, and told him to put it in his coat pocket and not the waistcoat. Edmund had put it in the dinner jacket. The dinner jacket had come off in the Rill, and been buttoned inside my coat, and worn by me on the Perch for fifty minutes, and rolled up and pushed under a bed in Wade Cottage by an old woman who did not know what to do with it.

"Leo says this fits nothing else," I said. "He was wrong. He forgot what his copies were. He said himself there was no measurement that would tell the one from the other. He made it exact."

I opened the little door in the back of the case. I found the winding square. I put the key on it.

It fitted.

I turned it. I felt the spring take up. I have wound a great many clocks; it is part of my work, though the ones at Teddington do not have keys. I had never felt one like that. It took up smooth and even, as though it had been waiting.

The clock did not go. The moon work was jammed; I had known it would be. The cock that carries it was cut too light in the winter of 1964, and somebody had laid it on its side.

"There's a thing he asked for," I said. "Leo. At the end. He asked that whoever opened this should look at the moon work, and know whose it is."

Hester was holding something out to me. It was a jeweller's loupe on a black cord, the cord gone grey with age.

"He left it on the bench," she said. "I took it, when I went. I didn't know why."

I gave it to Celia.

She looked at it in her hand. Then she put it into her eye, as she must have seen her father-in-law do ten thousand times, and bent over the open back of the case, and looked at the lunar train that her husband had cut between November 1973 and the eighth of February 1974, by himself, at the age of thirty-one, in eleven weeks.

She looked for a long time. Nobody spoke.

"I can't tell," she said at last. Her voice was not steady. "I never could tell a good one from a bad one. It all looks like little wheels to me." She took the loupe out. "Is it good?"

"His father says it's the best piece of wheel-cutting he saw in sixty-six years at the bench. He says it's better than Thorne's."

"Did he ever tell Edmund that?"

"Once. In the tower. At about ten to two. I heard him."

Celia put the loupe down very carefully on the table, beside the Copy.

"Well," she said. "That's something. That's one thing that got said in this house before it was too late." She looked round at all of us. "I'd like it said I sold it. I'd like that known. Not hidden. Leo said whoever opens it should know whose it is. It's his. And it was mine to sell, and I sold it, and I'm sorry. I'd like that to count."

"It counts," I said.

* * *

At five to one by the regulator, which was five to two by the radio clock and by everyone's telephone, I went down the hall to the front door.

I had the Vulliamy on the table behind me, going. I had the kitchen clock, eight minutes fast, beside it. I had the little radio clock in my hand. The radio clock would change by itself at two. It would get a signal from Cumbria, from my laboratory, from the room where the caesium fountains are, and it would take its hands back one hour, and agree with the house. I have spent twenty years making sure that it does that correctly, for fifty million people, while they sleep.

I had never before stood at a door and watched it happen.

They were behind me in the hall. Toby with his wife. Kit and Saskia on the bottom stair, where they had sat thirty years ago with hay in their hair. Gail by the fire, with her aunt's cake untouched on a plate. Hester at my left, where she had stood at the old man's elbow. Celia in the big chair, in Octavia's place, with Harry Cade's box on her knee.

Nobody had told them what to do. Nobody had needed to.

I put my hand on the bolt.

I want to tell you what I thought. I am a scientist, and I keep the time, and I know what the Act is and what it is not. I knew that nothing was going to come in at that door. An hour is not a thing. It is not kept anywhere between March and October. At one o'clock by the house a label would change on fifty million clocks and that would be all.

And I thought of a man who had made a machine to catch it. He knew perfectly well, on one day in four, that it could not be done, and he had sent the hour back to his wife all the same. I thought of a woman who had stood in the middle of this hall every year and told her family that they had been given something, so that they would behave as though they had. And when they did, they had.

I thought that the old man was right. I spend my life keeping one kind of time, and there is another, and they run at different rates. And on certain nights they stand for an hour together.

The clocks were stopped. There was nobody to strike in the house.

So the tower began.

I heard the whirr, and the clank, across the gravel. And then the little bell, which I had heard last from a yard away in the clock room, beside a wooden man in a dinner jacket. *Ting-tang.* The Jack was back on his pin. *Ting-tang. Ting-tang. Ting-tang.*

I drew the bolt. I opened both leaves of the door and folded them back against the stone.

The night came in. All the candles nearest the door lay over and some went out. The moon was up. The gravel was white. Across the yard I could see the tower, and the clock face, and under the little roof the two of them: the small man on the left, with his arm coming down from the quarters, and on the right the tall grey woman with her hammer up by her shoulder.

She brought it down.

One.

In my hand, at the same instant, the little radio clock went back an hour, by itself, without a sound. It said one. The Vulliamy behind me said one.

For the first time since the old man died, every clock at Compline that was going said the same thing as the rest of England.

I said, "Come in, then." I did not know I was going to say it. "You're late. You've been late since the last Sunday in March."

* * *

The Odd Hour. The family had a rule about it. What was said in it did not count. It was put back with the clocks at two, and nobody would hold you to it.

I said at the beginning of this book that every person in it was relying on that rule, and that it was not true. It all counted. That is the thing I am surest of.

So I made a different rule. I said it standing in the open door with my back to the yard. I said that what was said in this hour would count. It would be written down. Nobody would put it back.

They said things.

I am not going to give you all of them. Some of them were said to people who were not there. Toby went and stood in front of the empty place in the dining room for a while by himself, and when he came back his wife took his arm and they went and sat on the stairs. Kit and Celia talked by the fire for a long time, very low. He had his hands out of his pockets. I saw her put her hand on his head, once, and leave it there. Gail cut the cake.

Hester did not say anything to anybody for most of the hour. She stood at the door with me and looked at the tower. Then, at about half past one, she said, without turning her head:

"I'd like to know about the child. In the nursery. What she was singing."

"'Four Strong Winds,'" I said. "It's a Canadian song. My mother used to sing it. I only knew the first two lines."

"Will you sing them?"

So I did. I stood in the open door of Compline House at half past one in the morning, at thirty-nine, with a radio clock in my hand, and sang the first two lines of a song my mother taught me, to a woman who had heard them down a chimney thirty years before and had never been able to believe her ears.

She listened with her eyes shut.

"Yes," she said. "That's it. That's exactly it. It went up, and came down, and went up again in the same place."

"I'm sorry I frightened you."

"You didn't frighten me," Hester said. "I was frightened already. You were the only real thing I heard all night."

* * *

At a quarter to two, by every clock that was going, I went out of the house. Nobody stopped me.

I went down through the orchard to the gate in the sea wall. The tide was going out; it had turned a little before midnight. The road was coming up out of the water, black and wet, with the withies standing either side, and in the middle, at the dip, on its four legs, the Perch.

I had had it rebuilt in the spring. Imogen asked me to before she died; she said that it had wanted seeing to since Kit was at school. It has a new ladder, of oak, with every rung bolted through, and a handrail. There is a small brass plate on the door. I had it put there in August. It has his name on it, and two dates, and under them, in smaller letters: *He went in after a child.*

I stood at the gate where he had sat down with his head between his knees. I did not go out. I am not a fool about that water. I have never set foot on the Wade at night since, and never will.

The tower struck two behind me. I heard the Jack do the quarters first, all four, and then the grey woman twice.

The Hour was over. Nothing was put back.

I thought of the six of them. They were all of them right, and they all of them contradicted one another. A detective found a body, and was right. A wine merchant carried the same body across a yard, snoring, and was right. A nurse saw a ghost and a daughter put on a dress, and they were both right. A man shot a murderer off a tower and it was made of oak. An old man killed his son and then did not, and I have checked the old man's arithmetic, and it is sound. And none of them lied. Every one of them carried his own piece of that night to the grave, or near it, believing that it was the whole. Nobody ever laid the pieces side by side, because each of them was ashamed of his own.

And the one man who could have laid them side by side in a minute, any time he liked, was ashamed of his too. He carried it in a waistcoat pocket for twenty years. Then on the last night he cleared his pockets. He gave a ring to his sister, and the truth to his father, and a key to a child he had known for three hours. He told the child nine words to carry, and stood in the sea while she counted.

I have tried to carry them. I was thirty years late. They were his words, and they had the family weight in them, and they came out of the water.

The tide was going down fast now. The Rill was showing. In a few minutes the road would be open, all the way across, to the lights on the far side, where the farm is that is mine now, with the lamp in the kitchen window. I light it every night I am there. My father did, and his father before him. Imogen did, and I think now that I know who she was lighting it for.

I stood at the gate a long time.

Then I turned round and went back up through the orchard toward the house, where the door was standing open and the candles were lit, and they were all waiting for me in the hall, and all the clocks agreed.

Leopold Vane said that the hour is given back every year, and that you will be asleep. He said that you should be awake for it. He said that there will very likely be somebody in your house to whom you have not said a thing that you ought.

He was a horologist, and he was wrong about a great many things. I have checked. He was right about that.

It comes on the last Saturday night of October, in the small hours. You need no engine. Open a door.

Somebody is standing behind you at the gallery clock. They have been there five minutes.

Turn round.

## V. The Gallery

The narrow stair from the top floor comes down beside the head of the main staircase. One stands there on a square landing, with the well of the hall on one hand and on the other the mouth of the long gallery.

I had the lamp. I had come down slowly, since my knees were not to be trusted. When I reached the landing I set the lamp on the bottom tread behind me, to have my hands free for the rail. So I was standing in my own shadow when I turned.

All through the house the clocks were telling midnight. I have said how they did it, each in its own time. The small quick one in the morning room had begun, and the hall clocks were taking it up, one by one, beneath me. In the gallery the cracked one had not yet spoken.

I looked along the gallery. I did so because I was afraid of it, and I had been taught as a child to look at what I am afraid of.

There was a light at the far end.

It was small and yellow, a single flame, and it was low down by the end wall, where the door of the Grey Room is. That door had been shut and locked for the whole of the three weeks that I had been in the house. I had seen the old man lay his hand on it every night.

The light moved. It came out from the wall into the middle of the gallery, and it began to come toward me.

I did not move. I do not think that I breathed. The gallery is a hundred feet long and the floor sags in the middle, so that the light seemed to come down a slope and up again. As it came I began to see what carried it.

First there was the gown. It was grey, and it shone. It took the candle flame all down its length and gave it back in long soft folds, like water running over a sill. I knew that grey. I had stood before it on twenty nights and tried to move out of the way of the eyes above it.

Then the bare shoulders, and an arm, lifted, holding a candle in a silver stick. The other hand hung at her side and had something in it. I saw what it was as she came nearer. It was a pair of shoes. She was carrying her shoes by the heels and walking in her stockings, and her feet made no sound at all on the old boards.

Then I saw the face.

She passed me at a distance of perhaps four yards. She did not turn her head. She was looking straight in front of her, at something a long way off, and her lips were parted, as if she had just said a thing or was about to. The candle was on the far side of her from me, so that her profile was dark against it and edged with light. I saw the line of her brow and her cheek. I saw the eye nearest to me, and it was grey. Her dark hair was piled up.

As she passed, the cracked clock at the end of the gallery, under the empty stretch of wall where the candle no longer reached, struck its first note.

As the clocks of Compline told midnight I saw Marguerite Vane come down the long gallery in her grey silk, and she had been twenty years in Thorney churchyard.

I have written that sentence as I first wrote it, in pencil, on the back of a temperature chart, two days afterward, when I could hold a pencil. I am not going to alter it now.

She went by. There is a second stair at my end of the gallery, a back one, that goes down to the garden passage and the door to the orangery. She turned to it. I saw the grey skirt gather and slip round the corner, and the light go down the wall after it. I heard nothing. I did not hear a step or a board.

There was a scent in the air where she had been. It was lavender, and camphor, and something else underneath, sweet and old, like a drawer that has not been opened for many years.

* * *

I shall now say what a sensible woman would conclude, as I have undertaken to do. Dr Imrie tells me that I am not obliged to. But I should think less of myself if I did not.

There was a living woman in that house who had those eyes. I have said so. I had met her the day before, and been startled. She was of the same height and build and colouring as the woman in the portrait, since she was her daughter. She had a key to the Grey Room; I had been told that on my first morning. She had been standing at the drawing-room window half an hour earlier in a state of great agitation, and she had left that room directly after I did.

A sensible woman will say that Miss Imogen Vane went upstairs and unlocked her mother's bedroom. She took her mother's gown out of the wardrobe and put it on, and came down the gallery carrying her shoes so as to make no noise. I did not see the white lock in her hair, but the candle was on the other side.

I have been told, since, by several people, that this is what happened. Miss Vane has told me so herself. She wrote me a kind letter in the spring, when she heard that I was ill. She said that she was very sorry to have frightened me, and that she had not known anyone was on the landing. She had had a reason for wearing the dress that night which had nothing to do with me.

I believe that she believes it. I have no wish to quarrel with her.

I can only say what it is that I am unable to get past. I had seen Miss Vane's face at dinner, and in the drawing room, and I should have known it anywhere. It is a closed, careful face. It is the face of a woman who has spent her life looking closely at things and keeping her opinion to herself. The face that passed me in the gallery was not careful. It was open, and alight, and a little amused, with the lips apart. It was the face in the portrait. I had studied that face more closely than I have ever studied a living one, and I saw it go by me in profile at four yards, by candlelight, and it had not changed by a line.

And she was carrying her shoes. Gail had told me, on my first morning, what the woman in the portrait had done on the night she died. *She'd took them off. She went out to the Wade in her stockings.* I did not know then that it was written in the red book, in her own hand. *I shall go in my stockings; the Wade eats shoes.* Miss Vane cannot have read that. No one had read it but her brother and I.

I do not say that there are no explanations. A daughter may walk as her mother walked. A woman of forty-four may look, for a moment, by one candle, on one night, as her mother looked at thirty-five. I know what grief and candlelight will do to a face.

But I will say this for my own judgment. I was not, at that moment, looking for a ghost. I had just come down from a room where I had found a warm bed and no child. I was thinking of a missing bottle and a missing old man, and of a man drugged and out of doors. If I had been asked what I most wanted to see in that gallery, I should have said Mr Vane, in his dressing gown, winding a clock. I had no thought to spare for the dead. And she came down the gallery to me all the same.

* * *

When I could move, I took up the lamp and went to the end of the gallery.

I did not want to. I went because of the door. I had seen the light come from it, and I had to know.

The portrait was in its place. I held the lamp up to it. I had been half afraid, I think, that I should find the frame empty. It was not empty. She stood there in her grey gown with her hand on the chair, as she had stood since 1956.

I looked at her for a long while. The paint was only paint. The silk had no light of its own, but was a flat grey-white, laid on with a brush, and I could see the strokes. The eyes did not follow me. I moved from one side to the other, with the lamp, and they stayed where the painter had put them, looking at a point in the middle of the floor.

It was a picture of a woman. For twenty nights it had been something else, and now it had gone back to being a picture. I can only put it as I felt it then. It was like looking in at the window of a house and knowing, from the stillness, that the person who lives there has gone out.

The door of the Grey Room stood open. There was a key in the lock, an old iron one, with a loop of faded ribbon through the bow.

I went in.

I had imagined that room for three weeks. I had thought of dust sheets and cobwebs, and a smell of decay. It was nothing like that, and it would have been easier if it had been. The bed was made, with a white counterpane. There was a dressing table in the window with a triple glass, and on it silver brushes laid out, and a comb, and a little china tray with hairpins in it. There was a book on the table by the bed, with a marker in it. It was a room that someone had left a few hours ago, meaning to come back.

Only the cold was wrong. It was the cold of a room that has had no fire in it for twenty winters, and it had got into the walls.

There was a clock on the mantelpiece. It was stopped. All through that house, in every room, there was something ticking; I had not been out of the sound of it since the first of October. In that room there was none. The hands stood at twenty-five minutes to four. I learned afterward that the old man had stopped it with his own hand on the morning they brought her home, and that it was the only clock at Compline he had ever stopped before that night.

By the bed, on the floor, side by side, there was a pair of shoes. They were grey satin, with a small heel, gone yellowish. They had been set down neatly, toes to the wall. They had stood there for twenty years, where she had left them for her husband to find.

So there were two pairs. I thought of that, standing over them. The woman in the gallery had carried her shoes in her hand. And here were the dead woman's shoes on the floor where she had left them.

I looked round, and I saw the rest.

The wardrobe door was open. It is a big mahogany one with a long glass. Inside there were dresses, a row of them, in cotton bags, pushed along the rail. One hanger hung by itself at the end. It was a padded one, covered in grey silk to match, and it was empty and swinging a little. On the floor of the wardrobe lay a cotton bag that had been pulled off and dropped.

And across the white counterpane, flung down, there was a black dress.

I knew it. I had sat two places from that dress at dinner for an hour and a half. On the carpet by the bed there were a pair of black evening shoes, kicked off, one on its side. On the dressing table one of the silver brushes had been moved out of its line. I went and looked. There were hairs in it. Most were dark. One was quite white.

I have said that I do not make up evidence, and I do not leave it out. There it was. A living woman had come into that room within the last half hour, and taken off a black dress, and put on a grey one, and brushed her hair with her mother's brush.

I stood and looked at the black dress on the bed. I thought that I ought to feel relieved. I had been frightened nearly out of my senses a few minutes before, and here was the explanation, lying on a counterpane.

I felt no relief at all. I was colder than I had been in the gallery.

I shall try to say why. On the stairs I had asked myself what I had seen. In that room I began to ask a different question. I asked what makes a quiet, guarded woman of forty-four go up alone, at midnight, on the twentieth anniversary, to a locked room, and take off her own clothes there, and put on her dead mother's. Why should she take off her own shoes and leave them, and carry another pair in her hand, when she could not know that that had been done before? And why had she gone down the gallery with that face?

I thought: she did not decide to do this. Nobody decides to do such a thing. She was standing at a window, and something came to her, and she went.

I had come to Compline with a poor opinion of the books in which the dead walk about in sheets. I still have it. I do not think that Marguerite Vane walked that night as a white thing with no one inside it. I think she had waited twenty years in that cold room for someone to come who was the right size.

I went out. I did not touch the black dress. I drew the door shut behind me, and I did not turn the key. It was not mine to turn.

* * *

I found Mr Vane in the hall.

I came down the main stairs with the lamp, at about ten minutes past twelve, and he was there. He was standing in front of the stopped regulator with its long door open. He had on his overcoat over his evening clothes. I had not put it on him. His white hair was dark with rain and flattened to his head. His thin shoes had left wet marks all the way from the gunroom passage.

On the settle by the fire, the old clergyman was laid out, asleep, on his side.

Mr Vane had something in his two hands. He was lifting it into the clock case, and it was heavy, and he could not find the hook. I saw that his hands were shaking. I had never seen them shake before, and I had watched him cut a wheel the size of a lentil.

"Mr Vane!"

He did not turn. "One moment," he said. "One moment, my dear. I must just put this back. It's very wrong to leave a regulator without its weight; the line will kink."

I set the lamp down and went to him. He had got the thing up to the pulley and it had slipped. I put my hands under his.

"Let me. Tell me where it goes."

"The hook. There. No, the bow goes over. So."

I took the weight from him. It was a cylinder of lead cased in brass, as thick as my wrist and about eight inches long, with a brass loop at one end. It was a great deal heavier than I expected. It was cold, and it was wet, and I supposed that was the rain. I lifted it and found the hook with the loop and let it hang. The line took the strain and creaked.

My fingers had come away with something on them. I did not look. I wiped them down the side of my dress, without thinking, as one does.

"Shall I start it?" I asked. The pendulum hung dead.

"No. No. Leave it stopped." He shut the long glass door himself, carefully, and stood with his palm against it. "One stops the clocks. When there is a death in the house. My mother did it for my father, and I did it for—" He lost the name. "It is the custom. They are not to go again until after the funeral."

"There has been no death, Mr Vane."

"Yes," he said. He turned round and looked at me. I have never seen a face so empty of everything but fact. "I have killed Edmund."

I thought that I had not heard him. Then I thought that I had, and that he was wandering. He often said things at night. He had told me once that he had left the gas on at a house in Clerkenwell in 1938.

"You've been out of doors," I said. "You're wet through. You've had a dream, or you have been walking in your sleep. Come to the fire."

"Miss Quaile." He said it quite sharply. "You undertook not to lie to me. I'll thank you not to begin by telling me that I'm mistaken as to where I have been. I have been to the ice house. I followed him there. He went out of the drawing room, and I saw his face as he went, and I thought, that is a man going to do a thing he's ashamed of. I've seen him go out so before. I got up and went after him. I took this from the case as I passed, to stop the clock. I don't recollect why I thought it should be stopped. I must have known."

"Mr Vane, sit down."

"He was on the landing at the top of the steps. He had a lamp. He had a box in his arms, and in the box was her clock. The Thorne. He had it in a blanket, like something he was going to drown." His voice was thin and exact. He might have been dictating. "I said, 'Edmund, it was you.' He said, 'Of course it was me, Father. Who else in this house could have done it?' I said, 'Why?' I asked him quite quietly. I've waited a year to ask him. And he laughed at me. He was not himself; his speech was thick and he could hardly stand. He said, 'It's a copy, you old fool. They're all copies. There's nothing in this house that isn't a copy of something. You've been winding fakes for four years and never knew it. You never knew anything. You never knew about her.'"

He stopped. His lips moved for a moment without sound.

"I asked him what he meant by that. He said, 'Ask the girl. Ask your nurse. She's got it in her pocket.'"

I put my hand to my pocket. I could not stop myself. The red book was there.

"And then he said the thing about the clock again. He said, 'It was never the clock.' He laughed. And I had this in my hand, on its line, and I swung it. I don't know that I meant to. It went of itself, like a pendulum." He touched his own left temple with one finger. "Here. He went backward, with the box. I heard him go down the steps, all of them. There are twelve. And then nothing. There was water dripping."

I had hold of his arm. I do not know when I had taken it.

"I stood there. I called to him. Then I thought, I must fetch Margot, she will know what to do. I came away. I pulled the gate to after me, because one always shuts that gate; the ice goes else. I came across the yard, and there was a child."

"A child?"

"Standing in the rain by the stable wall. A small girl, with dark hair. She was looking at me. I thought she was Imogen. I said, 'Go in, Immy, you'll catch cold.' She ran away." He frowned. "But Imogen's a grown woman. I can't account for that."

I could not speak. I thought of three wet footprints with stars in them.

"So you see," said Mr Vane, "that I have killed my son. It was at ten minutes to twelve. I looked at the dial as I came in, and every clock in this hall said the same."

* * *

I was a nurse for five years, and I have to say what a nurse did then.

I looked at him. I looked at his hands, back and front, for a cut. There was none. I looked at his shirt cuffs. The left was clean. On the right, where it came out of the black sleeve, there was a stain, and smaller ones running up toward the link. It was the size of a half-crown. It was red going brown.

I thought: he has fallen, out there, and has grazed himself where I cannot see. I thought: it is rust, from the gate. I told myself several things of that kind while I stood holding his wrist.

It was not that I disbelieved him. I should like that to be understood. I knew him too well by then. He was a man who could not have invented a thing to save his life; he had no gift for it. When he was confused he lost pieces of the world, but he did not make new ones. And he had given me a place, and a time, and a count of steps.

But I also knew something that he did not. I knew that Edmund had gone out of that drawing room with half a bottle of chloral in him. A man in that condition, struck on the head, at the top of twelve stone stairs, in a place as cold as a grave—

I said, "I must go to him."

"No." His hand closed on mine. It was astonishingly strong. "No. You promised. You said that you would stand by me at one."

"Mr Vane, if he is lying hurt—"

"He is not hurt. He is dead. I heard him go down." He was holding me with both hands now, and his face was working. "And it does not signify. Don't you see? It doesn't signify in the least. It is ten minutes past twelve. In fifty minutes the hour comes back, and I shall take it. I told you. I have her coupled and set. I shall go through it again, from twelve o'clock, and when I come to the ice house I shall not strike. It's perfectly simple. I had thought that I should need it only for Margot. I see now why I was made to build it. It was for both."

"That can't be done! I told you in the tower that it can't be done. He may be alive. He may be lying at the bottom of those steps with his skull broken, and every minute—"

"You'll not leave me."

"I must!"

I pulled my hands away. I had the lamp. I had taken two steps toward the gunroom passage.

I heard a sound behind me that I knew. I have heard it on a ward a hundred times. It is a small sound, like a sigh, and then the slither of cloth.

When I turned he was on the floor.

He had gone down all of a piece, as the very old do, without putting out a hand. He lay on the flagstones at the foot of the regulator with his long legs folded under him and his eyes half open. His face was the colour of putty and his lips were blue.

I do not remember crossing to him. I had him on his back and his collar open and my fingers on his neck before I knew that I had moved. The pulse was there, but it was thin, and it ran, and missed, and ran again. His skin was cold and wet. He had been out in a gale in thin shoes, at eighty-one, and had done whatever he had done, and walked back, and it had caught up with him.

I could not leave him then. I say it plainly. A nurse doesn't leave the patient who is on the floor in front of her to go and look for one who may be lying somewhere else. It is the first thing one is taught, and it is right. I got his feet up on a footstool. I loosened everything. I took the rug from the back of the settle, where the vicar lay, and I wrapped him in it, and I knelt on the stone with his head in my lap and counted his pulse against the big clock by the stairs.

I called out. I called for Mr Fenn, and for Mrs Pask, and for Gail. Nobody came. The drawing-room door was shut. The kitchen is down a flight and through a baize door, and I learned afterward that Mrs Pask was not in it, and that Gail had her shoes off by the range and heard nothing over the wind.

I counted. I thought: twenty minutes past twelve. I thought of twelve brick steps and water dripping. I thought of a child standing in the rain.

I had been set to keep two things safe in that house. I had left one of them, the bottle, for an hour and a half, and I had left the other for five minutes. I knelt on the floor of the hall with the old man's head on my knees, and I knew that between them my two absences had done it. I did not know yet what had been done. But I had left a drug where a hand could reach it, and it had made the son helpless. Then I had left the father alone, and he had got up and followed him with a weight.

I had come there so that I should never again be asleep when I was needed. I had been awake the whole time, and it had happened all the same.

* * *

He came round at about twenty to one.

His colour came back first. Then his eyelids moved, and he looked up at me, and I saw at once that it had gone. I mean the knowledge. His eyes were mild and clear and puzzled.

"Have I been asleep?" he said.

"You fainted, Mr Vane. You're in the hall. Lie still a moment."

"In the hall? How very odd. I don't remember coming down." He turned his head and saw the candles and the tall clocks. "Is it time? Have I missed it?"

"It's twenty minutes to one."

"Then there is time. Help me up, my dear. I mustn't be found on the floor." I got him into a chair. He sat with his hands on his knees, getting his breath. He looked at his wet shoes with surprise. "I have been out. Did I go out?"

I knelt by the chair. I looked into his face. He had asked me a question, and I had given him my word.

"Yes," I said. "You went out. You told me that you had been to the ice house."

"The ice house? Whatever for?"

I opened my mouth. I had it all ready. *You told me that you followed your son there and struck him with the weight of that clock, and that he fell down the steps.* I had told him on twenty nights that his wife was drowned. I had never spared him.

I could not say it. I had no rule for this. I did not know that it was true. If it was, I should be telling a man who had forgotten that he had killed his son. If it was not, I should be putting a horror into his head that had not been there. I looked at his mild face and I thought, for the first time since I came to that house, that there might be a thing worse than a lie.

"You didn't say," I told him. "You were very tired."

It was the second lie. I notice that I have kept count.

"Ah," said Mr Vane. He was satisfied. He looked across at the regulator. "That clock has stopped. How very vexing. It is never out. I shall look at it presently." His eyes went round the hall and came back to me, and brightened. "Has Margot come down?"

"Not yet, Mr Vane."

That was the third. I said it as they all said it. I heard my own voice.

People began to come in. It was nearly a quarter to one. Mr Fenn came from the drawing room with Dame Octavia on his arm, grumbling, and put her in the big chair. He carried bottles in from the pantry. Gail came up with a taper and began to light more candles, dozens of them, until the hall was as bright as a church. I got to my feet. My knees were stiff from the stone. There was a dark smear down the side of my blue dress, where I had wiped my fingers, and I held my hand over it.

I went to Mr Fenn. I spoke low.

"Mr Fenn. I can't leave Mr Vane; he has had a faint. But someone must go out to the ice house at once, with a light. I have reason to think that Mr Edmund is there, and that he's hurt."

He looked at me over his spectacles.

"What reason, Miss Quaile?"

"Mr Vane said so. Before he fainted. He has forgotten it since."

"Leo says a great many things."

"He said it very exactly."

"He is always exact. He told me last month with great exactness that I had died in 1987." But I saw that his face had changed. He looked old and wary. "Who is there to send? Kit is not to be found. I am sixty-seven and I do not know the way in the dark. The cook has vanished." He glanced round. "I will send Mr Pargeter, when he appears. He's young, and willing, and has nothing to do. Will that content you?"

It did not content me. But the old man had hold of my sleeve again and was asking me whether the door had been unbolted.

It was at that moment, at about ten minutes to one, that Mrs Edmund Vane came into the hall.

She came from the garden passage. She had on the green dress that she had dined in, and her shoes, and her fair hair was combed. But it was wet. It lay dark and flat against her head, and there was a leaf caught in it, behind her ear, that she had missed. Her face had no colour at all. She walked across the hall like a person carrying a full glass.

I went to her. I do not know what I meant to say. I think that I meant to ask her to go out to her husband.

"Mrs Vane. Have you seen Mr Edmund?"

She looked at me. Her eyes were quite blank.

"No," she said.

"I think that he may be ill. The bottle is gone from my tray. Mr Vane's sleeping draught. Someone has taken it out of his room, this evening, while we were at dinner, and has given a large dose of it to your husband in a glass of brandy. The vicar drank what he left, and is insensible." I was watching her face. "I think that Mr Edmund may be lying somewhere out of doors."

She did not ask me how I knew. She did not ask who would do such a thing, or exclaim, or put a hand to her mouth. Any woman would have done one of those. She stood and took it, as one takes a blow that one has seen coming for some time.

"Has someone," said Mrs Vane. Her voice was dry and very quiet. "Then he will be asleep, won't he? Somewhere. I shouldn't distress yourself, Miss Quaile. Edmund always turns up."

She went past me. She walked to the far end of the hall and took up a place by herself under the largest of the clocks, where the candles were thickest, and folded her hands in front of her, and stood.

I have said that I had no proof. I have none now. But I had seen her head come up in the drawing room, when he yawned, and her face when he went out. I had told her that her husband was drugged and lying out in a storm. She had answered me as though I had told her the time.

I knew then whose hand had been at my tray. I did not know why. I have never told anyone. She is gone abroad, I am told, and he is dead, and it was my bottle.

Mr Vane was tugging at my sleeve.

"It wants five minutes," he said. "Give me your arm. You'll stand on my left. You promised."

I gave him my arm. I thought: I will stand by him while he opens his door. It will take two minutes. And then, whatever he says, whoever holds me, I shall take a lamp and go to the ice house myself.

I walked him down the length of the hall to the great front door. His hand was steady again. He laid it on the bolt.

Behind us, one after another, the clocks began to strike.

## IV. The First Passage

§ 34. I come to the part of this relation which I should prefer not to dictate. Miss Quaile has offered to leave it out. I have told her that a paper from which the experimenter omits the observations that he dislikes is of no value to anyone.

I went out by the side door. It was blowing hard from the south-west, with rain. I had no hat nor coat. I did not feel the cold; I record that as a fact, since at my age I ordinarily feel it very much.

There was a lamp ahead of me. It was some sixty yards off, low down, going along the front of the stable range, and it rose and fell with a motion that I knew. I followed it. I had no light of my own and did not want one. I have crossed that yard in the dark to wind the tower upon every night for sixty years.

The lamp went round the end of the stables and down the track to the home copse. It turned in among the trees.

I knew then where he had put it. There is an ice house in that copse, which my great-grandfather built; a brick chamber, sunk in the ground, with a domed roof and twelve steps down. We have kept ice in it within my memory. It is a place that no one visits from November to June. It has an iron gate with a spring lock that fastens of itself.

The gate stood open. There was a light within.

§ 35. I went in. I did not go stealthily. I am told that I must have, since he did not hear me; but it was only that the wind was loud and that I was wearing my evening pumps, which have thin soles.

He was upon the landing at the head of the steps. He had set his lamp upon the floor. He had a box in his arms, a tea chest, and was in the act of lifting it; he had it against his chest and his chin upon the lid. He was swaying. I saw at once that he was more than drunk. His eyes were not right. They were dull, and the lids hung, and when he saw me he was a long moment in understanding who I was.

'Father,' he said.

It was said without surprise. He might have been a boy at the bench, looking up.

I said, 'Edmund. It was you.'

'Of course it was me.' His speech was thick. He leaned his shoulder upon the brick to keep upright. 'Who else in this house could have done it? Who else knows a Knibb from a Quare? You taught nobody else. You hadn't the patience.'

I looked at the box. The lid had been corded down, but one corner of a blanket showed, and under the blanket a glint of brass.

'What have you there?'

'You know what I have. You told the whole table. One of you has been robbing me. Well, here he is, at it.' He laughed. It was not a pleasant sound. 'It's the last one, Father. I give you my word. After this there's nothing left in the house worth the trouble.'

'That is the Thorne.'

'That's the Thorne.'

'It is thirty-one pounds, in that box, and you are carrying it down an ice house in the dark, with a bad knee, and you can hardly stand.'

'I'm not carrying it down. I'm carrying it out. I put it here to wait.' He shifted his grip. 'There's a boat. You needn't know about the boat. I'll be gone by two, and you can tell Fenn's policeman that it was Edmund, and he may put it in his book.'

§ 36. I must set down my own state of mind, as far as I can recover it.

I was not angry. I wish to be exact. I have been asked whether I struck him in anger, and I have considered the question, and the answer is that I do not believe so. I had expected it to be he. I had seen his hand at dinner. I had come out into the rain with one purpose, which was to ask him a question that I had been carrying for the best part of a year.

So I asked it. I said, 'Why?'

I said it quite quietly. I had thought a great deal about how it should be said.

I think that he had expected something else. I think he had expected me to speak of the police, or to order him back to the house. He looked at me over the top of the box. For a moment his face was that of the boy who had stood outside the Shop with the watch in pieces.

'Why,' he said. 'You want to know why.'

'I have wanted it since September. I had rather know that than have them back.'

'Because I owe a man in Southend a hundred and eighty thousand pounds. There. That's why. Because the sea wall wanted mending in 1988, and there was no money, and I borrowed it. And then I borrowed more to pay that. I'm no good with money, Father. I'm no good with anything. You've had twenty years to notice.'

'You might have come to me.'

'Come to you?' He stared. 'When did anyone ever come to you? You were in the cellar. You've been in the cellar since 1948. Mother used to say that if the house burned down you'd come up to ask who had altered the temperature.'

'I should have given it you. I should have sold whatever was needed.'

'I know you would. That's the damnable thing. You'd have looked at me through that glass, and said, "I expect you wanted it," and written a cheque. And you'd not have asked one question. Not *why did the wall want mending*. Not *why have you been drunk since you were thirty-two*. Not *what is the matter with my son*.' His voice had risen. 'Do you know what it is to live for fifty years in a house with a man who can hear a clock losing two seconds a week?'

I did not answer. I was holding the weight by its line. I felt it turn a little, in the draught.

§ 37. I put down what was next said as nearly as I can. It is the centre of the matter.

I said, 'Edmund, put that box down. You will fall. Come up to the house and we shall talk of it in the Hour. I said so at dinner. I said that whoever it was might come to me and it would not be held against him. I meant you. I knew that it was you.'

'The Hour.' He laughed again. 'God. The Hour. Mother's parlour game. Do you know what she made it for?'

'For the family.'

'For you. She made it for you. So that once in the year there'd be sixty minutes when somebody might be able to get a word through that skull. And in twenty-three years you never asked her anything in it. You danced with her. I used to watch you. You'd waltz her round the hall with your eyes shut, counting, and she'd look over your shoulder at the rest of us, and her face—' He stopped. He shook his head, as a horse shakes off a fly. 'Never mind her face.'

'I'll ask you now. I am asking you. Why did you take them? Not the money. I have had that. Why mine? Why did you put my copies in their places?'

He looked down at the box in his arms.

I shall always remember how he looked at it. It was with a kind of hatred. I have seen a man look so at a tool that had cut him.

'Because nobody would know the difference,' he said. 'That's all. That's the joke. I took your Tompion and stood your own copy in the case, and you wound it for a year. I took the Knibb. I took East. I'd lie awake waiting for you to come down to breakfast and say, "Edmund, somebody has been at the bracket clock." You never did. You walked past your own work every day and took it for the real thing.'

'I knew it in September.'

'After three years! Three years, Father!' He was shouting now. 'I thought, he'll see it. He must see it. He's the best eye in England. He'll look at that dial and know it's his own hand, and he'll come to me. He'll have to speak to me then. And you never looked.'

I said, 'Then you wished to be found.'

He did not answer that. He said the other thing. I have gone over it every night since.

'It's a copy, you old fool,' he said. 'Don't you understand yet? They're all copies. There's nothing in this house that isn't a copy of something. You made forty-three of them down there, and then you made me, to hold the file. A copy of you, that was the idea. Only it came out wrong. It would serve, though. Wouldn't it? You said so. *It will serve.*'

§ 38. I said, 'That was unjust of me. I have known it for some time.'

I do not think that he heard. He had gone on. He was no longer speaking to me; he was speaking to the box, or to the dark at the bottom of the steps.

'You never knew anything. You sat down there with your glass in your eye and you never knew one thing that went on over your head. You never knew about the money. You never knew about Celia. You never knew about her.'

'About whom?'

'Ask the girl.' He jerked his head toward the house. 'Ask your nurse. The little white one who tells you the truth. She's got it in her pocket. She found it in the clock. The one clock in the house you were told not to open, and you never did, in twenty years, because you'd given your word. God, you're a wonder. You'd keep your word to a dead woman and not hear a living one.'

I did not follow him. I asked what Miss Quaile had in her pocket.

'It's all there. In Mother's writing. Where she was going that night, and who to. You'll like the handwriting; it's very regular.' He swayed, and caught himself. 'She was leaving you, Father. She'd had enough. She was fifty-four and she was walking out of this house to a man with sheep on him, because he used to look up when she came into a yard.'

I said, 'I know that.'

It stopped him.

'I have known it since about 1980,' I said. 'I did not allow myself to know it. But I am not so great a fool as you think me. A woman doesn't cross the Wade at two in the morning, in her stockings, to post a letter.'

He stared at me. His mouth was open.

'It makes no difference,' I said. 'She was entitled to go. I ask you to understand that. I should not have stopped her. It is not the going that I cannot forgive myself. It is that she went by my clock. I had put it back that afternoon. She looked at the regulator, and it told her that she had an hour that she had not got. I killed her with a clock, Edmund. That is what I have had to carry. Whoever she was walking to.'

And my son laughed.

It was the worst sound that I have heard in my life. It went round the dome of that chamber. He put his head back against the bricks and laughed until he choked.

'The clock,' he said. 'Oh, my God. The clock. Twenty years. You've turned the whole house into a tomb for it. A hundred and eleven of them, all an hour slow, and you going round every week with the key.' He wiped his mouth upon his shoulder. 'It was never the clock. Do you hear me? It was never the bloody clock. She knew what time it was. She always knew what time it was. She was the only one of us that did.'

§ 39. I have asked myself what he meant to say next. I believe that he meant to tell me. He had it in his mouth. He told me two hours afterward, in the tower, and it was the same breath going on.

He did not get it said. I prevented him.

I must describe my hand.

I had the regulator weight by the loop of its line. The line is of gut, about eighteen inches of it, with the brass loop at the end, and I had the loop over my right forefinger. The weight hung at the height of my knee. It is a cylinder of lead in a brass jacket. I had carried it across the yard without knowing that I carried it.

When he laughed, I felt it move.

I am going to state this as accurately as I can, since it is the observation on which everything turns, and I have no witness to it but a child, of whom more presently.

I did not decide to strike him. I am certain of that. I have decided a great many things in my life, and I know what the act of deciding is like. One weighs. There was no weighing.

But neither will I say that I did not do it. That would be a coward's way. It was my arm. He laughed, and said that it was never the clock, and something went through me that I can only compare to the letting-go of a mainspring that has been held wound for twenty years. I had built my life upon that clock. I had stopped the house for it. It was all the reason that I had. He was telling me that I had been mistaken, and he was laughing.

My arm came up. It came from the shoulder. The weight swung out upon its line, in an arc, as a pendulum swings. I have set ten thousand pendulums going with that same motion of the wrist. It took him upon the left side of the brow, above the eye.

It did not make a great noise. It was a small flat sound, like a mallet upon a wedge.

He stopped laughing. He looked at me. I saw surprise in his face, and nothing else. His arms opened. The box went first; it dropped from him, and struck the edge of the landing, and went over. I heard it go down the steps, striking each one.

Then he went. He did not put out his hands. He went backward, quite slowly, and his heel missed the top step, and he fell the length of the stair. There are twelve. I heard every one.

At the bottom there was a sound of something heavy coming to rest in straw. Then there was none.

§ 40. The lamp was still upon the landing. I took it up and held it over the well.

He lay upon his back at the foot of the steps. His legs were upon the lowest three and his head was lower than his feet. One arm was out. His face was toward me. His eyes were open. They did not move when the light came upon them.

There was blood upon his forehead. It was running back into his hair.

I said his name. I said it several times.

I am a man of exact habits. I looked for the signs. I watched his chest, under the shirt front, for as long as it took me to count sixty. I could not see that it rose. I listened. There was water dripping from the ice, into a pool, with a clear sound, at intervals of about two seconds. I heard that. I heard nothing else.

I did not go down. I should say so. I ought to have gone down those steps and put my hand upon him. I could not. They are steep and without a rail, and I am eighty-one, and I had the lamp in one hand and the weight in the other. But that is not the reason. The reason is that I knew what I should find. I had seen my wife. I knew how they look.

I stood upon the landing. I looked at my watch. I do not carry one; I looked at the place upon my wrist where I wore one as a young man. Then I recollected that I had passed the hall clocks as I came out, and I reckoned the minutes from there.

At ten minutes to twelve by every clock in my house I killed my son.

I set that down as an observation. It is entered here in its place, with the time. I do not qualify it.

§ 41. The lamp went out in my hand as I turned. There is a great draught through that passage with the gate open. I set it down upon the landing, dead, and felt my way out by the wall. I have been sorry since that I left him in the dark.

I came out through the passage. At the gate I turned and pulled it to behind me. It shut, and I heard the lock drop. I had no intention in it. One always shuts that gate; I have told the servants so for fifty years; the ice wastes if the air gets to it.

I had the weight in my hand. I held it up, under the trees. There was something upon it that was not rain.

I walked back up the track. I was thinking, quite clearly, of one thing. I was thinking that I must fetch Margot. She would know what was to be done. She had always known, with Edmund. When he was small and had hurt himself he would not let me near him; he would stand and bleed until she came.

I came round the end of the stables into the yard.

There was a child there.

She was standing against the wall of the stable, at the corner, in the rain. She was a small girl, of nine or ten, with dark hair, wet, and a pale face. She had on a coat that was too large for her. She was quite still. She was looking at me, and at what I had in my hand.

I took her for Imogen. Imogen at that age had just such a way of standing at the edge of a thing and watching it, with her head a little on one side. She would come down to the Shop and stand in the door for an hour, and I would forget that she was there.

I said, 'Go in, Immy. You will catch cold.'

The child did not answer. She looked at me a moment longer. I have not forgotten her eyes. They were very large and they were not afraid of me. They were afraid for somebody.

Then she turned and ran. She ran toward the house, along the grass, and I lost her in the dark.

I have considered that child a good deal. I was not at that moment leaking; of that I am sure; the rain was upon my face and I had the weight in my hand. And yet Imogen is a woman of forty-four. There has been no child in this house since she went to school. I have asked Mrs Pask, who tells me that I was dreaming. I have asked Miss Quaile, who looked at me strangely and said that she did not know.

I record her as an anomaly. I shall return to her under the results.

§ 42. I came in by the side door. I walked the length of the gunroom passage and into the hall.

Someone had laid the vicar upon the settle. I did not ask why.

I went to the regulator. I opened the case. It seemed to me a matter of the first importance that the weight should be hung again upon its line. A regulator must not be left without its weight; the line will take a set. I had the loop in my fingers and I could not find the hook. My hands were shaking. I had never seen them do it. I stood and looked at them as one looks at a tool that has failed.

Miss Quaile came down the stairs with a lamp.

She took the weight from me and hung it. I told her to leave the pendulum stopped. I told her that one stops the clocks.

And I told her what I had done.

I shall not repeat the conversation; she has it in her own account, I believe, and it will be more accurate than mine. I told her the place, and the time, and the number of the steps. She did not believe me at first. Then she looked at my cuff.

She wished to go to him. She said that he might be alive. She was very urgent.

I would not let her. I am ashamed of it. I took hold of her and would not let her go. But I ask the reader to see how it stood with me.

It was ten minutes past twelve. I had killed my son twenty minutes before. And in fifty minutes, by every clock in that hall, the hour was to be returned.

I had built the engine for my wife. I had set it to a night twenty years gone. And I saw, standing there with the girl's wrists in my hands, that I had been a fool. I had not understood what I was making. I had thought that I should be given back an hour of 1974. But the take-up does not know one year from another. It takes up the hour that is going by. It would give me back *this* one. The hour between twelve and one, upon the night of the twenty-second of October, 1994, in which I had gone out to the ice house.

I should pass through it again. I should come again to that landing. And I should not lift my arm.

I said this to Miss Quaile. I said that it did not signify. I said that he was dead, and that in fifty minutes he would not be, and that she must on no account leave me, since I should want a witness.

She said that it could not be done. She had said so in the tower. She pulled her hands away. She took up the lamp. She said, 'I must.'

§ 43. Here there is an interval of which I have no record.

I am told that I fell. Miss Quaile says that I lay upon the floor of the hall for some twenty-five minutes, insensible, with my lips blue, and that she knelt by me and counted my pulse, and could not leave me.

I have no recollection of falling, nor of the floor. I was standing by the regulator, and she had turned from me toward the passage. Then I was in a chair, and the candles had been lit, and she was kneeling beside it asking whether I knew where I was.

I knew where I was. I did not at once know what had happened. It had gone. I looked at my wet shoes and could not account for them. I asked whether I had been out. She said that I had been to the ice house. I asked what for.

She looked at me. She is a truthful person. I saw her prepare to tell me.

And she did not. She said, 'You didn't say.'

I knew that it was a lie. I have told her since that I knew. One cannot live with a truthful person for three weeks and fail to mark the first time that she is not. I thought, she is sparing me something. I did not ask what. I was very tired, and I had the Hour before me.

I have considered that interval with care, as bearing upon the experiment.

The physicians would call it a faint. I do not say that they are wrong. I had been out in a gale, at my age, and had had a shock.

But I note the following. It began at about a quarter past twelve. It lasted until twenty minutes to one. During it I was absent from the house, in the sense that nothing reached me. And when I came out of it, I had lost the preceding hour. I did not know that I had been to the ice house. I did not know that I had struck my son. It had been wiped from me as one wipes a slate.

I submit that this is what one would expect.

If an hour is to be gone through a second time, the man who goes through it cannot carry with him the memory of the first. Else it would not be the same hour; he would be a different man in it. The engine had forty-five minutes still to run before the stroke. But a spring begins to draw before it is released. I believe that what Miss Quaile observed upon the floor of the hall was the first motion of the take-up. I was being made ready. The hour that I had lived was being taken off me, so that I might be given it again clean.

I put that forward as a hypothesis only. I am aware that there is a simpler one.

But I ask the reader to hold it in mind against what follows. For at one o'clock I stood at my door with no knowledge that I had killed my son. And at ten minutes to two, I found him alive.

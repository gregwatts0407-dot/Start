## Chapter Five

She got as far as the garden passage, and there her courage left her.

It was a narrow stone corridor that ran from the garden door to the back of the hall. At its far end was a baize curtain, and through the curtain came candlelight, and voices. She could hear Fenn. She could hear Octavia Prowse demanding champagne. She stood ten feet from them in the half dark, in the grey silk, and could not go on.

She had been so sure, in the orangery. With his hands round hers it had seemed a simple thing. She would walk into the hall and say it. *Papa, I am Imogen, and there is something I must tell you about the night Mother died.*

Now she thought of his face. She thought of every one of them turning to look.

There was an old mirror on the wall of the passage, spotted, in a black frame. She saw herself in it by the light from the curtain. The damp had darkened the silk on her shoulders. Her hair had come down a little on one side. There was a dead vine leaf caught at her waist.

She looked like a woman who had come in from the sea.

The garden door opened behind her, and the wind came in, and someone with it.

It was Celia. She was in her green dress with a man's waxed jacket over it, far too big, and gumboots. She had a rubber torch in her hand. Her fair hair was plastered flat to her head and running with wet, and her face under it was the colour of ash.

She shut the door and leaned on it. She had not seen Imogen. She stood with her eyes closed, breathing through her mouth, like someone who has run a long way. Then she began to get out of the jacket, with slow clumsy movements, and kicked off one boot, and looked up.

She made a sound that was hardly a sound at all. She went back against the door as though she had been pushed.

"Celia. It's only me."

"Oh, my God." Celia put her hand flat on her chest. Her eyes went from the grey silk to Imogen's face and back. "Oh, my God, Imogen. I thought—" She stopped. She began to laugh, without any breath in it. "No. Why not? Why shouldn't she? It's the night for it. I've just seen worse."

"What have you seen? Where have you been? You're soaked."

"I went out for some air."

"In that? Celia, what's happened? Where's Edmund?"

"I don't know where Edmund is."

She said it too quickly. She had got the other boot off and was standing in her stockings on the stone. She took a pair of evening shoes out of the jacket's deep pockets, and she put them on, holding the wall. Her hands were shaking so that she could hardly manage the straps.

"He's asleep, I should think," she said. "Somewhere. He's had a great deal to drink. He always turns up." She went to the spotted mirror and looked at herself. She took a comb from somewhere and dragged it through her wet hair, and pressed her lips together, and pinched her cheeks, hard, with finger and thumb. Imogen had seen her do that in the wings of a village hall, thirty years ago, before going on as Lady Bracknell. "There. Shall I do? I've got to stand where everybody can see me."

"Why?"

Celia met her eyes in the glass. For a moment the trained face slipped, and what was under it looked out. It was not an actress. It was a frightened woman of forty-eight who had done something and could not undo it.

"Because I've been told to," she said. "By a man who knows about these things." She turned round. She looked at Imogen's dress again, more steadily, and at her face. Something came into her own that was almost tenderness. "You've been with him. Haven't you? The Garrow boy. You've got it all over you. I saw a light on the Wade from my window and I thought, that's either the devil or a man in love, and nobody else would be such a fool."

Imogen said nothing.

"Good," said Celia. "I'm glad. I mean it. One of us should." She took a step toward the curtain, and stopped, with her back turned. "Don't go in yet. Not in that. Let him open his door first. If Leo sees you coming at him in that dress before one o'clock, he'll think the Hour's begun without him, and he will never forgive you."

"I wasn't sure that I could go in at all."

"Oh, you'll go in. You're a Vane. You'll stand about for a quarter of an hour feeling sick, and then you'll do it, with your chin up. It's the one thing you're all good at." She put out her hand to the baize. "Immy."

"Yes?"

"Whatever you hear. Tomorrow, or after. About tonight." Celia's voice was very low. "I only wanted him to stay. That's all it was. You of all people might understand that. I only wanted him not to go."

She lifted the curtain and went through into the light, like a woman walking onto a stage.

Imogen stood in the passage. She did not understand. She thought that she would ask, in the morning. In the morning she was at Saltcote, and Celia was on a ship.

* * *

She waited, as she had been told.

The clocks began. She heard them through the curtain, the hurried silver one and the cracked one and the deep slow one by the stairs. She heard them finish. She heard her father's voice say, "Now," and the iron bolt go back, and the two leaves of the great door groan.

The wind came down the passage and laid the baize against her legs.

"Come in, then," her father said. "You're late."

Far off, across the yard, the tower began to tell the quarters.

She stood with her hand on the wall. She was counting, as she had as a child, lying in bed, when the Hour was something the grown-ups did downstairs. Four chimes. Then the great bell.

It struck.

And on the stroke, as though the bell itself had burst, there were two shots.

She knew what they were. She had been taught on the sea wall. A twelve-bore, both barrels, the second on the heel of the first, and close; they had come from the yard.

She did not hear what the hall said. She heard a woman cry out, and a laugh, and Octavia's voice. She was not listening. She was already running.

She went back along the passage and out at the garden door, into the wet. The moon was out. She ran down the brick path in her mother's sandals, with her skirts in her fists, and wrenched at the door of the orangery and fell through it.

The candle had burned out. There was the lantern still, turned low, on the floor by the trough. It showed her the mare. Mab was standing where he had left her, tied to the stove pipe by her halter rope, with the saddle blanket over her back. She had her head up and her ears pricked toward the yard.

There was no one with her.

The saddle lay on the edge of the trough. Imogen went to it. She knew before she touched it. The long canvas case hung open and flat along the flap. It was empty.

"Tom!"

Her voice went up into the dead vine. A leaf came down.

She ran out again. She went along under the wall of the house, on the grass, to the corner, where one could see the front. She stopped there with her hand on the stone.

The gravel lay white under the moon. It was empty from end to end. The front door of the house stood wide, pouring yellow light, and the wind was taking the candle flames. Across the yard the stable block was black, and the tower stood up out of it. Under the little roof she could see one of the figures, with its arm lifted.

Nothing moved anywhere. There was no man with a gun. There was no one lying on the stones.

She stood and shook. The wind went through the silk as though she had nothing on.

He had not left it. She had asked him, for his child's sake, and he had looked at it, and said nothing. And when she was gone he had taken it out of its case and gone to find her brother.

*I'll not hurt him, if he tells me.*

*And if he laughs?*

She thought: I must find them. She took three steps out onto the gravel.

Where? The shots had come from the yard, but the yard was empty. He might be in the stables. He might be on the sea wall, or the Hythe. She did not know this island by night any longer. She had no lamp. And if she found them, in the dark, with a loaded gun between them, what would she do? She had had her chance to stand between Edmund and someone else on a dark night. She had sat on a pot.

She stopped. The gravel was sharp through the thin soles.

It came to her slowly, and it was the coldest thought she had ever had. She was doing it again. In the house behind her the door was open and the Hour had begun, and she was running away from it. Twenty years ago she had hidden in the orangery. Tonight she had a better reason. There was a man out there whom she loved, with a gun. But she was still about to spend the Hour out in the dark, and she knew what her father would be asking.

*Is Margot down?*

If Tom had done it, it was done. She could not change it by finding him ten minutes sooner. If he had not, if he had fired into the air, or at a shadow, then he would be at the orangery at two, as he had said. He was a man who did not say what he was not sure of. *I don't reckon to miss you by an hour.*

She had one thing left that she could do. Nobody else could do it.

She turned round. She went back along the wall. In the orangery she stopped by the mare and laid her face for a moment against the warm white neck.

"Wait for him," she said. "I'll come back."

Then she went in at the garden door. She did not go to the baize curtain. She went up the back stairs.

* * *

She knew how it must be done. She had watched it every year of her childhood, through the banisters, in her nightgown.

Her mother had never been in the hall when the door was opened. She said that it was Leo's moment. He liked to stand there alone with his clocks and let the world in. She went up after the music and took off her shoes, and lay on her bed for half an hour with a book. And when she heard the tower strike one, she got up, and put her shoes on, and came down.

She came down the main staircase, slowly, in the grey silk, with one hand on the rail. Papa would be standing by his chair. He would look up. Every year he looked up as though he had not expected her. She would say, from the fourth stair, "Well, Leo? Have you got your hour?" And he would say, "I have it, Margot. Come and help me spend it."

Then they would dance. It was the only time in the year that he did. And after that she would stand in the middle of the hall and clap her hands and say, "Now, my darlings. The truth. Who is going to begin?"

Imogen came out at the top of the back stair, into the gallery.

She did not stop under the portrait. She walked the length of the long floor, past the dark windows. She came to the head of the main stairs and stood there, out of sight of the hall, in the shadow.

She could hear them below. A young man's voice was saying something loud and cheerful about ice. There was a silence. There was a murmur, and her father's voice, mild and exact. She could not hear the words.

She waited. She heard him speaking again, for a long time. She heard him say Fenn's name. She stood with her hands gripped together and her eyes shut. She thought of Tom. She thought of a white gravel yard with nobody on it.

Then there was a hiss, and a crackle, and music.

It was the waltz. It was "Gold and Silver." He had asked for it.

She opened her eyes. She put her hand on the rail. And she went down.

* * *

She saw the hall as one sees a painting, all at once, before any part of it.

It was full of candles. They were on every ledge, on the stairs, along the tops of the clock cases. All the brass was shining. She saw the long pendulums going. She saw the Font on its table, and beside it a thing on a grey blanket that she knew at a glance. It was the Thorne. No. She had cleaned pictures for twenty years; she knew the look of a surface that had not lived long enough. It was Papa's Copy. It was standing on the hall table like a guest who had arrived at the wrong party.

So that had been Edmund's arrangement. And it had gone wrong.

She came down another step, and they saw her.

She had not thought of them. She had thought only of her father. She saw Octavia Prowse heave herself up out of her chair, and her big plain face break. She heard her say a name. She saw Kit at the gramophone with his hand out, and Fenn bow his head. She saw the little nurse go back against the wall and slide down it to the floor.

She saw Celia, under the tall clock, quite still, with her hand at her throat.

None of them said, *Imogen.* She understood then what she had done. She had put on a dress to keep a promise to the dead. She had not known that she was putting on the dead.

She very nearly turned. She had her weight on her back foot. She thought, this is wicked. I must go up and take it off.

Her father had risen.

He stood by a chair near the open door, with the night behind him. The rug had fallen from his knees. He was looking up at her, on the fourth stair.

She had seen his face in every state. She had seen it bent over a bench, absorbed. She had seen it puzzled and mild, lost in the middle of a sentence. She had seen it at her mother's grave, like a shut door. She had never, in forty-four years, seen it as it was now.

He looked at her as a man looks who has waited on a railway platform for twenty years, every day, meeting every train, and one day she gets down from a carriage.

"You came back," he said. "Margot. You came back."

She had her sentence ready. *Papa, it is Imogen.* She had rehearsed it in the passage, and on the stairs. She had come into this hall, in this dress, for no other reason than to say something true at last.

She crossed the floor to him. She saw his eyes. They were pale and clear and they were not confused. That was the thing that undid her. He was not wandering. He knew quite well that it was 1994, and that he was eighty-one. And he knew that his wife was coming toward him across the hall, in her grey gown, as she had every year.

He held both those things. He had made room for both.

*Papa, it is Imogen.* It was the truth. And she saw that if she said it, now, in front of them all, he would hear it, and his face would change. It would go back to what it had been for twenty years.

The girl who sat with him at night told him the truth, straight out, every time. Imogen had honoured her for it. She had thought, I could never be so brave.

She found, standing in front of him, that there were two kinds of courage, and that the one she had come for was not the one that was wanted.

She put out her hand.

"Yes, Papa," she said.

It was not enough. She saw that it was not. He had not been waiting twenty years to be called Papa.

She took a breath. She let her mother's name for him come into her mouth. She had heard it ten thousand times, across this hall, in that light amused voice.

"Yes, Leo," she said. "I came back."

* * *

He took her hand. His own was dry and cool. He set his other hand at her waist, exactly, as a man sets a hand who was taught to do it in 1930. He stood a moment listening for the beat.

Then he danced with her.

She had been afraid that he would fall. He was so thin; she could feel his shoulder blade under her fingers like the handle of a spoon. But he did not falter. He led her down the length of the hall in the old slow waltz, turning, and she followed, as she had seen her mother follow. Her body knew it. She had stood on his feet for this, at five years old, in her nightgown, on Christmas Eve.

The clocks went round them. The candles went round. She saw faces, and lost them. His eyes were closed.

"I had it wrong," he said.

He spoke low and quickly, close to her ear. It was the voice he used at the bench, when he was explaining a difficult thing.

"I had it wrong for you, the last time. I put the hall clocks back before dinner. To save a scramble. I never told you. And you looked at the regulator and saw two, and it was three out there, and the tide goes by out there. I gave you an hour that you hadn't got. I have known it every day since."

"Leo—"

"Let me say it. I've had it ready for twenty years, and I shall lose it if I stop." His hand tightened on hers. "High water tonight was at eight minutes to one. I have that from the Thorne. I went and looked. The Wade will not carry you on foot before half past four. You're not to try. Do you hear me? Not on foot."

She could not see. The candles had run together.

"But a horse may go at twenty minutes to three. Not a minute before. He must swim at the Rill; there will be three feet there and a strong set to the north, and he must be let to find it himself. The Garrows' horses always could. That is by this house, Margot. By our clocks. Not by any watch that anyone shows you."

Twenty minutes to three. By the house.

She heard Tom's voice, in the orangery, an hour ago. *Twenty to four. By my watch. There'll be three foot on the Rill and falling.*

It was the same minute. Her father, who had not left the house, who did not know that there was a man on the island or a horse, had given her the hour of her crossing as exactly as the man who had been born on that shore and could smell it.

He had worked it out for his wife. He had gone into the library that afternoon, an old man with a failing mind, and read the tide off a clock. He had carried the figures in his head all evening, through the dinner and the speech. He wanted to be sure that tonight, if she came, he would have them right.

"Do you hear me, Margot?"

"I hear you, Leo."

"You'll not go on foot."

"No."

"You'll wait for the horse."

"I'll wait."

"Then you will get across," her father said. He let out a long breath, and she felt his whole frame ease, as if a spring that had been held wound for twenty years had been let gently down. "You'll get across, my dear. That is all I wanted. I don't ask you to stay."

Her father danced with her, and called her by her mother's name, and told her the hour of the tide.

She understood that it was the only way he had ever known of saying the other thing. He could not say *I love you.* He could not say *I knew that you were unhappy,* or *forgive me.* He was not made so. He had found a thing that he could give her that was exact and true and would bring her safe to the other side, even though the other side was another man's kitchen. He had got it right to the minute. And he had told her that she might go.

*I don't ask you to stay.*

Her mother had waited thirty years to hear that he saw her. She had stood behind him at a clock in July. *If he had turned round and asked me one single question.* Imogen did not know that those words had ever been written. She knew only that something in the grey silk seemed to loosen, and that she was crying as she had not cried since she was a child, without any sound.

She had come to tell him that it was not his clock. She tried. She owed him that.

"It wasn't the clock, Leo," she whispered. "It wasn't your doing. I knew the time. I swear to you, it was never the clock."

She did not know that it was true. She believed that her brother had done it, on the Wade. But she said it as her mother, and it came out of her with a certainty that she could not have explained.

"You're kind," he said. "You were always kind to me, at the last. It was how I knew." He turned her, at the end of the hall, under the tall clock. "It doesn't signify now. I have got it right. One ought to be allowed to do a thing over, if one has it right the second time. I've built something that lets me. I'll show you, when you come home."

"Yes."

"You will come home? Not to stay. I don't ask it. But to see the clocks."

"Yes, Leo. I'll come and see the clocks."

"Good." He was smiling. "The gallery one is still gaining. Four seconds a week. I never have accounted for it. I think that you used to move the hands on, to tease me."

"I did," Imogen said. She did not know whether it was true. She found afterwards, from Dolly, that it was. "Every Sunday. While you were at church."

He laughed. She had not heard her father laugh aloud since she was a girl.

The music ran down. The needle hissed in the groove. He brought her to a stop in the middle of the floor and stood holding her.

"There," he said. "There, my dear. You see. It's quite all right. We had the time after all."

* * *

She kissed him on the forehead. She took his face between her hands. It was all bone, and his eyes were open now, looking into hers, very clear.

She did not know whom he saw. She has thought about it a great deal. She thinks that at that moment he saw them both, and knew it, and was content.

"Goodbye, Papa," she whispered. It was so low that no one else could have heard.

"Go along, my dear," her father said, as softly. "You've a man waiting with a lantern. You mustn't keep him standing on that wall."

She stared at him.

He patted her cheek. Then his eyes went past her, mild and tired, and he looked about for his chair.

She let him go. She turned. The hall swam. She saw Octavia with her hand over her eyes. She saw Kit standing at the gramophone with the tears running down. She walked toward the garden passage, quickly, and she did not look at any of them.

By the open door the little nurse was sitting on the floor against the wall. Her face was turned up, as white as paper, with enormous eyes. Imogen looked down at her. She thought: this is the one who tells him the truth. I have just told him the largest lie of his life, in front of her, and she has seen what it did.

She nodded to her. She meant, *I leave him with you.* She meant, too, *Forgive me. I could not do it your way.*

Then she was through the baize, and in the cold stone passage. She stood there holding the wall.

She had gone into that hall to say her piece. She had not said one word of it. Her father did not know that she had told Edmund, or that she had hidden. He did not know that she had seen her brother come up the lawn. She had given him her mother instead. She had stood up in front of her family in a dead woman's dress and answered to a dead woman's name.

It was the kindest thing she had ever done. It might also be the worst. She did not know which. She thought that her mother, who had made the Hour, would have known. And she seemed to hear her mother's voice then, very clearly, light and dry, in the dark of the passage.

*Darling. Don't be solemn. He asked for me, and you were the nearest. Now go and get on that horse.*

The clock in the morning room, through the wall, struck the half hour. Half past one. She had thirty minutes.

She went out to the orangery.

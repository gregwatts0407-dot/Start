## VI. The Second Passage

§ 56. I had a candle in a glass, from the hall table. The wind had dropped and the moon was out; I could have done without.

I did not go to the tower. I should have liked to; it was there that the engine was, and I had felt it take the load. But I had resolved, sitting in my chair, upon a method. If I was to claim that an hour had been passed through twice, I must go the second time where I had gone the first, and observe what was different. Otherwise I should have no comparison.

I therefore went as I had gone before. I crossed the yard. I went round the end of the stables and down the track to the home copse.

I shall record the two passages side by side, as a table, since that is how the thing presented itself to me.

Upon the first passage the gate of the ice house stood ajar, and there was a lamp within. Upon the second it stood wide open, pushed back to the wall, and there was no light. There was a ring of keys hanging in the lock. I knew them for Mrs Pask's.

Upon the first passage there was a man upon the landing with a box in his arms. Upon the second there was no one.

I held my candle over the well.

Upon the first passage I had looked down those steps and seen my son upon his back at the bottom, with his feet higher than his head and his eyes open. I had counted sixty and seen no breath. I had heard the box go down before him.

Upon the second there was straw at the foot of the steps. There was sacking, and the ice. There was no man. There was no box.

I went down. I had not been able to, the first time. I found that now I could. I went down sideways, with my hand upon the wall, twelve steps. I stood where he had lain.

The straw was pressed flat there, in a long shape, and in one place it was dark. I put my candle to it. I shall come to that under the next head. But there was no man in it.

I record one anomaly, for completeness. Upon the third step from the bottom somebody had set down a kitchen saucer with the end of a candle in it. And there was a silver spoon in the straw. I could not account for either. I had put them there in neither passage.

I stood in that place. I am not a man who prays. I said aloud, 'He is not here.'

For he was not. I had come to the same landing, in the same hour, by the same road; and what I had left there was gone. In the first passage I had struck. In the second I had come out into the hall and been told the tide, and danced, and had not gone to the ice house at all until now; and there was nobody at the bottom of the steps.

I climbed out. It took me a good while.

§ 57. I have said that I do not believe that a thing can be had for nothing, and I should note here an inconvenience in the result, since I do not wish to be thought to have hidden it.

The lamp was still upon the landing. It was the one that had gone out in my hand, upon the first passage, and that I had set down. It was where I had left it, cold.

There was the straw, which kept the print of a body, and was stained.

And there was my cuff. I looked at it by the candle. The stain was there.

Now if the first passage had been wholly undone, there should have been no lamp upon that landing and no blood upon my shirt. I considered this as I went back up the track. I do not find that it is fatal to the hypothesis. It appears that what is returned is the hour, and the acts done in it, and not every particle of matter that was moved. The engine is a timekeeper and not a broom. A man who is given a day over again does not find that the mud has been taken off his boots.

There was the weight itself. I examined it the following week, and it was clean. I must have wiped it, afterward. I have no memory of wiping it. One does these things.

But I record it. An experimenter ought to state what tells against him. There was a residue.

§ 58. I went to the tower.

The door of the harness room was open. There had been a lamp broken there, and spirits spilt. I climbed the ladder. I am told that I ought not to have been able. I went up as I have gone up every night for sixty years, by the count: fourteen rungs, and the trap.

The clock chamber was dark. The moon came in at the louvres, in bars. I could hear the great pendulum, and the train. And I could hear that it was labouring. The beat was uneven; there was a drag upon it.

I held up my candle. The first thing that I saw was the Reconciler.

One of the rods was gone. It had been sheared through, a foot from the cupboard, and hung down. The door of the cupboard was starred all over with small holes, as if someone had thrown a handful of gravel at it with great force. The take-up had been knocked from its clamp and was lying upon the floor in two pieces.

I understood then what the reports had been. The engine had taken the load at the stroke, and it had been more than the work would bear. It had burst.

I did not grieve. A fuse is meant to go. It had done what it was made for; I had the proof of that at the bottom of an empty flight of steps. I thought only that I had made the pinion too fine.

Then something moved against the frame of the clock, low down, and a voice said:

'Who's that?'

§ 59. He was sitting upon the floor with his back against the iron frame and his legs out. He had his left hand up at the side of his neck, pressing something to it. He turned his face to the candle.

It was my son.

I shall describe him as I should describe a movement that had been brought to me for repair. There was a swelling upon the left side of his brow, above the eye, the size of a hen's egg and dark. The eye beneath was half shut. There was dried blood from it down his cheek. His hair was wet. There was more blood, fresh, upon the left of his neck and upon his collar, in a number of small places, and the thing that he held there was a child's handkerchief with a pattern of red leaves.

He was breathing. His eyes followed the light.

I set the candle upon the floor. I went down upon my knees in front of him. I have not knelt since 1974.

I put my hand upon his face.

It was warm. I felt the bone of the jaw, and the bristle, and under my thumb, at the angle, the beat of the artery. I counted it. It was eighty to the minute, and strong, and perfectly even.

I had stood upon a landing two hours before and counted sixty and seen no breath.

At ten minutes to two I found him in the clock room, alive, and I did not strike him. I submit this as the experimental result.

I have Miss Quaile's leave to underline that, and she has done so.

§ 60. He said, 'Father?'

'Yes.'

'How in God's name did you get up that ladder?'

'I have come up it every night since before you were born.' I had not taken my hand away. 'You are alive.'

'I think so. I'm not altogether sure. I've had a peculiar evening.' He was looking at me strangely. His speech was clear; the thickness had gone out of it. 'You keep touching my face.'

'I must be certain. It is the result.'

'The result of what?'

So I told him. I told him of the hour, and where it is kept, and of the engine. I showed him the cupboard and the rods. I told him that I had built it to give his mother the tide, and that it had done that; she had come down, and I had sent her over upon a horse. And I told him the rest. I said that upon the first passage I had followed him to the ice house and had struck him with the weight of the regulator, and that he had fallen down the steps and was dead; and that at one o'clock the hour had been given back, and I had gone through it again, and had not struck him.

He heard me to the end. He did not laugh. I had been afraid that he would laugh.

He put up his right hand and felt of the swelling upon his brow.

'You did hit me, Father,' he said.

'Upon the first passage.'

'It still hurts on the second.'

'There is a residue. I have noted it. There is a stain upon my cuff also.'

He looked at me for a long moment. I saw his face work. I thought that he was going to tell me that I was mad. I had prepared an answer to that.

He did not say it. He let his head go back against the iron. I saw that there was water running from his good eye.

'All right,' he said. 'All right, Father. I was dead, and now I'm not. I'll not argue. God knows it's how it feels.' He took a breath. 'I woke up in the straw with a horse blanket over me and the cook's voice going away. I didn't know where I was. I thought I was eleven. There was a child there. I thought she was Imogen.'

'A child? With dark hair?'

'In a coat too big for her. She said she wasn't a ghost; I'd asked. She gave me this.' He moved the handkerchief. 'I don't know who she is. She's not one of ours.'

I told him that I had seen her also, in the yard. We looked at one another. Neither of us could account for it.

I record her again as an anomaly. She was observed by two persons, at different times, upon both passages. I have no hypothesis.

§ 61. I return here to the box, as I undertook.

He told me how he had come by it. He said that he had gone into the library when the lights failed, and lifted the clock from under its glass, and crated it, and carried it to the ice house, to wait for a boat. He said that it was to clear what he owed. He said that he had not looked at it. He had thrown the baize over it for three years so as not to have to.

I said, 'It was the Copy.'

'What?'

'It was the Copy that you took. I changed them in September. The Thorne is there.' I showed him. He could see it from where he sat, through the broken door of the cupboard, upon its shelf, with the moon upon the dial. 'I wished to learn whether you would know the one from the other. You cut the lunar train of that Copy. You had only to turn it over.'

He stared at the cupboard. Then he began to laugh. He had to hold his neck.

'I stole your copy,' he said. 'Oh, Lord. I've been four years robbing you blind, and sick with it, and at the last, for the big one, I walked off with your own work under my arm and never knew. And was going to sell it to a moneylender for a million pounds.' He wiped his eye with the back of his wrist. 'You said the best hands you ever trained had forgotten what they were for. Didn't you? You'll have said that to somebody.'

I said that I had thought it.

'You were right. I've not looked at a clock in twenty years. Not looked. I couldn't.'

I then told him what had become of it. I said that the box had been brought into the hall at one o'clock by Dorothea's grandson, who had taken it for a pudding.

I ask the reader to consider that.

Upon the first passage, at ten minutes to twelve, that box went down the ice-house steps before my son, and lay at the bottom. I heard it. Upon the second passage, at one o'clock, it was carried into my hall, whole, by a young man who had no notion what it was, and set down before me upon the table.

I did not send him for it. No one did. He believed himself to be fetching an ice.

I submit that this is what the hour running back would look like, to those inside it. A thing that had fallen is found standing. A thing that was taken is brought home, and by somebody who cannot say why he does it. He will have a reason. He will say that he was sent for a pudding. Persons inside an hour always have their reasons. But I was at the door and I saw it come in.

My son said, 'Toby brought it in? In front of everybody?'

'He said that the ice was coming.'

He laughed again until he was obliged to stop.

§ 62. I come now to what he told me.

I am in a difficulty. Miss Quaile has laid down her pen and asked whether I mean to dictate it. I have considered for some minutes.

I shall not. I give my reasons.

It was said in the Hour. It was the first thing that my son has ever told me in one. My wife made that hour so that her family might say to one another what they could not say at any other time, and she laid it down that it should not be held against them afterward. I never used it while she lived. I shall not break her rule upon the one occasion that I have been the gainer by it.

And it is his. It is not a thing that happened to me. It happened to him, upon the Wade, when he was thirty-two, and he has carried it by himself ever since, in a manner that I should not have thought him capable of. He told me that he had meant to tell his sister, and a man across the water. He said that he would tell them himself, in the morning. I do not think that it would be right for me to put it into a paper before he has done so.

I shall therefore record only so much as bears upon the experiment.

He told me where he was that night, and where his mother was. He told me what lay between them. I knew the place; it is the deepest water upon the road. He told me which of them was upon the wrong side of it, and that it was not she.

And he told me what she did.

I asked him to say it twice. I am not quick at such things. He said it a second time, in the same words, and then he could not speak.

I have it. I shall keep it. I shall say here only what I said to him.

I said, 'Then she didn't leave me.'

He said, 'She'd left you, Father. She was past the Perch. She was nearly at his door.'

'But she turned back.'

'Yes.'

'She was coming back to this house.'

'She was coming back for—' He stopped. 'Yes. Toward the house. She turned round and came back.'

'Then she did not leave me. She had gone, and she turned.'

I said it several times. I could not seem to get to the end of it. I am told that Miss Quaile heard me from the foot of the ladder.

The reader will think it a small distinction. A woman who sets out to leave her husband, and is drowned upon the road, has left him, whichever way her feet were pointing. I know that. I am not a child.

But I have lived twenty years inside a picture of that night, and in the picture she had her back to me. She was walking away, quickly, as she always walked, toward a light. She did not look round. I have seen it every night; it is the last thing that I see. And my son was telling me that the picture was wrong. At the end, in the last quarter of an hour, she was facing this way. If I had been standing at my front door, as I ought to have been, and had looked out across the Wade, I should have seen her lantern coming toward me.

It was not for my sake. He was clear about that; he would not let me think it. I do not care. I do not ask for what reason a clock is right.

§ 63. He said, 'It was never the clock, Father. That's what I was trying to say, down there, before you—before the first time. She knew you'd put them back. She told me so; she laughed about it. She had her watch on. She went an hour and a quarter before the water. She'd have been across with an hour in hand.'

It was the third time that I had been told it that night. I had it from him upon the landing, and struck him. I had it from her in the hall, and thought her kind. Now I had it again.

I said, 'Then what was it?'

'It was me.'

He said it without any flourish. I looked at him. He was fifty-two. He had been a beautiful child, and he was a ruin. The best hands that I ever saw upon a boy were lying open upon his knees.

'It was me,' he said. 'I couldn't let her go. I went out after her. I was drunk. I'd had a bottle of your port in the gunroom. And everything that happened afterward was because of that. She'd be living at Saltcote now, an old woman, with hens. I've known it every morning for twenty years. And every morning I've come down and heard you winding those damned clocks and telling them it was your fault, and I've let you.'

'Why did you not tell me?'

'How was I to tell you? You'd have forgiven me. You'd have looked at me through that glass and said that you expected I couldn't help it.' His voice broke. 'I didn't want to be forgiven. I wanted somebody to hit me. I've been waiting twenty years for somebody to hit me, Father. I never thought it'd be you.'

I did not know what to say. I have never known what to say to him.

I put my hand upon his head. It was what his mother did. I had seen her do it that evening, in another year, when he had played the Schubert.

I said, 'You were only a boy.'

'I was thirty-two.'

'You were a boy. You were never let to be anything else. Your mother made too much of you and I made nothing of you at all. Between the two of us, we left you nowhere to stand.' I found that I could go on. The hour was nearly out, but it had not gone. 'Edmund. That train that you cut for the Copy. The lunar. In 'seventy-four.'

'What about it?'

'It is better than Thorne's. I have had them both under the glass. His is good. Yours is better. The depthing is finer and the finish of the leaves is as good as anything that I have ever seen out of Switzerland. I ought to have told you so on the day that you brought it down. I knew it then. I said that it would serve.'

He did not answer.

'It was not that I did not see it. I saw it. I had not the habit of saying such things. I thought that you would know.' I took my hand from his head. 'I should like it to go upon the record that I said so. I have said it now. It is twenty years late and you have been dead in between. But I have said it.'

He had put his arm across his eyes. His shoulders were shaking. I sat back upon my heels and waited. One does not hurry a thing that has been wound so long.

§ 64. There was a third person in the chamber.

I set it down, since I have undertaken to record what I observed. I heard it while my son was weeping. There is a closet in that chamber, to the right of the frame, where the weights of the striking train go down; it has a door of slats. From behind those slats there came a small sound. It was like a child sneezing, and trying not to.

I said, 'What is that?'

My son lifted his head. He looked at the closet. Then he looked at me, and his face was the face of a boy who is hiding a puppy.

'Rats,' he said. 'There've always been rats up here.'

I have kept the tower clock for sixty years and there has never been a rat in that chamber; there is nothing for one to eat. But I did not press him. He had told me the truth about a great thing and I thought that he might be let to lie about a small one.

I note it. A sound, as of a child, in the weight closet, at about five minutes to two.

§ 65. I had one thing to give him.

I had carried it in my waistcoat pocket since September, upon a ribbon. It was the key of the Thorne. It is the original; Thorne made it; it is of steel, about three inches long, with a pierced bow in the form of a sprig, and it fits nothing else. The clock is wound with it every eighth day. I have done it myself, upon a Tuesday, since 1960.

I took it out. I put it into his hand.

'She wants winding upon Tuesday,' I said. 'You will do it. You cut her moon. I've left her to the Museum in my will, and that must stand. But the winding of her is yours, for as long as you are in this house. You'll bring her down from here tomorrow and set her back in the library, and look to that cock; I have always meant to. I should like you to put your glass upon her.'

He looked at the key upon his palm.

'I was going to sell her,' he said.

'You were going to sell the Copy. It is a different thing.'

'Not in my head, it wasn't.'

'No. But a man is not hanged for what he does in a passage that has been gone through again.' I closed his fingers upon it. 'Put it in your pocket. Not the waistcoat; you will lose it. The coat.'

He put it in the pocket of his dinner jacket. I watched him do it.

He said, 'I'll come to you in the morning. In the Shop. After breakfast. There's more; there's a thing I've not given you. And that little nurse has a book of Mother's that you ought to see. I'll have her by, to keep me to it. I don't trust myself by daylight.'

I said that I should be at the bench at nine.

§ 66. There was a voice from below. It came up the ladder. It was Miss Quaile, calling my name, and telling me that I should take my death.

My son put his finger to his lips.

I did not understand it. I said, quite low, 'It is only the girl. She would be glad to see you. She thinks a great deal of you.'

'I know she does.' He was whispering. 'That's why. Look at me, Father. If she comes up that ladder and sees this, she'll have me in bed with a thermometer in my mouth, and I've things to do before morning. I've to see Imogen. I've to stand on the wall and watch a boat not come.' He nearly smiled. 'Go down. Tell her I'm alive, if you like. She'll not believe you; nobody does believe you, do they? It's very useful.'

'I shall not lie to her.'

'I'm not asking you to. Tell her the truth. It's the best cover there is, in this house.'

I got to my feet. He helped me, with his good arm. For a moment we stood, the two of us, in that chamber, with the pendulum going between us and the moon upon the wreck of my engine.

I had not stood so near him since he was fourteen.

'Good night, Father,' my son said.

'Good night, Edmund. Mind the fourth rung; it is worn.'

I went down. Miss Quaile was at the foot with a lamp. I told her that he was alive. I told her that I had been through it twice. She looked up at the trap. She called to him.

He did not answer. I thought it a pity. But I had given my word that I should not lie, and I had not; I had only been disbelieved, which is a thing that I am used to.

We crossed the yard. Over our heads the great bell struck two. There were no quarters before it. I looked up. Jenny stood by herself.

The Jack was gone from his pin. So that was correct. I had seen a man fall at one o'clock, upon the left of the bell, and taken it for the price.

I said, 'The Hour is over. I should have liked it longer. But it was enough.'

§ 67. I now state the results, as they stood at two o'clock in the morning of the twenty-third of October.

*First.* An hour was received at this house at one o'clock, at the front door, in the presence of witnesses. I felt it enter.

*Second.* My wife, deceased twenty years, descended the stairs in the hall at about twenty minutes past one and was given by me the correct state of the tide. A woman in her gown subsequently crossed the Wade upon a horse at the time that I had named, and was not drowned.

*Third.* My son, whom I had struck upon the head at ten minutes to twelve, and observed for sixty seconds without sign of life, was found by me at ten minutes to two, in another place, living. He conversed with me for a quarter of an hour.

*Fourth.* An object which had fallen upon the first passage was returned to me whole upon the second, by a person who did not know what he carried.

*Fifth.* I was told three times in one night, by three voices, that it was never the clock.

*Sixth.* There was a price. A figure was thrown from the tower at the instant of reversal, and the engine was destroyed.

I add the following, which I did not design and cannot classify.

*Seventh.* In the course of that hour I said four things which I had not been able to say in the preceding eighty-one years. I told my family that I had not been a father to whom a person could come. I paid a debt to a dead man. I gave my wife leave to go. And I told my son that his work was better than mine.

I do not know that the engine was needed for those. It may be that any man could do as much, upon any night, who was willing to open his mouth. I can only report that I was not able to until the hour had been let into the house, and that when it had, I was.

I went to my bed. I am told that I slept without the draught, and smiling.

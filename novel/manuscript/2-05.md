## 5. In Which the Ice Is Served

Mrs Pask did not come down in five minutes. She did not come down in ten. I sat at the kitchen table among the little plates and watched the hands of the big clock go round to one, and past it.

I may say that I've sat in some anxious kitchens. I once waited forty minutes in one in Beaune while a grower decided whether to sell to me or set the dog on. But I have never felt time pass as I felt it in that kitchen at Compline. Every clock in the house was getting ready to strike. One could hear them at it, through the floor, gathering themselves, like a congregation about to stand.

At five past one by the wall, the door at the top of the kitchen stairs burst open and Gail came down them at a gallop, with her cap over one ear.

"Where's Auntie?"

"Upstairs. She said five minutes. That was a quarter of an hour ago."

"Upstairs? What's she want upstairs? They're all in the hall! Mr Vane's stood at the front door with his hand on the bolt. Mr Fenn's got the champagne open. Where's the ice?"

"In the larder. On the slab."

"Is it turned out?"

"No. She said I wasn't to touch it."

"Oh, my Lord," said Gail. "Oh my Lord. He'll say it. He says it every year. He opens the door and says his bit, and then he turns round and says, 'Ah, Mrs Pask, the ice,' and it's *there*. On the table, with the violets on. It's never not been there. Not since before I was born." She ran into the stone passage and I heard the larder door. She came back out with her face puckered. "That's not our box."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The ice. I carried it out myself, when the lights went. It's in a wine box, what the port come in. Says Cockburn's on the side. That one's got tin on."

I felt a slight chill. I put it from me.

"It was at the bottom of the steps," I said. "By the ice. Exactly where she said, and the right size, and heavy. It's the only box there. I looked." I hadn't looked very hard, to be truthful. I had been thinking about my cousin.

Gail stood on one leg and chewed her lip. Above us, in the hall, the first clock began to strike. It had a silvery, hurried sort of voice. A second joined it.

"Well, it'll be the ice," she said. "What else'd be in an ice house? Auntie'll have changed the box on me. She does that. She says I pack like a navvy." She made up her mind. "There's no time to turn it out. You take it in."

"Me?"

"As it is. In the box. He likes to see it come in; he'll not mind the box. You put it down on the hall table and get the lid off, and I'll be right behind with the dish and the big spoon, and we'll turn it out in front of them. It'll look like it was meant." She was already loading plates onto a tray. "Go *on*, Mr Toby! They've started!"

They had. There were six or seven going now overhead, all on different notes and none of them together. It was like bell-ringers who have never practised.

I went and got the box.

It was as heavy as ever. I held it against my chest with both arms and went up the kitchen stairs backwards, pushing the baize door open with my shoulders. Gail had thrust a kitchen knife into my top pocket, for the cord. She was behind me with the tray. I could hear the plates chattering.

I thought, as I went up, of what I should say. It seemed an occasion for a few words. A stranger to the family, a young relation, makes his entrance at the solemn moment bearing the traditional dish. I thought of something light. *Uncle Leo, I'm afraid I've nothing to give you but what you have already.* No. *Uncle Leo. The ice cometh.* I liked that. I was rather taken with it. I was still polishing it as I came through the door into the hall.

* * *

I have to describe the hall as I found it, because I only saw it for about four seconds before events moved on, and I've had to piece the rest together.

They had lit every candle in the house. The place was like a church at Christmas. All the tall clocks were striking, and their pendulums winked. On the settle by the fire lay the vicar, as I'd left him, on his left side, with his teeth beside him. In a big chair on the other side of the hearth sat Dame Octavia Prowse, wide awake now, with her feet planted and a glass of champagne. Mr Fenn stood at the hall table by the Font, with the bottle.

My cousin's wife was there. She was standing by herself at the back, under the largest of the clocks, very straight, with her hands clasped in front of her. Her hair was damp. I noticed that, because she was otherwise so perfectly turned out. She looked like a woman who is sitting for her portrait and wishes it were finished.

Kit and the girl Saskia were on the bottom stair. They had plainly only just come in. Saskia had a man's overcoat on over the green dress, and there was hay on it. Kit had hay in his hair. He was the colour of the candles and he was holding on to the newel post. He looked at me as I came through the baize door. I thought it was the look of a man who has been waiting for something dreadful and sees it come in carrying a box. He had the wrong dreadful thing in mind, as it turned out, but I didn't know that.

There was no Edmund. I, of course, knew why. There was no Imogen, and no detective.

And at the far end, at the great front door, with his back to us all, stood my Uncle Leo. He'd let the rug fall off his shoulders. He was standing up straight, as tall as a hatstand, with one hand on the iron bolt. Miss Quaile was half a pace behind his left elbow.

The clocks finished. The last of them was a deep, slow old fellow by the stairs, who took his time over his one note and let it die. There was a moment in which the hall was quite silent, apart from the ticking, which I had stopped counting as noise.

"Now," said my uncle.

He drew the bolt. He took hold of the two leaves of the door, one in each hand, and pulled them open and walked them back against the wall. The night came in. All the candles down that end lay over flat, and some went out. I could see straight through, from where I stood with my box, across a stretch of wet gravel shining in the moon, to the stables, and the tower, and the pale face of the tower clock.

"Come in," said my Uncle Leo, to the dark. He spoke as one speaks to a guest who has kept the soup waiting. "Come in, then. You're late. You've been late since the last Sunday in March."

Out across the gravel, the tower clock began to strike. I could hear the little bell first, the *ting-tang*, four times. I could even see something moving, up at the top, under the roof. There was a light up there, swinging. I thought it must be part of the ceremony.

Then came the big bell. It struck once.

And there were two bangs.

I can only describe them as I heard them. They were very loud, and flat, and close together. *Bang.* And then, before one had drawn breath, *bang.* It was as if somebody had slammed the lid of a grand piano, twice, out in the yard. The echo came back off the front of the house.

Miss Quaile cried out. It wasn't a scream. It was a short, sharp sound, like someone who has put a hand on a hot stove. She had both hands up to her mouth and she was staring out through the open door, at the tower, with her eyes as wide as I ever saw them. My uncle didn't move at all. He stood in the doorway with his arms a little out from his sides, looking the same way.

"Ah," I heard him say. He sounded interested. "Yes. I thought there would be something to pay."

Nobody else had seen whatever it was. We were all behind them, down the hall, in the light. Mr Fenn said, "Thunder." Dame Octavia said, "Thunder my foot. That's a twelve-bore, both barrels. Somebody's after the duck." Saskia said, "At one in the morning?" and Dame Octavia said that it was the best time, under a moon, with the tide making, and that her first husband had never been home.

That seemed to settle it. It settled it for me. I had come to a stop just inside the baize door, with twenty-odd pounds of pudding in my arms and a speech prepared, and I had lost my moment. I could feel it going. A nicer judgment would have let it go.

"Uncle Leo!" I said.

He turned round. They all turned round.

"Uncle Leo," I said. "The ice cometh."

* * *

I advanced up the hall. I have made some entrances in my time, mostly backwards, but I think that was my best. I carried the box before me like a man bearing the crown at a coronation. I reached the table. Mr Fenn, with a solicitor's instinct, moved the Font out of the way. I set the box down.

"Mrs Pask has been detained," I said. "But she asked me particularly to see that the tradition was observed. I understand it's her forty-second." I drew the kitchen knife from my pocket, with a flourish, and cut the cord.

I lifted off the lid.

There was a blanket. It was a grey army blanket, folded. I was a little surprised. I had expected ice, and salt, and straw. But I reasoned that a blanket would keep the cold in very well, and I folded it back, left and right, like the flaps of an envelope.

Under the blanket there was a pillowcase, with something inside it. The something was about two feet tall. It was the right height for a bombe. It was hard, and cold to the touch, and it seemed to have corners.

I began to have doubts. But there is a point in any performance beyond which one cannot stop. Gail was at my elbow with the big dish. I took the pillowcase by its hem and drew it off upward, in one sweep, in the way that waiters lift the cover from a joint.

"Voilà," I said.

It was a clock.

I don't know how to convey the quality of the silence. I've told this story a good many times, and at this point people laugh, and I let them. But nobody laughed in the hall at Compline. There were eleven people there, if one counts the vicar, and they were all looking at a table. On it, in a nest of grey blanket, with fifty candles shining in its glass, stood a brass clock. It had a big white dial, and a lot of little ones, and a painted moon.

Gail said, "That's never the ice."

I looked at it. I looked into the box, in case the bombe was underneath. There was nothing underneath. I looked at my great-uncle. I believe I said, "It was at the bottom of the steps."

Several things happened in the hall, and I didn't see all of them. I heard a sharp sound from the stairs. That was Kit; he had sat down, suddenly, on the bottom tread, and put his head on his knees. I saw Mr Fenn take off his spectacles and close his eyes, like a man who has been expecting a bill and is nonetheless shocked by the figure. I saw my cousin's wife. She hadn't moved a muscle. She was looking at the clock, but she looked as if she were seeing something else, about a hundred yards behind it.

My Uncle Leo came down the hall.

He came quite slowly. He fitted the glass into his eye as he walked. He stopped at the table and bent from the waist, with his hands clasped behind his back, and looked at the clock. He looked at the face of it and then, moving round, at the side, and the back. He put out one finger and touched something underneath, very gently. He was a full minute about it. I counted the buttons on his waistcoat.

Then he straightened up and let the glass fall.

"Ah," he said. "The Copy. I wondered where I had put it."

I waited to be struck dead.

"It has stopped," he observed. "It will have been laid on its side. One must never lay it on its side; the moon work is suspended from a single cock and will not bear it. I made that cock in the winter of 1964, and I made it too light. I've always meant to do it again." He turned to me, and he smiled. He had a very sweet smile, when it came. "It was thoughtful of you to bring it, my boy. I am much obliged. It should not have been left in the damp. But I believe we were promised an ice."

"I—yes. Sir. I'm awfully sorry. I think there's been a mix-up with the boxes."

"There often is," said my Uncle Leo.

"Is it a clock or a pudding?" called Dame Octavia from her chair. She was a little deaf and had not left it. "I can't see from here. If it's a pudding, I'll have some."

"It's a clock, Octavia."

"Another one? Good God. Tell the boy to take it away and bring something one can eat."

* * *

I am aware that the reader, who is cleverer than I am, will have understood a good deal about that clock which I didn't.

I didn't know what it was. I had never been in the library by daylight. I had heard nothing about a Thorne Sidereal, or that there was such a thing in the house worth a million pounds and more. Nobody had told me that at twenty past ten that night, when the lights failed, it had gone from its case. All I knew was that I had been sent for a pudding and had come back with a clock, and that my great-uncle called it "the Copy" and seemed mildly pleased to see it.

It was years before I heard the rest. I heard it from a man in the trade who had a cousin in the auction business. He told me what a Thorne Sidereal was, and what had become of the real one, and asked me if it was true that I had once served it at a party. I said it was not true. I said I had served the copy. He said that came to the same thing.

So I can't tell you what Edmund was doing with that box in the ice house. I have my own idea. But I can tell you that I carried it into the hall and unveiled it in front of the whole family, on the stroke of one, with the words, "The ice cometh." And whatever plans had been laid for that clock that night, by whomever, I don't think they can have included that.

I've sometimes thought it was the most useful thing I ever did, and I did it by accident, as usual. Whether it was useful to the right people, I've never been able to work out.

* * *

They lifted it out. Mr Fenn and Kit did it between them, at my uncle's direction, and stood it on the table on its blanket. Kit's hands were shaking so that the little glass door rattled. He didn't once look at his mother.

I took the empty box back to the pantry. It seemed the least I could do. I had a feeling that one of us ought to be put away. I pushed it under the marble slab with its lid. I looked for Mrs Pask in the kitchen, but she had not come down. I called up the back stairs and got no answer. And here I must confess something odd. I thought, standing at the bottom of those stairs, that I could hear her, very faintly, a long way up. She was calling something. It sounded like a name, a short one, said over and over, soft and urgent, as one calls a cat that has got out.

I assumed she had a cat.

When I got back to the hall, Gail had rallied. She had fetched up a trifle that had been meant for Sunday. She served it onto the thirteen glass plates. One was set down on the hall table at an empty chair that somebody had brought through from the dining room. Nobody remarked on it. I ate mine. I was extremely hungry.

And then my Uncle Leo made a speech.

It was not like the one at dinner. He'd stood up for that. For this he sat, in a chair somebody had put for him near the open door, with the night at his back and the rug over his knees. His voice was quite different. At dinner he'd sounded like a judge. Now he sounded like a man talking to his family, late, when the guests have gone.

I didn't take it all in. I was at the back, with a plate. But I heard the heart of it, and I can give it very nearly as he said it, because I've never heard anything I liked better.

"I asked a question at dinner," he said. "I said that one of you had been robbing me, and that I should know which by one o'clock. Well, it is past one. And I find that I no longer want to be told."

Nobody spoke.

"I've been sitting here trying to think what a clock is for. I ought to know. I've given my life to them. It is for telling a man how much time he has got. That is all. It has no other use. And I have got a hundred and eleven of them, and not one has ever told me that. They have only told me how much I'd had."

He looked round at us. His eyes rested on the clock on the table, and went on.

"If any of you has taken anything from this house, I expect you wanted it. I expect you wanted it more than I did. I hadn't looked at half of them in years. I daresay there was a reason, and I daresay I'm some part of the reason, and I was not a father that a person could come to and ask. I know that. Your mother knew it. She used to say I could hear a watch losing two seconds a week and could not hear my own children." He was silent for a moment. "So I shall put it on the Hour's account. Whatever it is. The matter of the clocks is closed, and I don't wish to hear it spoken of in this house again. Lionel, you will kindly make a note of that. It's the only instruction I've given you in ten years that I am sure I mean."

"I have noted it, Leo," said Mr Fenn.

"Good. And there's another thing. Get your pen. There is something I owe across the water, at Saltcote, that has been owing a long time. I can't pay it to the man. I let him die without. I'll pay it to his name. You'll write it down as I say it, and Octavia shall witness it, and the vicar, if he can be made to hold a pen."

"The vicar's asleep, Leo," said Dame Octavia.

"Then wake him."

It took Gail and me and a wet napkin. But we got the Reverend Aubrey Stiles upright for long enough to write his name at the bottom of a paper, with his eyes shut and his teeth in the wrong way up. He said, "Bless you, my children," and went down again. I didn't know what the paper was. Mr Fenn folded it into a long envelope and put it in his breast pocket.

I should perhaps say that I had been listening to my uncle's speech with very mixed feelings.

On the one hand, it was plain by now that it wasn't the clock in the Blue Room he had meant. Something much larger was going on, and I'd blundered through the middle of it with a pudding box. I could see that. On the other hand, he had said "if any of you has taken anything." He had said "whatever it is." And I had, in the strictest sense, taken something. I had a third of it in my pocket at that moment. He had forgiven it, sight unseen, in front of a solicitor.

I felt a great weight go off me. This was premature, as most of my feelings are. But I felt it.

* * *

After that there was the dance.

I shall tell this part as plainly as I can. I've left it out of the after-dinner version altogether. It doesn't get a laugh, and it isn't meant to.

My uncle had finished with Mr Fenn. He sat for a little while with his eyes closed, and I thought he had dropped off. Then he opened them and said, "Kit. Wind the gramophone. I should like the Lehár."

There was a gramophone in the corner, a real one, with a brass horn like a lily. Kit went over to it. He seemed glad of something to do. He turned the handle and found a record in the cupboard underneath and put it on, and lowered the arm. There was a noise like frying. Then came a waltz, with a great many violins, very sweet and very slow to begin, the sort of thing they must have danced to at Compline when the old gentleman was a boy.

"'Gold and Silver,'" said Dame Octavia, to nobody. "He would. He danced that with me at the Hunt Ball in 1934. He trod on my foot and talked about escapements."

I was looking at the gramophone. I didn't see her come.

It was Saskia who saw first. She was sitting halfway up the stairs, in the overcoat. She said, "*Kit*," in a sort of gasp. I looked round.

Someone was coming down the stairs.

It was a woman in a grey dress. It was a long dress, of silk, cut very low at the back and plain in front, of the sort one sees in photographs of one's grandmother when she was presented. It was a soft, clear grey, and it took the candlelight in a way that made it look almost like water. She had dark hair, put up. She came down slowly, with one hand on the rail, looking straight ahead of her.

It was my cousin Imogen. I knew her at once, by the white streak in her hair. She had gone up and changed.

I thought it a little much. I may as well admit it. It was past one in the morning, everybody else was in what they had dined in, and it did seem to me a strange time to go and put on another frock. But it was a very pretty one. And she looked quite different in it. At dinner she had looked like a clever woman in black who mended pictures. She looked now like somebody about to be painted.

Nobody else, I gradually saw, was taking it as I was.

Dame Octavia had got up out of her chair. I had not seen her stand unassisted all evening. She was holding the back of it, and her big plain face had gone all to pieces. "Oh, my dear God," she said, under her breath. "Oh, Daisy." Mr Fenn had bowed his head. My cousin's wife had her hand at her own throat. Kit stood at the gramophone with his arm half out, and I saw his lips move, and I'm almost sure he said "Granny."

And Miss Quaile, who had been standing by my uncle's chair, took one step back, and another. Then she sat down on the floor with her back against the wall, as though her legs had been taken away. She was staring at my cousin Imogen as I hope never to be stared at by anyone.

I didn't understand it. I'd never been up to the long gallery. I had not seen the portrait at the end of it, as everyone else in that hall had, some of them every day for twenty years. I'd no way of knowing what dress it was, or whose.

My Uncle Leo had risen. He stood by his chair, quite still, with the rug at his feet.

"You came back," he said. "Margot. You came back."

My cousin Imogen came across the hall to him. I could see her face then. It was perfectly white, and wet, and she was smiling. She put out her hand.

"Yes, Papa," she said. And then, very low, and I think she'd been getting ready to say it all the way down the stairs: "Yes, Leo. I came back."

He took her hand. He put his other hand at her waist. And he danced with her.

I have said he was eighty-one. I've said he had to be helped to his chair. He waltzed with that woman in the grey dress the whole length of his hall and back, among the clocks, without a stumble. He held her as if she were made of something that might spill. His eyes were shut. Hers were open, and she was looking over his shoulder, and the tears ran down her face the whole time and she did nothing about them.

He talked to her as they went. I caught a little, when they came round by where I stood. It was a low, quick, urgent murmur, like a man giving directions to somebody who has a train to catch. He said something about the tide. He said, "ten minutes to one," and "not a minute before," and "a horse." He said, "I had it wrong. I had it wrong for you the last time, my dear, and I've got it right." She nodded. She kept nodding.

I'd taken a step forward. I don't know what I had in mind. I think I felt that somebody ought to dance, besides, to make it look less strange. I thought I might ask Dame Octavia.

A hand like a ham closed on my sleeve.

"Sit down, boy," said Dame Octavia Prowse. She wasn't looking at me. She was watching the two of them go round, with her old eyes swimming. "This one isn't for us."

So I sat down, on the stairs, a few steps below Saskia. I looked at nothing but my plate. I didn't understand what I was seeing. But I've been in enough churches to know when to keep my head down.

* * *

The record ran out. They stopped in the middle of the floor. I heard my uncle say, "There. There, my dear. You see. It's quite all right. We had the time after all."

She said something to him that I couldn't hear. She kissed him, on the forehead, and held his face for a moment between her hands. Then she let him go, and turned, and went away across the hall toward the garden passage. She went quickly, in the grey dress, and she didn't look back at any of us.

My uncle stood where she had left him. He looked very tired, suddenly, and very old, and entirely happy. Miss Quaile had got up off the floor. She went to him. She moved like a person walking in her sleep. She put her hand under his elbow, and he let her lead him toward his chair.

Kit shut the lid of the gramophone.

I thought someone would say something, and I waited, and nobody did. Dame Octavia lowered herself back into her chair and held out her glass for more champagne, without a word, and Mr Fenn filled it. Celia was gone; I had not seen her go. Saskia was crying into the collar of the overcoat. Kit went past me up the stairs and sat down beside her and put his arm round her.

It was, I suppose, about twenty-five past one.

I looked across the hall at the nurse. She had settled the old man. She was standing by his chair with her hands hanging at her sides. Her face was grey. She had the look of someone who has been ill for a week. She was looking through the open front door, across the gravel, at the tower with the clock on it. I thought she looked as if she were waiting for it to do something else.

I should have left her alone. Any man of sense would have. She had clearly had some kind of shock. I didn't know what kind. I thought the business with the dress had upset her, as it had upset everybody. I didn't know that she'd also seen something out of that door at one o'clock that the rest of us had not.

But I had been told by an expert that in this hour nothing counted. One could ask for anything. I had meant to ask my uncle for five thousand pounds, and I found, looking at Miss Quaile, that I had lost all interest in the Thracian valley.

I put down my plate. I pulled my cuffs down, as far as they would go. And I went across to her.

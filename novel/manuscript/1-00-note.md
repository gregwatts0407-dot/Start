# PART ONE {.part}

::: {.parttitle}
Dead Man's Hour

*by Harry Cade*
:::

## Editor's Note {.note}

Henry Arthur Cade was a detective sergeant in the Metropolitan Police until 1988 and a private enquiry agent, working mostly for solicitors, from then until his knees gave out. He died in a nursing home in Southend-on-Sea in the spring of 2019, and the manager, who had no next of kin to send his effects to, kept a cardboard box of them in her office for longer than the rules required because, she told me, she had liked him. The box held a Ronson lighter, a wristwatch with a cracked glass, some photographs of a girl on a pony, and two hundred and eleven sheets of typescript in a Basildon Bond box, typed on a machine with a damaged *e*.

The title page says *Dead Man's Hour* and under that, *a novel*, and somebody, I assume Mr Cade, has crossed out *a novel* in blue ballpoint. From internal evidence he began it in the winter of 1995 and went back to it, on and off, for the rest of his life; the last chapters were typed in the home. He sent it to nobody. There is no carbon.

It is written in the manner of the American paperbacks he liked, of which there were about forty in the room where he died. I do not think the manner is an affectation. I think it is how he had come to see things, after the kind of working life he had, and that by 1994 he could no more have looked at a woman opening a door without wondering what she wanted than he could have looked at a clock without reading it.

He is the only one of the seven who was paid to be there, and the only one who was trained to observe. For both reasons he is the most confident of them. You should know that he had been drinking on the night in question, though less than he implies; that he was struck on the head at about ten past midnight, which he tells you himself; and that every time he gives is from his own wristwatch, which he had declined to surrender at the door and which was therefore, for most of the night, the only timepiece on the island that agreed with the rest of England. I have had the watch examined. It is a Tissot, and it keeps good time still.

He names real people. All of those who could be harmed by it are dead, with one exception, and I have asked her.

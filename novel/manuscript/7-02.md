## 2. Clues

The notebook is a spiral-bound school one with a blue cover, bought at a drugstore in Pincher Creek, Alberta. I had begun it on the aeroplane. The first six pages are about the Atlantic Ocean, which I could not see, and a list of what my father had told me about England, with ticks against the things that turned out to be true.

On the seventh page I started again. I am going to give you what is written there. I have not corrected the spelling, and where I have left something out I say so. I was nine. I had a wristwatch that my father had set that morning by the wireless, a Casio with a light in it, of which I was very proud, and I had lately read eleven books about a girl who solved mysteries. That is all the equipment I had. It turned out to be more than anyone else in the house.

**THE SECRET OF COMPLINE HOUSE**
**by Wren Garrow age 9¾**
**Saturday Oct 22**

*9.04 a.m. In Mrs Pasks van. Under a sack. It smells of goose. Gail says if I sneeze she will sit on me. We are on the sea road. I am not on it. I am IN A VAN on it. That is diffrent. I did not set foot.*

I want you to notice that. I had been made to promise the evening before, and I was a truthful child, and I spent the whole of that crossing working out whether I was breaking my word. I decided that I was not, and wrote it down so that I could show my father if it came up.

I should say how I came to be under the sack. Mrs Pask has had some hard things said about her in this book, by implication, and I would like to put her side.

She had agreed with my father that her sister would mind me. But Vi was eighty-three and deaf, and the week before she had put a kettle on the gas and gone to sleep in her chair, and Dolly had come home to a kitchen full of it. Dolly was to be on the island from nine in the morning until the road dried on Sunday. She stood in her yard that morning with the van loaded, and looked at Vi, and looked at me, and I saw her change her mind.

"I can't leave her with you," she said to her sister. "Not for a day and a night. I'd never forgive myself."

"Eh?" said Vi.

"Get in the back," Dolly said to me. "Under that. Quick, before your dad looks round."

She meant it for the best. She thought that she would put me in a room at the top of a big house, with a plate of food and a rocking horse, where nobody went, and that I should be safer there than with an old woman and a gas stove. She told Vi what to say if my father came. She did not think that he would come. She thought that he would be at the lawyer's all day and asleep by nine.

She could not tell him, you see. That was the difficulty. He would have asked where I was to be, and she would have had to say *at Compline*, and he would have said no. And she could not have told the Vanes that she had brought a Garrow child into the house. Mr Edmund had sent a notice to the farm that week. There was bad blood. She had her place to think of. She was sixty-three.

So she hid me. It was a small deception by a tired, decent woman, to get through one difficult day. I have put her name on a stone in St Botolph's churchyard, next to her husband's, and I take flowers.

*9.31. We are there. Up the back stairs with a box on my head so Im a parsel. Mrs P says NOT A SOUND WREN GARROW. There are 4 flights. A room at the top. It has BARS on the window like a jail. There is a rocking horse with a red saddle and most of his hair gone. A bed. A cubbord with animals painted. It smells of mice.*

*RULES (Mrs P):*
*1. Stay in this room.*
*2. Not a sound.*
*3. If anybody comes, under the bed.*
*4. She will bring my dinner.*
*5. Tomorrow when the sea goes down she will take me home and DAD IS NOT TO KNOW.*

*I asked why its a secret. She said becos the people here and the Garrows dont speak and if they found one in their nursry she would lose her place. I said what did we do. She said its not what you did, its what was done. Then she locked the door.*

*She LOCKED it. I heard.*

*9.50. I am a PRISONER in a house on an island and nobody knows I am here exept the cook. This is the best thing that has ever happened to me.*

I am afraid that is exactly what it says.

* * *

I shall go through the day more quickly than the notebook does. There are fourteen pages before dark. A good deal of it is about the rocking horse.

*10.15. CLUE NO. 1. There is a clock in this room. It says 9.15. My watch says 10.15. I thot it was broke. But I can here another one thru the floor and I counted when it struck and it struck 9. So thats TWO and they are BOTH an hour wrong THE SAME WAY.*

*That is not broke. If they were broke they would be wrong diffrent. Somebody has done it ON PURPOSE.*

*WHY would you make all your clocks wrong?*

I had it in the first hour. I had it before the detective sergeant had got his loupe out. I do not say that to boast. He was looking for something complicated. I was looking at a clock. And I had not been told by a solicitor to expect it, or by a cook to put my watch back. I simply had two times in front of me and wanted to know which was lying.

I never did put my watch back. I kept it on Alberta habits: you set it by the radio and you leave it alone. So the times in this notebook are the same as Mr Cade's, and my father's. They are the rest of England's. I was the only person inside that house, apart from a detective with a Tissot in his sock, who knew all night what time it really was. And I was the only person anywhere who wrote down both.

*11.02. I am OUT.*

*It was easy. Its in my book (The Clue in the Old Mill). She left the key in the lock on the OUTSIDE. There is a gap under the door. You get a peice of paper and push it under so its flat on the floor outside. Then you poke the key out with a pencil from inside. It falls on the paper. You pull the paper back. The key comes with it.*

*It worked FIRST TIME. I did not think it would. In the book it takes her 3 goes.*

*I am going to put it back after so she wont know. I am only going to look. I will not make a sound.*

*11.10. There is a little door in the wall on the landing with ropes in. I looked down. It is a LIFT but tiny. For dinners. It goes all the way down. I can FIT.*

I could. I was small for nine. I rode in that dumb waiter, I should think, fifteen times in the course of the day. If you pull on the one rope you go down and on the other you go up, and it stops at three places: the nursery landing; a little serving pantry behind the dining room, with a hatch that looks into it; and the larder off the kitchen. It was slow, and it creaked, and it was the most wonderful thing I had ever been in. I thought of it as mine.

It was through the hatch in the serving pantry that I watched the dinner. It was in the larder, at five past one in the morning, that the rope jammed and I spent what I told Mr Pargeter was ages. I had been in there eleven minutes. I have the time.

*11.40. This house is FULL of clocks. I have counted 31 and I have only done 2 floors. They are all an hour wrong. They all tick but not together. It sounds like a lot of people wispering about you.*

*There is a long room with windows and a pictur at the end of a lady in a gray dress. She is looking at you. I went from one side to the other and she still was. She looks like she knows a joke. I like her. I think she is the lady who drowned. Under her is a clock that goes cuh. cuh. like it has a cold.*

*There is a door next to her that is locked. No key in it. I looked thru the keyhole. A bed. SHOES on the floor. Nobody in.*

*12.05. Had to hide. A man came. I got behind a curtin.*

*He is not from here. He has a raincoat over his arm and a magnifing glass and a lot of photos. He goes up to a clock and opens it like hes scared of it and looks INSIDE at the back. Then he looks at a photo. Then he writes in a book. He did 4 while I watched. On 2 of them he shook his head.*

*He has shoes like the police have. I think he is a DETECTIVE in disgize. So there is a CRIME. I knew it.*

*What is he looking for at the back of the clocks?*

I followed Mr Cade for most of an hour. He never saw me. He was, I think, the best-trained observer who was ever in that house, and I walked behind him through four rooms. It is not that he was careless. It is that there are some things a grown man looking for a thief does not look for, and one of them is a child of nine standing very still behind a curtain. He was right about what a detective is worth. You see everything. You could pass an examination on it. He simply had not been told that I was on the paper.

I worked out what he was doing by going back afterwards to a clock that he had shaken his head at, and one that he had not, and opening them myself. I had to stand on a chair.

*12.30. CLUE NO. 2. I looked where he looked. In the ones he liked there is a NAME cut in the metal at the back. Tho. Tompion Londini. In the ones he didnt like there is NO NAME. Just smooth.*

*So some of the clocks are REAL and some are FAKES and he is finding out which.*

*Somebody has been taking the real ones and putting fakes. That is the CRIME. Its like in The Hidden Staircase only with clocks.*

I have that in my handwriting, at half past twelve in the afternoon, eight hours before Leopold Vane stood up at his own table to announce it. I had no idea that it was a secret. I thought that everyone must know.

* * *

*1.30 p.m. Mrs P came with bread and cheese and 3 little oranges. I was back in time. I had put the key back in the outside and shut the door and I was on the horse. She said have you been good. I said yes. She said have you made a sound. I said no. Both true.*

*She said theres a lady going about, a nurse, pale, in a blue dress, and if she comes up here I am to be SILENT AS THE GRAVE becos she notises things.*

*I said who else is in the house.*

*SUSPECTS (from Mrs P and from looking):*

*1. MR EDMUND. Big. Limps. Drinks. He is the son. Mrs P says he is to be pitied. She would not say why. He sent the paper to Dad. So he is the VILLAN, probly.*

*2. THE DETECTIVE (I am not ment to know). Mrs P says he is from the insurence. He is not.*

*3. MRS EDMUND. Fair hair. Pretty but old. Mrs P says she was on the stage.*

*4. THE OLD MAN. Mr Vane. He is 81. He made all the clocks wrong. Mrs P says he is not right in his head but she says it like she likes him.*

*5. MISS IMOGEN. The daughter. She is the lady who touched the box on the road. I saw her on the stairs. She has a white bit in her hair like a skunk. She looks like the pictur.*

*6. KIT. He is the grandson. He plays piano. He has a girlfreind with NO HAIR.*

*7. THE NURSE. Have not seen yet.*

*Mrs P said there are more coming for dinner. A vicar and a lawyer and a Dame. I said what is a Dame. She said a lady who has shot more foxes than you have had hot dinners.*

*She said there will be 13 at table and one of them a ghost. I said WHAT. She said never you mind, and went.*

*She forgot to lock it this time!!! No she didnt. I heard. But she took the key.*

*That is all right. I have the lift.*

I did not need the door after that. I went down the shaft.

I am going to pass over the early afternoon, except for one thing. At a little after three by my watch I was in the long gallery, on my stomach, with my ear against a place where the floorboards did not meet. The library is underneath. I had been listening to the clock down there, which had more ticks in it than any of the others, when two people came in.

*3.10. In the libary under me. The nurse (I think, a little quiet voice) and the Big Man. I cant here all.*

*She said I should like to speak to you. He made a joke. She did not laff.*

*She has a BOOK. She found it in a clock. He said have you read it. She said yes. He said all of it. She said to the end.*

*Then she said DID YOU FOLLOW HER.*

*He did not say anything for ages. Then he said YES.*

*Thats a CONFESSION. But I dont know what to. Who is her?*

*He said he would tell his father tomorrow. He said after breakfast. He said if I am not there you tell him EDMUND SAID HE WAS SORRY AND IT WASNT THE CLOCK.*

*What wasnt the clock???*

*He sounded sad. Villans in books do not sound like that.*

I underlined the last sentence twice. I can see where the pencil went through.

It is the first place in the notebook where the case stops being a game. I did not know it then. I only knew that I had gone down on that floor to listen to a man who had sent my father a paper, and had heard somebody who sounded as my father had sounded, by the range, the night my grandfather died.

* * *

*4.55. I was singing. I FORGOT.*

*I was on the horse and I was singing Four Strong Winds, just the start becos its all I know, and I heard FEET on the stairs. Fast.*

*I got off. I got against the wall by the door where the keyhole cant see. The horse was still going.*

*Somebody tried the door. Then knocked. A lady said is someone there. I didnt breathe.*

*Then Mrs Pask came up. She was out of breath. I heard it all. The lady said she herd a child singing in the chimney. Mrs P said its the WIND. The lady said there is no wind. Mrs P said theres always wind at Compline.*

*The lady said the horse was rocking. Mrs P said its the draft.*

*The lady said I SMELT ORANGES.*

*I had just eaten the last one. The peel is on the table.*

*Mrs P said she has a box in the kitchen. That is a LIE. She gave them all to me. I have never heard a grownup lie that good.*

*She said THERE IS NO CHILD AT COMPLINE. She said it like in church.*

*The lady went. Mrs P waited. Then she said thru the door very quiet: Wren Garrow. I will skin you.*

That was Hester Quaile. She has written about that afternoon herself, and I have compared the two. She says that she smelled oranges on the landing, and I had the peel on the table. She says that the rocking horse was moving as a horse moves after someone has got down. I had got down four seconds before.

She believed Mrs Pask. She says so. A sensible woman of sixty-three, with flour on her arms, told her to her face that there was no child, and she went downstairs and lay on her bed and told herself that the flue sang.

I have thought about what that cost her. She came to Compline as a nurse, with a rule about telling the truth and a trained eye. She heard a child. She was right. And she was told, by someone she had every reason to trust, that she had not; and from that minute she could no longer be sure what in that house was real. Eight hours later she stood on a landing and watched a woman in grey come down the gallery, and she had already been taught that afternoon that her ears could not be relied upon.

I do not say that Dolly Pask made Hester see a ghost. But she did take away the ground Hester was standing on, and I was the reason.

*5.15. I have drawn the tower. You can see it out the back window on the stairs. There are 2 people on top. Wooden. A man who hits the little bell and a lady who hits the big one. The boy with the piano and the girl with no hair went up and put CLOTHES on them. A black jacket on the man. A scarf on the lady. They were laffing.*

*The lady is gray like the pictur. She has her arm up all the time waiting. She doesnt look at the bell. She looks at the house.*

That is the drawing that Hester found on the floor. I had written under it what she copied down. I dropped it at five to one in the morning, when I came back into that room running, and did not know that it was gone.

* * *

I shall give you two more entries from the daylight, since they both come into it later, and then the dinner.

*6.30. Nearly CAUGHT. I was in a blue bedroom looking at the clock in there (No. 44) when the big girl came in with towels. I got under the bed. It has a frill. She went. I was coming out when ANOTHER one came in.*

*He is new. Very tall, pink, wet. He was holding his shoes. He took off his sweater and knocked the clock off the table with his elbow. It rolled RIGHT UNDER THE BED. It stopped by my foot.*

*He came under after it.*

*I thot thats it. I pulled my legs up. There is an old suitcase under there and I was behind it. He was on his tummy. He got hold of the clock. He sneezed. He banged his head. Then he put his KNEE on it.*

*It went crunch. A bit flew out.*

*He said OH I SAY.*

*He sat on the floor and looked at it for ages. He looked like he was going to cry. Then he put ALL THE BITS in the pocket of a black jacket that was hanging on the door.*

*He never saw me. I was 2 feet away.*

*I dont think he is the theif. A theif would not break it. He is just an accident. But he has HIDDEN THE EVIDENSE so I am putting him down.*

*8. TALL PINK MAN. Broke clock no. 44. Hid it in his pocket. Not on purpose.*

I have had that entry open in front of Toby Pargeter. He read it standing, in the hall at Compline, with his reading glasses on the end of his nose. He was fifty-nine. He went the colour he says in his memoir that he went, and then he sat down on the stairs and laughed until his wife had to fetch him a glass of water.

"Two feet," he said. "Two feet away. I've told that story for thirty years. I've done the budgerigar at weddings. And there was a small Canadian under the bed the whole time, taking notes."

And the other.

*6.50. CLUE NO. 5. The Detective has a room on the 1st floor at the back. I looked thru the keyhole.*

*He was sitting on the bed. He took his WATCH off. He looked at it. Then he pulled up his trouser and put it DOWN HIS SOCK.*

*Why would a person put a watch in their sock?*

*7.05. I know why. I watched from the top of the stairs. Everyone who comes in has to put their watch in a big bowl in the hall. The fair lady holds her hand out. The Dame threw hers. The Pink Man had a MICKEY MOUSE one (!!) and the Old Man picked it up and said it was honnest.*

*The Detective said I DONT WEAR ONE.*

*That is a LIE. Its in his sock.*

*So he is the only one in the house (exept me) who has a watch that says the real time. The rest have only got the wrong clocks.*

*He wants to know what time it REALLY is. Good. So do I.*

I had not then seen what it would come to. But I had seen the thing itself. There were two times in that house, an hour apart, and from seven o'clock that evening every person in it except two had been separated from the true one. They would be told the hour, all night, by a hundred and eleven dials that were all agreed with one another and all, as far as the rest of England was concerned, wrong. And they would each remember, afterward, what the dials had said.

I wrote one more line under that entry. I had drawn a box round it.

*If somebody says something happened at 1 o clock, you have to ask WHICH 1 o clock.*

It took the adults thirty years.

* * *

*9.10 p.m. (8.10 by the house). DINNER. I am in the little pantry behind the dining room. There is a hatch. If you open it a crack you can see the whole table. I am in the lift with my knees up so if anyone comes I can pull the rope.*

*There are 12 people. I counted. But 13 PLACES.*

*The emty one is next to the Old Man. They put soup at it. Then they took the soup away. Then goose.*

*He talks to it. He turns his head and says something and waits.*

*That is the ghost. That is the lady who drowned. It is her chair.*

*I am not scared. He isnt. He looks at it like Dad used to look at the door when Mom was in the hospitel and it was time for her to come back from the tests.*

I am not able to say anything about that entry. I will only point out that the others who sat at that table have each described the chair, and that none of them describes it as I did. The detective says it was doing most of the talking. The young man in love counted the table and got thirteen and was uneasy. The nurse says that it was cold on that side of her. Imogen kept it at the edge of her eye.

I was nine, and I had seen a person look at a door. So I knew what the chair was for. It was the only thing in that house which I understood better than anybody.

*9.40. The Old Man stood up. He said TO ABSENT TIME. They all said it.*

*Then he said ONE OF YOU HAS BEEN ROBBING ME.*

*!!!!!!*

*I WAS RIGHT. Its the clocks. He said somebody has been taking them and leaving copies. He said he made the copies himself. He said he could tell blindfold.*

*He said I dont know which of you it is. He said I shall know by one o clock.*

*This is a REAL CASE. It is not in a book. I am in it.*

*WHO LOOKED GILTY:*

*Pink Man went white and held his pocket. But I know what is in his pocket and its only No. 44.*

*The fair lady looked at Big Man.*

*The piano boy looked at Big Man.*

*The nurse looked at her lap.*

*The skunk lady looked at the emty chair.*

*The Detective did not look at anyone. He was eating.*

*BIG MAN LOOKED AT THE DETECTIVE AND SMILED.*

*That is the one. You dont smile if its not you. And you smile at the detective if you KNOW he is one.*

*So: it is MR EDMUND. Suspect No. 1. I said so at 1.30.*

*But he is the one who was sad in the libary. He is the one who said yes.*

*I dont like this as much as I thot I would.*

There is one more line on that page. It is in a different pencil, blunter. I wrote it at five past ten, by my watch, in the nursery, when I had gone up to fetch my coat and had looked out of the barred window at the dark where the road had been.

*10.05. The sea is over the road now. Nobody can get on or off. In the books that is called a CLOSED CIRCLE and it is when the murder happens.*

*I dont want there to be a murder.*

*I am going to watch Suspect No. 1 and see he doesnt do anything.*

I had the wrong person down as the one who needed stopping. Everybody did. But I ask you to notice what a child of nine resolved to do, at five past ten, on the top floor of that house. Mr Cade was hired by a wife that night to stay near Edmund Vane. Hester Quaile loved him. His father followed him with a piece of lead and his sister with twenty years of anger. My own father came for him through seven feet of water.

I was the only one of them who actually did stay near him. I was behind him, or beside him, or above him, with a notebook, from twenty-five past eleven until five o'clock in the morning.

It is why he is dead. I am going to show you that. I am going to show you also that it is why none of the others killed him.

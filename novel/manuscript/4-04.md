## Chapter Four

The key turned. The cold of the room came round her.

She set the candle on the dressing table, where three flames looked back at her out of the triple glass. She did not give herself time to think. If she thought, she would stop. She would stand in the middle of the carpet for twenty years.

She unhooked the black dress and let it fall. She stepped out of her shoes. She stood in her slip, with the gooseflesh coming up on her arms, and opened the wardrobe.

It was at the end of the rail. She pulled the cotton bag off it and dropped the bag on the floor.

It went on over her head like cold water. She had been afraid that it would not fit, and then that it would. It fitted. It settled on her shoulders and at her waist as though it had been cut to her that morning. She had her mother's bones, Octavia had said. She did up the hooks at the side with fingers that would hardly obey her. The silk lay against her skin and began, slowly, to take her warmth.

There had been a reason. She had found it on the stairs, coming up, and she held on to it now. Her mother had taken off this dress, in this room, to go to a Garrow, and had not arrived. She had been stopped halfway. And Imogen, who was to have gone with her, had stayed behind a vine.

She was going down to him now. She would go as her mother ought to have gone, in the dress that had been left on its hanger. It was not for him to see. He had never seen it; he had never been inside this house. It was so that the dress should get there. She knew that it was not sensible. She was past sensible. She had been sensible since 1974.

She sat down at the glass. She took the pins out of her hair and put it up again, higher, as her mother had worn hers. She used her mother's brush. Three women looked at her out of the three mirrors, in grey, with bare shoulders. They had her mother's eyes. They were not careful.

She saw the white lock at her temple. She did not try to hide it. That was hers.

She could not wear her own black shoes. And she could not, she found, so much as touch the satin pair by the bed. Those were not to be moved. But on the floor of the wardrobe there were others, in a row, and among them some grey kid sandals with a low heel. She took them up. She would not put them on yet. The back stairs creaked, and the passage was stone.

*The Wade eats shoes,* her mother had said, laughing, on that bed.

Imogen took the candle in one hand and the sandals in the other. She went out, and drew the door to, and left the key in the lock. She did not want it any longer.

The lamp under the portrait had been lit again. She stopped beneath it for one moment. She looked up.

She had expected to feel like a thief. Her mother stood in the frame in the same silk, with her hand on the chair and her lips apart. And for the first time in twenty years Imogen looked into those eyes and did not see a question there. They had the look that her mother wore when somebody at last did the thing she had been waiting for them to think of.

"I'm going over," Imogen said. "I'm sorry it's so late."

Under the frame the cracked clock drew breath to strike.

She went down the gallery in her stockinged feet. The long floor was cold. All about her the clocks began to tell midnight, one after another, in the hall and on the stairs and behind the shut doors. She did not look to right or left. She passed the head of the main staircase without turning her head. She was looking at the dark arch of the back stair, and through it, in her mind, at a glasshouse with a vine.

She did not see anyone on the landing. She has been told since that someone was standing there. She is sorry. She would not, that night, have seen her own father.

* * *

The orangery was colder than the house and darker than the night.

She let herself in by the garden passage. The old door had dropped on its hinges and had to be lifted. Inside there was the smell. Wet earth, and rot, and something faintly sweet. She had not smelled it for twenty years, and it took the strength out of her knees.

The vine had gone on without her. It had been a great thing then. It was a monster now. The stem by the door was as thick as her waist, and it had gone up through the roof and along the ridge and down the far wall, and filled every pane that was left with brown leaves. Half of them had fallen. They lay in drifts on the brick floor, and the wind got in at the broken lights and turned them over. Up in the dark there were still bunches hanging, shrivelled black, that no one had picked.

The rain had thinned. She could hear it ticking on the glass.

She found the two upturned pots. They were where they had always been, in the corner by the stove pipe. She set the candle on one. She put her sandals on, at last, and stood up in them, and waited.

She did not know that he would come here. He might go straight to the house. He might go to the stables, to get the mare under cover. She had only the certainty that she had had at the window. He would have to bring the horse somewhere out of the wind, and this was the nearest roof to where the Wade came ashore, and he had known the way to it blind.

She was shaking. She told herself that it was the cold.

Through the wall, in the house, the last of the clocks finished. There was a silence. Then, from across the yard, slow and deep, the tower began. She counted the strokes. She had counted them from this corner on many nights, with her hand in his.

The twelfth had not died when she heard the hoofs.

They were on the brick path outside, slow and heavy and tired. There was a jingle. A voice said something low. The door at the far end, the wide one that the gardeners had used for the tubs, groaned and gave.

At midnight Tom Garrow was in the orangery with the rain on his shoulders and twenty years in his face.

He came in backwards, leading her. The mare filled the doorway, white in the dark, with her head down and the water running off her. He had a lantern hung from the saddle horn, turned low. He was soaked to the chest. His hat was gone; his hair lay flat and dark on his forehead. He had one hand on the mare's neck and was talking to her, as she had first heard him talk, a string of words with no gaps. *There. That's my old girl. That's the last of it. You're under a roof.*

He got her in, and turned to shut the door on the wind.

He saw the candle. He saw who stood beside it.

He stopped as though he had walked into a wall. All the colour went out of his brown face. His hand came up, slowly, the way a man's does who is not sure of what is in front of him.

"Mrs Vane," he said. It was a whisper.

She had not thought of that. She had not thought what it would be to him. He had been twenty-three. He had seen her mother a hundred times, across a yard, on a horse. And he was looking now, by one candle, at a woman in grey silk with her hair up, in the place where the Garrows had lost her.

"No," she said. "Tom. It's me."

She stepped forward so that the light fell on the white in her hair.

He looked at it, and at her face. She watched him find her in it, a piece at a time. The mare shook herself, and the bit rang.

"Imogen," he said.

He made it sound, as he always had, like the name of a place.

* * *

Neither of them moved. There were ten feet of brick floor between them, and dead leaves.

"Dolly said you were in Scotland," he said at last. His voice had deepened. There was something laid over it now, slow and flat, that was not Essex. "She said you never came home, this time of year."

"I never have. Until now."

"I'd not have come this way if I'd known. I'd not have frightened you."

"You didn't frighten me. I was waiting for you."

He took that in.

"I saw your light," she said. "From the drawing-room window. I watched you the whole way. I watched you put her at the Rill." Her voice shook. "You might have been drowned. Both of you. There's six feet in it."

"There's nearer seven. She swam it."

"She's twenty years old!"

"She knows that water better than I do. She was foaled beside it." He laid his hand on the mare's wet shoulder. "She went in of herself. I'd have turned her. I sat her on the edge of it and I thought, no, that's too much to ask, and she took the bit and went. I only had to stay on." His mouth moved, not quite a smile. "You named her right. There's something not canny about her."

Imogen laughed. It came out like a sob. She put her hand over her mouth.

He was looking at her. He looked at her face, and the dress, and her bare arms, slowly and completely, the way he looked at everything. She saw his eyes come to her left hand, and rest there, and go away.

"You've not changed," he said.

"I'm forty-four. I've a white streak like a badger and I wear spectacles to read."

"I said you've not changed. I never said you'd not got older." He wiped the wet from his face with his sleeve. "I have. I know it. I've seen myself in a shop window."

"You look like someone who has been out in the weather."

"That's about the size of it. There's a lot of weather, where I've been."

"I had your photograph. The yellow grass and the mountains."

"I didn't know if it would come to you."

"It came. I ought to have written. I looked at it for a year, trying to find something to say back that was good enough, and then it was too late." She took a breath. "Tom. I'm sorry about your father. I'm so very sorry."

"He was ready." His face closed. "He'd been ready a long time."

He turned from her. He began to see to the mare. He loosened the girth and lifted the saddle off and set it on the edge of a stone trough. He took a handful of dead leaves and began to rub her down, in long strokes, along the back and the quarters. She stood with her head hanging and let him. He did it with his whole attention.

Imogen watched his hands. They were the same hands. They were broader and scarred across the knuckles, and one of the fingers had been broken and set crooked.

Then she saw what else had been on the saddle.

It had been strapped along the near side, under the flap, in a long case of oiled canvas. He had laid the saddle down with that side uppermost. The wet canvas had fallen open at the end. There was a foot of dark wood showing, and beyond the wood, blue steel.

She had been taught to shoot on the sea wall when she was ten. She knew what it was.

"What is that for?" she said.

He did not look round. He went on with the mare.

"Tom. What is the gun for?"

"Vane," he said.

* * *

It was only the one word. He did not raise his voice. He might have been telling her the name of a field.

She had known. She had known at the window. But it was different to see it there, on the stone trough, with the candle on the barrels.

"My brother."

"I've a thing to ask him. He'll not answer it for me without. He never answered a Garrow in his life but to tell him what he owed." He threw down the leaves and took up more. "I'll not hurt him, if he tells me. I want him to have that to look at while he does."

"What do you want to ask him?"

He was silent for so long that she thought he would not say. The mare sighed, and shifted her weight.

"My father talked, at the end," he said. "The last night. He'd not said twenty words to me on the telephone in twenty year. I got there the Monday. He was propped up in the kitchen, in the chair, he'd not go to bed. He knew me. He said, 'You've took your time.' Then he never spoke till about three in the morning."

He was rubbing the mare's near foreleg, bent, so that she could not see his face.

"He said he'd stood on the wall that night with the lantern. From one o'clock. He'd the horse saddled, the mare's dam. He saw her light leave the house. Your mother's. He said he knew it for hers the way it moved; she walked quick. He watched it come down onto the Wade and out. He said it was the longest quarter of an hour of his life, and the best. She'd said he wasn't to come to meet her. She'd said, I'll walk off that island on my own feet and you'll stand and watch me come."

"Yes," Imogen whispered. "She said that."

"She got three parts over. She was past the Rill, past the Perch. He could near enough see her face. He said he'd his hand up to call to her." Tom straightened. He stood with the wet leaves in his fist. "And there come another light. From the house. Fast. It come down onto the Wade and out after her like something let off a chain. Dad said he knew that one too. He said there was only one man on the Ness ran like that, with a hitch."

Imogen had put her hand on the cold iron of the stove pipe.

"It caught her up. A hundred and fifty yards from where Dad stood. He heard voices. He couldn't hear words, the wind was wrong. He said a man's voice, loud, and hers, low. He started down the ramp. And he stopped, because she'd told him. *You stand and watch me come.* He thought, she'll deal with it. She'll send the boy home and come on."

"They stood there," Imogen said. "The two lights. They stood a long time."

His head came round.

"He said an hour. He said he stood on that ramp an hour with the lantern held up, so she'd see where he was. He said the tide turned. He felt it turn. He shouted then. He said he shouted till he'd no voice. And the two lights stood there, with the water coming. And then hers turned round."

He stopped. His jaw was working.

"It went back. Toward the house. Away from him. The other one went with it. He watched her light go all the way back to the Rill, and there it went out. And the water come over the road. He stood there till it was up to his knees on the ramp."

"Tom—"

"He never knew. That's what he said to me. He said, 'I never knew, boy, if she turned back of herself or was turned. I never knew if she thought better of me at the last, with the water coming, and chose the house. Or if that drunken young devil took hold of her and dragged her.' He said he'd lain awake on it twenty year. He said he'd sooner she'd been dragged, God forgive him, because then she'd still have been coming." Tom's voice had gone rough. "He'd got hold of my wrist. He'd no strength in him and I couldn't have broke that grip. He said, 'Find out, Tom. You go over there and you ask Edmund Vane which it was. And you make him tell you.' That was the last thing he said to me that had sense in it. He went about six."

The rain ticked on the glass. Up in the roof a dead leaf let go and came down, turning, through the candlelight.

"So that's what the gun's for," Tom said. "I've come to ask him. I buried Dad Thursday. I come back from the church and there's a paper from your brother's lawyer to say we're to be off the ground by Lady Day, a hundred and eighty year, for caravans. I stood in Dolly Pask's kitchen with that in my hand and I thought, well, that's plain enough. He's took the woman off my father, and now he's took the land, and he's never once had to stand in front of a Garrow and say why."

He looked at her. His eyes were as she remembered them, grey-blue, steady, with the lines cut deep round them now.

"I came for Vane," he said. "I'll not lie to you. I never have. I didn't know you were in the house. I put the mare at that water tonight to find your brother, wherever he's hid himself, and make him tell me how your mother died."

* * *

She had waited twenty years for this, and dreaded it. She had put on a dead woman's dress for it.

"I can tell you how she died," Imogen said.

He did not move.

"The second light was Edmund's. Your father was right. I saw it too. I saw both of them, from here. I was sitting on that pot." She pointed. Her hand was quite steady; she noticed it with surprise. "I watched my mother's lantern go down to the Wade, and I watched my brother's go after it. I saw them meet and stand. And then I fell asleep. I was twenty-four and I had cried until I was sick. When I woke it was light, and he was walking up the lawn, wet to the neck."

"You saw him come back."

"At six in the morning. He went past that door. He told them at breakfast that he'd slept in the stable, and I sat at the table and let him say it." She made herself go on. "I've let him say it for twenty years. I've let my father believe that his clock killed her. I've never told a living soul what I saw."

"Why?"

It was the question. She had known that he would ask it, in just that way.

"Because of why he went," she said.

She told him. She did not soften it. She told him about the jewel case on the bed, and what she had said to her mother. She told him about the plan, the four of them in his father's kitchen. Her voice shook once, there, and she steadied it. She told him about the tea table, and the quarrel over a horse.

"It was Mab's dam. Edmund said your father had no business breeding a mare of that quality on our grazing, and that the foal by rights belonged to the estate. I was so angry. I was so angry on your account. And I had it in my mouth, the one thing that would hurt him more than anything on earth, and I said it. *Mother's going to Amos Garrow tonight. She's leaving. Ask her yourself.*" She shut her eyes. "He adored her. She was the only person who ever looked at him. I knew that. I used it."

"Imogen."

"Let me finish. I have to say all of it." She opened her eyes. "I was to walk with her. I'd promised. I came out here at eleven, to wait, and I sat in the dark and thought about what I'd done. And at one she came through that door with her lantern, in her stockings. She called me. She said, 'Immy? Are you there? It's time.' I was six feet from her. I was behind the vine."

She could not look at him now. She looked at the candle.

"I didn't answer. I held my breath. I couldn't face her. I couldn't face you. She waited. She called again. Then she said, 'Never mind, my darling. Come over in the morning.' And she went by herself. If I'd gone with her, Tom, there'd have been two of us on the Wade when Edmund came. I could have held him. I could have stood in front of him while she went on. She was a hundred and fifty yards from your father's hand."

The tears had come. She let them.

"That's why I wouldn't see you. It wasn't your father. It wasn't that I couldn't bear the sight of a Garrow. You stood in that yard in the rain for two hours, and I stood behind a curtain and watched you. I'd my coat on. I thought, if I go down, I shall have to tell him that I told. And that I hid, and let her walk to her death alone, because I was ashamed. I'd see his face change. I could not have borne that."

She stopped. She had said it. It had taken perhaps three minutes, and it was out.

The orangery was very quiet. She heard the mare breathe. She heard the wind come in at a broken pane and move the leaves along the floor.

She waited for him to speak. She thought that she had known every kind of fear there was. She had not known this one.

He put down the handful of leaves. He did it carefully, on the edge of the trough, beside the gun. He wiped his palms on his wet coat.

"You were twenty-four," he said.

"So were—"

"I was twenty-three. I know what I was. I'd not have known what to do with a thing like that either." He came a step toward her. "Twenty year. Twenty year I've thought it was him. Dad. I thought you'd looked across at that farm and seen the man your mother died going to, and seen his son. I thought every time you looked at me you'd see her on the mud. I never blamed you. I'd have felt the same."

"It was never that."

"I see it wasn't." He was quite close now. She could smell the wet wool and the horse, and the salt. "I went to the other end of the world on account of it. I got on a boat because I thought my face was more than you could stand. And all the while you were up at that window thinking yours was more than I could."

"I told Edmund," she said. "Don't you understand? It was my doing. If I'd kept my word—"

"You said a thing at a tea table in a temper. You were a girl. You never took hold of her on the Wade. You never kept her standing there an hour with the tide making." His voice was low and rough. "If I'm to hate somebody for a hard word said in a temper, I'd best begin with myself. I said one or two to my father, the day I left, that I'd give this arm to have back."

"You don't hate me."

"Hate you." He said it as though she had spoken a language he did not know. "Imogen. I've carried a bit of your writing inside my shirt for twenty year. I had it on me when I wed. I told Jeanie what it was; I'd not deceive her. She said, keep it. She said she'd sooner have a man that could love like that than one that couldn't, even if it wasn't her."

He put his hand inside his coat. He brought out something flat, wrapped in a square of oilcloth. He unfolded it on his palm, under the candle.

There was a stiff blue paper, folded small, with a solicitor's name printed at the head. And under that there was a half sheet of cream writing paper, gone soft and brown at the creases, and worn through in one place. She saw her own hand on it, the hand she had written at twenty-four, round and confident.

*O, then, I see Queen Mab hath been with you.*

"It was in the same pocket as the notice," he said. "I never thought. I put the one in on top of the other. I've been carrying your brother and you against my chest all day, and didn't know which was the heavier."

She could not speak.

"I said I came for Vane." He folded the oilcloth over. He put it back. "That's true. I got on the mare for that. I told myself so the whole road down to the water. And then she lost her feet in the Rill. We went under. I'd the reins in one hand and her mane in the other and the sea in my mouth, and I thought, that's it, that's the finish. And I'll tell you what I thought of. I didn't think of your brother. I didn't think of Dad."

He stopped. He had always been a man who could not say things.

"I thought, I'll not see her. I've been home a fortnight and I've stood and looked at that house every day like a dog at a gate, and I'll go down in this water and never have seen her. That's what I thought." He looked at her with those steady eyes. "So I'll say it straight, since we're saying things. I came for you. I've been coming for you for twenty year. I only never knew it till I was drowning."

She went to him. She did not know that she had moved. She was against the wet canvas of his coat, with her face in his neck, and his arms had come round her as slowly and completely as they had on the tenth of July under the vine. He was shaking. So was she. The sea water came through her mother's silk and she did not feel it.

He kissed her. It was not as it had been at twenty-three. He kissed her like a man who has been a long time in an empty country and has come to a house with a light in it.

Behind them the old mare turned her head, and looked, and blew softly through her nose.

* * *

It was some time before either of them spoke.

They were sitting on the two pots. He had put his coat round her shoulders, wet as it was, and the saddle blanket over that. He had her hands between his. He was looking at them.

"You've not got it on," he said.

"The ring? It's in Edinburgh. In my workbox. I've kept it oiled."

"Have you?" He turned her left hand over. "And nobody's ever put another there."

"Somebody tried. I gave it back."

"Why?"

"It was gold," she said. "It wasn't iron."

He laughed. It was the first time. It took years off him.

"Will you come away?" he said. "Now. Tonight."

"You can't cross. Not even you. Not till—"

"Twenty to four. By my watch. She can do it then; there'll be three foot on the Rill and falling, and I'll lead her through. I'll have you at Saltcote by four." He held up his wrist. He had a big steel watch on a leather strap. "It's twenty past one now."

"It's twenty past twelve. I heard it strike."

"That's your father's time. That's Compline. It's twenty past one in England." He almost smiled. "I'd forgot. Dad always said the Vanes even kept a different hour from other folk."

She looked at the watch. She had not worn one since she was a girl. She thought of a hundred and eleven clocks, and an old man in a cellar who had given his wife the wrong time.

"I can't come yet," she said.

His hands tightened.

"Not because I don't mean to. I mean to. I'll come across that water behind you if I have to swim it." She held his eyes. "But I have to go into the house first. At one o'clock by the hall, my father opens the door. It's the Hour. My mother made it; it's when we're supposed to say what's true. Twenty years ago I was sitting here on this pot when I should have been in that hall. I'm going to stand in it tonight. I'm going to tell my father what I saw, and what I did. Before I leave his house."

"In that?" He touched the grey silk at her shoulder.

"In this."

"He'll think he's seeing her."

"Perhaps. I don't know. I only know I can't go to you from behind a curtain a second time." She freed one hand and laid it against his face. The stubble was rough and wet. "An hour, Tom. Give me till two by the house. Then I'll come back here, and you'll put me up on that mare. I won't look round."

He was silent. She saw him set it against the thing that he had come for.

"And your brother?"

"I don't know where he is. Nobody ever does, on this night. He goes to ground."

"I'll find him. I've a notion where he's bound for. There was a man in the Plough at dinnertime, an oysterman, talking louder than he should about a job of work at the Hythe tonight, at the top of the tide." He turned his head and looked at the gun on the trough. "I've an hour of my own to fill, it seems."

"Tom. Leave it here."

He said nothing.

"Please. Ask him. Make him tell you; you've the right, and so had your father. I'd have told you myself, only I don't know what happened out there, I only know what I saw. But leave that here. He's my brother. And whatever he did on the Wade, he did because I sent him."

"I said I'd not hurt him if he told me."

"And if he doesn't tell you? If he laughs? He does laugh. It's what he does when he's frightened." She took hold of his sleeve. "I know you. You've never raised your hand to a living thing. You'd not know what you were until you'd done it, and then you'd have to carry it. You've a child."

His face changed at that.

"Dolly told you."

"She said a little girl. Nine. Dark."

"Wren." He said the name as he had said the mare's, low. "Lorraine Wren, after her mother's mother. She's nine and three-quarters; she'll tell you so. She's at Dolly's cottage now, asleep, with old Vi. I went to the door at dusk and Vi wouldn't have her woke. She said she'd gone up at seven with her notebook."

"What is she like?"

"She watches." He looked at Imogen. "She don't say a lot. She sits and she looks at a thing till she's got it. She's like you, that way. I've thought it before. I used to see her looking at a horse, with her head on one side, and think, now where've I seen that." His voice dropped. "She's all I've got that's any good. Her mother died two year back, in the winter. It took eight months. Wren sat with her. I'd find her on the bed with a book, reading out loud, and Jeanie asleep."

"Then for her," Imogen said. "Leave the gun."

He looked at it for a long moment. The candle was burning down. Across the yard, thin and clear, the small bell on the tower rang three times. A quarter to one. She had fifteen minutes.

He did not say that he would. She noticed that. He was a man who never said what he was not sure of. He stood up, and drew her up with him, and put his mouth for a moment against her hair, on the white place.

"Go and say your piece," he said. "I'll be here at two by your father's clocks. Or she will." He nodded at the mare. "If I'm not, you get up on her and give her her head. She'll take you home."

"You'll be here."

"I've come three thousand mile and through seven foot of water," Tom Garrow said. "I don't reckon to miss you by an hour."

She gave him back his coat. She went to the garden door in her mother's dress, with the wet marks of his arms on the silk. At the door she turned.

He had gone back to the mare. He was standing with one hand on her neck and his head bent, the way he had stood in a yard in the rain. He was not looking at Imogen. He was looking at the long shape on the edge of the stone trough.

She had asked him. She could do no more. She went in.

# A Note on the Time

There were fifteen people on Compline Ness on the night of Saturday the twenty-second of October, 1994, and by the following Monday there were fourteen. Seven of those who were there have left an account of it. This book is those seven accounts, in the order in which I think they should be read, which is not the order in which they were written and not the order in which I found them.

I should say at the beginning what you are going to notice in any case. The accounts do not agree.

They do not agree about small things, such as what was served after the goose, and they do not agree about large ones, such as whether a man was dead. One witness finds a body at a quarter past one. Another wheels the same man across a stable yard twenty minutes later and complains of the weight and the snoring. A third watches him fall forty feet from a clock tower on the stroke of one. A fourth speaks to him in a scullery passage at twenty past two and is spoken to in return. A fifth shot him. A sixth killed him before midnight with a piece of lead, and then, by his own careful account, went back an hour and did not.

I have had these documents for some time now. I have had them on a table in the room where I am writing this, in six piles, and I have done to them the things that a person of my training does. I have been through them with a pencil and a ruler. I have drawn up the hours of that night on a long sheet of lining paper, across and down, and entered every statement of fact in its square. I have checked the weather against the Meteorological Office's daily report and the tides against the Admiralty tables for the port of reference, corrected for the place. I have checked the moon. I have stood in the yard with a stopwatch. Where a thing could be measured I have measured it.

I want to tell you the result before you begin, because if you do not know it you will read these pages the way a jury reads, looking for the liar, and you will find one. You will find six, one after another, and you will be wrong each time.

None of them lied. I have checked.

I do not mean that they were fair, or wise, or that they understood what they were looking at. Most of them did not. I mean something narrower and harder than that. I mean that wherever one of these people writes *I saw*, that person saw it; and wherever one of them writes *it was such-and-such o'clock*, a clock said so. Every account in this book is true. Every account in this book contradicts the others. I am aware that those two sentences cannot both stand. They stand. It took me thirty years to see how, and I had an advantage that you will not have until the end.

* * *

Some things it will help you to know.

Compline House stands on an island that is an island for roughly half of every day. The road to it is called the Wade. It is nine hundred yards long, and the sea covers it twice in twenty-four hours, to a depth that depends on the moon and the wind and that on the night in question was a little over six feet. When the Wade is covered there is no way on or off the Ness for a person on foot, and there has never been a telephone line that survived a gale. So the people in these pages were, for the hours that matter, shut in together. If you are of a certain turn of mind you will already have the name for that kind of story. Hold it loosely. It is only one of seven.

It was the night the clocks went back. I do not know how much attention you ordinarily pay to that. Most people pay none; they wake on the Sunday, feel faintly that they have been given something, and reset the oven. But it means that in the small hours of that night there is an hour that happens twice. The clocks run from one to two, and are put back, and run from one to two again. Any event set down as taking place at half past one that night took place at one of two different moments, an hour apart, and unless the witness says which, you cannot tell. Very few witnesses say which. Very few know there is anything to say.

And at Compline, in particular, there was a difficulty about what time it was to begin with. I will leave the first witness to explain it to you, because it was explained to him, and he did not much like it either.

The family there had a name for the hour that comes twice. They called it the Odd Hour. They had a rule about it, which was older than the trouble and which was made, I think, by someone who loved them and was tired: that what was said in the Odd Hour did not count. It was an hour off the ledger. You could say in it what you could not say in the other eight thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine, and at two o'clock it would be put back with the clocks, and nobody would hold you to it.

I mention the rule because every person in this book was relying on it, and it was not true. It all counted. That is not the solution; it is only the thing I am surest of.

* * *

A word on what I have done to the text, which is as little as I could.

Each account is given whole. I have not cut them to make them agree or rearranged them to make them clear. Where an author changed the names, I have changed them back, and I say so. Where an account was spoken rather than written, I have set it down as nearly as I can in the way it was spoken, and I say that too. Before each one I have put a note of where it came from and what state its author was in, since I think you are entitled to know who is talking to you. I have tried to keep my own voice out of those notes. I have not always managed it.

You will find that each of these people tells the night as a different kind of story. I did not arrange that. One of them could only see the world as a place where everybody is on the take and the woman is lying; so that is the night he had, and it is a true night. One could not see a disaster except as an embarrassment; one could not enter a house without it having been waiting for her. One was in love. One had come to settle something. One was very old, and believed that he had built a machine. People do not choose the kind of story they are in nearly as often as the story chooses them, and it turns out to matter a great deal, when you are trying to find out what happened, to know which kind of story each witness thought it was. A man looking for a murder will find the body. A man looking for the pudding will walk straight past it.

There is a seventh account. It comes last, and it is the only one for which I will not offer you a note on the author.

For the six, here is my suggestion. Read each one as if it were the only one. Believe it; they have all earned that. Notice the times, and notice who is holding the clock. And when you find that you can no longer believe the one you are reading without calling the one before it a liar, do not choose. Put a finger on the place and go on.

They fit. I promise you that they fit.

We will begin with the man who found the body, because in his kind of story somebody always does.

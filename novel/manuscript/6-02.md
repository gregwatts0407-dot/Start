## II. Of the Engine

§ 14. Before I describe the apparatus I must give some account of the instrument upon which it was founded, and of how I came by it. The reader who has no interest in clocks may pass over this section. I should be sorry if he did.

In the spring of 1951 I attended a sale at a farmhouse some miles out of Norwich. The owner had died, an old bachelor, and the contents were being cleared. I had gone for a lantern clock that proved to be a marriage. In the yard, among the outside lots, there was a tea chest of scrap brass, fire irons and a broken fender, which was to be sold by weight.

I turned it over, as one does. At the bottom, under the fender, there was a plate.

It was about eleven inches by nine, of thick brass, black with tarnish, and pierced with a great number of holes. I knew what it was before I had it out of the box. It was the back plate of a movement. And along its lower edge, engraved in a round hand under the dirt, I could make out letters. I wet my thumb and rubbed. *Isaac Thorne. Colchester. Fecit 1771.*

I ought to explain that name. Thorne was a clockmaker of Colchester of whom almost nothing is known. He left perhaps a dozen ordinary long-case clocks. But there is an entry in the minutes of the Board of Longitude for 1773, which I had read as a young man and never forgotten. It records that a Mr Thorne of that town had submitted for the Board's inspection a table regulator of his own design, showing at one view the mean time, the sidereal time, the equation, the age and place of the moon, and the state of the tide at a named bar upon the Essex coast; and that the Astronomer Royal had examined it for a month and found no error. The Board gave him twenty pounds for his trouble. The clock was never heard of again. It was supposed to have been broken up.

I bought the tea chest for eleven shillings. The auctioneer asked whether I wanted the fender.

There was more of it in the house. I found the dial in an attic, serving as the lid of a meal bin. I found the moon work, complete, in a drawer of nails. Over the next four years I traced the frame and two of the trains to a marine store in Great Yarmouth and a dentist's widow in Diss. About a third of it was gone for good. That third I made.

It took me nine years. I do not say that with complaint. They were the best of my working life. To restore a movement by a man of that quality is to keep his company. One learns his hand. Thorne cut his teeth a shade deeper than the fashion of his day, and left his pivots long. He was left-handed; one sees it in the turning. And he had a habit, where he was pleased with a piece of work, of scratching upon it, in some place where it would never be seen, a small device like a sprig of blackthorn. I found it upon the back of the moon disc and under the left foot of the dial. I found it nowhere else. I took it that those were the two parts that he was proudest of.

I finished it in the autumn of 1960. I set it going on the table in the library window on the evening of the twenty-first of September, at the equinox, when the equation is small. It has not stopped since. It has not, in thirty-four years, been out by one minute from the sun, nor by five from the tide upon Compline Bar, which is the bar that Thorne made it for. I had not known that when I bought the plate. I found the name engraved upon the tidal dial when I cleaned it. He had made it for this water.

My wife said that it was the only clock in the house that told you something you needed to know.

§ 15. I made a copy. I began it in 1962 and was eight years about it.

I did so for the reason that I have always made copies, which is that I do not understand a thing until I have made it. I wished to cut every wheel that Thorne had cut, in the order that he cut them, and to find out at each step why he had done it so. I wished also to have something upon which to teach.

For I had by then a pupil. My son Edmund came into the Shop when he was nine years old. He stood beside the bench for an hour without speaking and then asked whether he might hold the file. I have had some gifted apprentices sent to me in my time, from the trade schools. I never had one to compare with that boy. He had it in his fingers. He could feel a thousandth. At fourteen he cut a scape wheel that I could not have bettered.

I did not tell him so. I record that against myself. It was not the custom of the trade, as I learned it, to praise; one looked at a piece of work, and if it would do, one said that it would do. I thought that he understood. I see now that I never asked.

He cut the lunar train of the Copy. It is the most delicate part. He was sixteen when he began the original with me, and thirty-one when he finished the same train for the Copy, by himself, in eleven weeks, and brought it up to show his mother before he showed it to me. It is a better piece of work than Thorne's. I say so now. I did not say so then. I looked at it through my glass and said that it would serve.

I have had that sentence in my ears a good deal lately.

He never came into the Shop again after his mother's death. He did not say why. He put down his tools on the Monday of that week, in their rack, in order, and I do not believe that he has touched a file since. I supposed that it was grief. I let him alone. One does not ask.

The Copy is exact. I will stake my reputation upon it. There is no measurement that would distinguish it from the original. It differs in two respects only. I did not scratch Thorne's device upon it, since it was not his. And I cut the cock that carries the moon work somewhat too light, in the winter of 1964, and have always meant to do it again. It will not bear being laid upon its side.

§ 16. I return to September last, and the long cupboard.

I kept my copies there, in the Shop, upon shelves, each under a cloth, with a ledger. There were forty-three. I opened the cupboard to look for a pattern, as I have said. I lifted a cloth and there was nothing under it. I lifted the next.

I counted eleven empty places.

I am not, I think, a suspicious man. My first thought was that I had moved them myself and forgotten. I do forget. I spent a day searching the house. On the second day I was winding the month-going clock in the hall, which is by Tompion, and I looked at the dial, as I had looked at it every month for forty years.

It was mine.

I knew it as a man knows his own handwriting. The silvering was mine. The engraving of the chapter ring was a little more upright than Tompion's; I have never been able to get his slope. I opened the door and looked inside for the signature upon the back plate, and it was clean brass.

Somebody had taken my copy out of the cupboard and stood it in the case. And had taken Tompion's away.

I went round the house. I shall not describe that afternoon. I found ten more.

§ 17. I considered the matter as a mechanic considers a fault. One asks what must be true for it to have occurred.

It must be someone with the freedom of the house. It must be someone who knew that the copies existed, and where, and which answered to which; that is, someone who had read my ledger. And it must be someone with the skill to take a movement out of its case and put another in, and set it to time and in beat, so that I should wind it for a year and observe nothing. That last is not a small matter. I do not think that there are thirty men in England who could do it to my satisfaction.

I had trained one of them. He lived in my house.

I did not wish it to be Edmund. I say that as a fact about the investigator, which the reader should allow for. I turned over every other possibility. I thought of my daughter, who knows a copy from an original at sight and might have been shown the rest. I thought of my son's wife, who has not been happy, and of my grandson, who has good hands and no money. I am ashamed to say that I thought of the cook.

And I found that I could not go on with it. Each time I had the reasoning complete, it leaked away. I would wake in the morning knowing that there was something that I had resolved to do, and not what. I wrote it down, and lost the paper. I began to be afraid that the thief was relying upon exactly this, and need only be patient.

So upon a day when I was clear I wrote to Fenn. I told him to find me a man who could look at people. I told him that I might not remember having written, and that he was to act regardless.

I am informed that he did so, and that a person came. I have no recollection of him whatever. Miss Quaile says that he was a heavy man with a tired face who dined with us and was struck upon the head. It seems a hard return for his trouble.

§ 18. But I did one thing more, and that I do remember, since it was a matter of the hands.

The eleven had been taken from the less regarded cases. Whoever it was had begun with the small things and was working upward. It was plain to me where that must end. There is one clock in this house which is worth more than all the rest together. I had only to ask how long it would be before he came for the Thorne.

I resolved that when he did, he should not get it.

I went into the library on a night in the middle of September, when the house was asleep. I took the Thorne out of its glass. I carried it down to the Shop. It weighs thirty-one pounds and I was two hours upon the stairs. I took the Copy from its place in the long cupboard and carried that up, and stood it in the library window under the glass, and set it to time, and covered it with the baize as I do each night.

I was aware of what I was doing. I was laying a bait. And it was not only that I wished to save the original. I shall be honest. I had a second purpose, and it was not a kind one.

I wished to see whether he would know.

If it was Edmund, he had cut the lunar train of that Copy with his own hands. He had helped me to restore the original when he was a boy. There was no one alive but myself who was better placed to tell the one from the other. He had only to turn it over. He had only to look beneath the left foot of the dial for a sprig of thorn. If he looked, he would see that I had found him out, and would know that I had stood my own work in the place of honour and was waiting.

And if he did not look? If he carried off my Copy for Thorne's clock, in the dark, in a hurry, without once putting a glass to it?

Then I should know something else. I should know that the best hands I ever trained had forgotten what they were for.

I am not proud of that test. A father ought not to set such a thing for his son. But I had told him once that his work would serve. I think I wished to learn whether he had believed me.

§ 19. I now had the true Thorne in my Shop, and nowhere to keep it. It could not stand upon the bench; my son's wife comes down, and the girl who dusts.

I thought of the tower.

No one goes up the tower but I. It is a hard climb. There is in the clock chamber, in the corner, a tall cupboard of deal, built against the wall, where my grandfather kept his oil and his spare lines. It has a lock. I carried the Thorne across the yard that same night, wrapped in a blanket, and up the ladder. I do not know how. I rested upon every rung. I stood it upon the shelf and locked the door.

And there, standing in that chamber at four in the morning, with my heart going, I looked from the cupboard to the frame of the turret clock, a yard away, and I saw the engine entire.

I had been drawing it for a week and could not get the ratio. I saw it then as one sees the answer to a sum upon waking.

§ 20. I shall describe it in terms that a layman may follow. The trade will want more, and I have left the drawings in the top drawer of the bench.

The difficulty was this. I required a movement that should, at one instant, take account of two times together: the false time, which the world keeps, and the true, which this house keeps; and should at the moment of their coming together gather up the difference.

Now I had in that chamber, by the accident of that night, the two best timekeepers that I own. The one is the turret clock. It was made in 1790 by my great-great-grandfather and it has a pendulum of fourteen feet and a bob of two hundredweight. It is not fine. But it is the house's own; it has told Compline time for two hundred years, and all the others are set by it. It is the true time made in iron.

The other is the Thorne. And the Thorne, the reader will recollect, shows upon one of its dials the equation of time, which is to say the difference between two noons. It has within it a differential: a pair of wheels so mounted that the motion of a third is the difference of their motions. Thorne put it there to subtract the mean sun from the true. It is the most beautiful piece of gearing that I have ever handled. It is, in plain terms, a machine for reckoning how far two times disagree.

I had only to couple them.

I cut two rods of brass, a yard long and a quarter-inch in section. I bored the side of the cupboard. I carried the motion of the turret clock, from the arbor of its hour wheel, by the one rod, to the Thorne. I returned the motion of the Thorne's equation work, by the other, to a cage of four wheels of my own design which I clamped upon the frame. The cage is the heart of it. I shall call it the take-up. It runs free, doing nothing, so long as the two inputs keep their proper relation. But let the one fall back upon the other by a known quantity at a stroke, and the take-up is wound. It stores the difference. It is in effect a fusee. And what is wound into a fusee may be let out again.

I called it the Reconciler. The name was Miss Quaile's, though she does not recollect having given it. She said, upon the first night that I showed it to her, that it looked like a thing for making two people agree.

§ 21. I worked upon it through the last of September and the first three weeks of October. I worked at night, since by day I was watched. Miss Quaile came to me upon the first of the month. She found out within a week where I went, and thereafter she came with me and held the lamp.

I must record my debt to her. She told me plainly that she did not believe the thing could be done. She told me also, each time that I asked it, that my wife was dead. I had been told for twenty years that Margot had gone up to change. I cannot convey what a relief it was to have by me one person who would not manage me. I found that I could think. I had not been able to think for some years, among so much kindness.

She held the lamp for eleven nights while I cut the last pinion of the take-up, which has seven leaves and is three thirty-seconds of an inch across. I am eighty-one. I did not break one.

§ 22. There remained the setting.

A fusee lets out what it has taken up. But it lets it out along the line on which it was wound. It was not enough that I should be returned one hour; I have explained that. I wanted a particular one.

Here I shall be candid. I am upon ground where I can no longer show the working.

The Thorne carries a calendar. It knows the day of the year, since without it the equation cam would have no meaning. I had only to consider what the equation was upon the night of the twenty-sixth of October in the year 1974, and what the age of the moon, and the state of the tide upon the bar. Those three together are a kind of address. There is no other night in a century upon which the sun, the moon and the water stood in just that relation. I have the figures. I had them by heart before ever I thought of the engine.

Upon the afternoon of the twenty-second of October last I went up the tower at noon, when they supposed me at the bench. I disengaged the Thorne from its own going train. I turned the moon by hand to her age upon that night: she was eleven days old. I set the equation cam to sixteen minutes and three seconds, sun before clock. I set the tidal dial to low water at twenty-eight minutes past midnight.

I set her, in short, to the hour that I wanted. And I engaged the rods.

I looked at her for some time. She was telling a night twenty years gone, to the minute, and beside her through the wall the old turret clock was telling this one. Between them the take-up stood ready, slack, with its click resting upon the first tooth.

I thought: at one o'clock tonight the world falls back upon us. An hour is paid. It will go by my door, as it has gone by for twenty years. But this time there is something set to catch it. And when it is caught, it will be let out along the line that I have laid. I shall stand at my door, and it will be the same door, and the same hour. And she will be coming down the stairs.

I ask the reader to believe that I knew how this sounded. I was not at that moment leaking. I had my faculties. I said to myself, Leopold, you have spent sixty-six years refusing to believe anything that you could not measure, and you are now proposing to travel through time by means of two brass rods and a tide dial.

I answered myself as follows. I said: I have measured it. I have stood at my door upon twenty nights and felt it pass. I do not know what an hour is. Nor does the Astronomer Royal. But I know that it is given back, at a stated instant, to every clock that lent it; and I know that I have here the only house in England that stands outside the account, and the only instrument ever made that can reckon the difference between two times. If it cannot be done here, it cannot be done.

And I said one thing more. I said: if I am wrong, I shall have lost nothing but a night's sleep. And if I am right, I shall have given my wife the tide.

I locked the cupboard. I went down and let them dress me.

§ 23. I must note here a second leakage, which took place upon the ladder as I descended. I note it because of a thing that I learned in it.

I had my foot upon the fourth rung. Without interval I was standing in a yard, in sunlight, among a crowd of farmers, with a brass plate in my hand, wet where I had rubbed it with my thumb. I was thirty-eight. The auctioneer was saying that he was bid eleven shillings.

It was the Norwich sale. I had not thought of it for years. I looked down at the plate. I read the name again. And I turned it over, there in the sunshine, as I had turned it then, to look at the back.

I had forgotten what was on the back. It is covered now by the frame, and I have not had the movement down since 1960.

There is a line scratched there, very small, in Thorne's left-handed script. I read it in 1951 and thought it a workman's memorandum. It says: *Made for M. that she may know when to cross.*

I stood in the yard with the plate in my hand and the sun upon it. I understood it. I had not understood it at thirty-eight. He had made the clock for a woman. He had put the tide upon it for her sake, at that bar, so that someone whose name began with that letter should be able to look at a dial and know whether the road was open.

I do not know who she was. I do not know whether she crossed.

Then I was upon the ladder again, holding the rung, and Miss Quaile was calling from below to ask whether I was ill.

I said that I was perfectly well. I came down. I did not tell her what I had seen. But I record it here, since I think that it has a place in the evidence. That instrument was made by a man who wished to give a woman the hour of the tide. It lay in pieces for a hundred and eighty years. It was put together again, in this house, by another such man, who had failed to. And upon the night that I asked it to do the same thing over, I do not think that it was being asked to do anything outside its nature.

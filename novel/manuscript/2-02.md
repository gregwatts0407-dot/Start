## 2. In Which We Are Thirteen

The basin was called the Font. It was explained to me by the fair woman in the green dress, who was my cousin Edmund's wife, Celia, and who had the kind of voice one associates with the better sort of railway announcement. Guests at Compline gave up their watches at the door. It was my great-uncle's rule. One had them back with the kedgeree.

I said, "Oh, rather," and began to roll up my cuff, and then I stopped.

I have promised to explain the watch. In the spring of that year I had been attached, briefly, to a girl called Fenella who worked in advertising. Fenella held that I took myself too seriously. I have never been able to see this; I should have said the difficulty was all the other way. But she held it, and for my birthday she gave me a wristwatch with Mickey Mouse on the dial. His arms were the hands. He wore yellow gloves, and at twenty to four he looked as if he were directing aircraft. Fenella said it was ironic. She left me in June for a man who made documentaries, and I went on wearing the watch. It kept excellent time, and I could not at that stage afford another.

There is nothing wrong with a Mickey Mouse watch in Fulham. I wish to make that clear. Things are different when one is about to lay it in a pewter basin of the sixteenth century, on top of a thing by Patek Philippe, with a family looking on.

"I say, must I really? Only it's a bit—"

"Everybody must," said Celia Vane. "Even the vicar."

I unstrapped it. I held it over the Font, and it was at that moment that my cousin Kit came down the stairs, in a dinner jacket, with the girl Saskia. He looked into my hand.

"Good God," he said.

"It was a present. From a girl. It's ironic."

"What's ironic about it?"

"Well. I mean. One wears it ironically."

"Does Mickey know?" said Kit.

I let it go. It lay there among the gold ones with one glove pointing at six and the other at twelve, in the attitude of a man surrendering. I found that I rather missed it.

* * *

I now met my family. I shall introduce them in the order in which they frightened me.

My cousin Edmund frightened me first. He was fifty-two, about my height and twice my width, and good-looking in the way that cabinet ministers used to be. He had a tumbler in his hand which he seemed to have been issued with at birth. He looked at me for some time and said, "Dorothea's lot. You'll have come for money," and when I began to explain about the Thracian valley he said, "Don't. I can't bear it. I'll tell you what, I'll toss you for who asks him first," and laughed, and gave me a whisky the size of a vase. I never knew, with Edmund, whether I was being teased or warned. I can say that he gave me the drink, and that in my experience the men who really mean you harm don't.

My cousin Imogen frightened me second. She was quiet, in black, with a white streak in her dark hair like a badger's, and she had a way of looking at one as if deciding which century one had been painted in. She restored pictures. She asked me what I did, and I said wine, and she said, "And is it going well?" in a perfectly kind voice. I heard myself say, "Not awfully," which I had not said to anybody, including my mother.

There was a solicitor called Fenn. He looked like a solicitor. There was a vicar called Stiles, a dear old boy with cotton wool in his ears, real cotton wool, who shook my hand and said, "And how is your father keeping?" My father had been dead eleven years. I said, "Much the same," and he was delighted.

And there was the man Cade.

I have mentioned him. He was presented as a Mr Cade, from the insurance company, who had come to count the clocks. Well. I am not a noticing person. But I had been turned down by enough insurance companies to know one of their men when I saw him, and I knew I was not seeing one now. He was fifty or so, thick in the shoulder, in a blue suit that had been good in another decade. He had the kind of face that is called lived-in, but it looked more as if someone had lived in it and gone, leaving a few things behind. He stood against the wall with a glass. He did not talk to anybody. He just looked, at each of us in turn, slowly and without any expression. When it came round to me I had a strong impulse to tell him where I had been on the night of the fourteenth.

"Who *is* that?" I asked Dame Octavia.

"The insurance."

"He doesn't look like insurance."

"No," she said. "He looks like a bailiff. I asked him if he hunted and he said, only people. I thought that was rather good." She took his whisky away from him a moment later, by mistake, and he let her. I thought that was rather good too.

Then there was an incident on the stairs which I shall touch on only lightly, because a gentleman would not touch on it at all. Celia Vane had gone up to finish dressing, or to see about something. She came down again. And she and the girl Saskia were found to be wearing the same frock.

I mean the very same. Green, silk, expensive, with no sleeves. On my cousin's wife it looked like something from the cover of a magazine. Saskia wore it with army boots and a ring through her eyebrow, and on her it looked like a costume for an opera about the Amazons. They stood and regarded one another. The hall went absolutely silent. I have known that silence in the mess of a good regiment when somebody has passed the port the wrong way.

"Oh, bloody hell," said Saskia. "The Liberty sale?"

"The Liberty sale," said Celia.

I thought there would be blood. But my cousin's wife was a remarkable woman. She came down the last few stairs, smiling, and said they would call it a uniform, and that Edmund had always said one of her wasn't enough.

"I've never said that in my life," said Edmund.

Everyone laughed. I laughed. I record, though, that for the rest of the night I was never perfectly sure, at a distance or from behind, which of them I was looking at. This led to one or two awkwardnesses. At about half past eleven I followed a green dress along a dark passage for some way, speaking to it as "Mrs Vane," and it turned out to be the other one. She said, "Do I look forty?" and I had to pretend to have been choking.

* * *

At seven minutes past six by every clock in the hall, my great-uncle came in.

I had expected, I think, something bent and mumbling in a bath chair. My mother had said "nearly dead." He was eighty-one and he came across that floor like a drawn sword. He was very tall, taller than me, with white hair that stood straight up, and he had a glass screwed into one eye on a black cord, which he let drop as he approached, and caught, and put back, without seeming to know he did it. He stopped at the Font and stirred the watches with one finger. Mine came to the top. He picked it out and held it up to the candles.

There was a pause in which I aged.

"This is an honest watch," said my Uncle Leo.

Nobody said anything.

"It's the only honest watch in this basin. Look at it. A plain lever movement, pin-pallet, such as you might have bought for a shilling in 1910. No jewels to speak of. And the maker has put a mouse on the dial, so that no one shall take it for more than it is. I respect that. I wish Monsieur Patek had had the same candour." He laid it down again, carefully, on top of the others. "Whose is it?"

I put up my hand. I couldn't speak.

"You are Dorothea's grandson. You have her wrists. She could never keep a bracelet on." He looked me over through the glass, and it was the second time in an hour that I had been examined by an expert. "You've come to ask me for something. No, don't tell me now. I shan't remember. Tell me at one."

I looked at Dame Octavia. She closed one eye.

And then I saw who had come in with him. She was half a step behind, with her hand just under his elbow, so lightly that you would not have noticed unless you were looking. I was looking. It was the girl from the stairs. She was in the same blue dress, and her hair was the same, and there was no tray. She glanced round the hall once. She found me, and I thought she checked my shoulder, to see whether the crab was still there.

"Who's that?" I said to Dame Octavia. I said it as casually as I could.

"The nurse. Miss Something. Quaile. She sits up with him. He wanders at night, poor old boy; they found him on the sea wall in September in his pyjamas, winding his watch." She gave me a sideways look out of her horse's eye. "You've gone pink."

"It's the fire."

"It's a very plain gel."

"Is she?" I said. "I hadn't noticed."

"Ha!" said Dame Octavia Prowse.

My uncle had turned from the Font. He looked about him, at all of us, and a little frown came between his eyebrows, as if he had mislaid someone.

"Is Margot down?" he said.

Now I didn't know. Nobody had told me. I thought Margot must be another cousin, and I was about to say brightly that I didn't think I'd met her yet. Then I noticed that no one in the hall was moving at all. Edmund was looking into his tumbler, and Celia was turning a ring round on her finger. It was my cousin Imogen who answered, from over by the fire, quite gently. She said, "She'll be along, Papa."

"Who's Margot?" I whispered.

"His wife," said Dame Octavia, without moving her lips. "Marguerite. She's been dead twenty years. Drowned on that road you came in by, on this night. Don't sit in her chair."

"Her chair?"

"You'll see."

* * *

I saw. We went in to dinner at eight, and I counted the places, as one does, to see who one had got.

I had my cousin Imogen on my left and my cousin's wife on my right, she being at the foot of the table. Opposite me were Kit, the girl Saskia, and the man Cade. Beyond Imogen, going up my side, were Edmund, and then, three places from me, close enough to see and too far to pass her the salt, the nurse. And beyond her again, on my uncle's right hand, in the place of honour, there was nobody.

It was fully laid. There were three glasses and a napkin done up like a mitre. The chair was pulled out a few inches, as for someone who has just risen to powder her nose. And when the soup came, the big girl who was waiting, Gail, a jolly red-faced child who breathed through her mouth, put a plate of it down at that place as a matter of course and went on round. Presently she came and took it away again, full. I watched her do it. She did not so much as glance at anyone.

I am not superstitious. I walk under ladders, though I do not, as has been seen, walk up them. But I counted the table over, including that chair, and I got thirteen.

"We're thirteen," I said, to my cousin's wife. I thought she might not have noticed.

"We always are," said Celia. "Have some bread."

I should say something about the dinner, since Mrs Pask, who cooked it, deserves to be remembered for more than what happened later with the pudding. It was a goose. It was brought in by Gail on a dish the size of a coffee table, and my great-uncle rose and carved it himself. I have seen sommeliers at the Savoy open a bottle. I have seen a man in Jerez pour sherry into a glass from behind his own head. I have never seen anyone do a thing with his hands so well as that old man carved a goose. He never looked up, and every slice fell the same thickness as the last. When he had finished there was nothing left on the carcass that a cat could have got at.

"He could do that blindfold," said Imogen quietly, beside me. "He used to, for us, at Christmas. With a table napkin tied round his head. Edmund could too."

"Could he?" I looked up the table at my large cousin, who was refilling his own glass.

"Edmund had the best hands of any of us. Kit has them from him. You should have seen him at fourteen, with a graver." She said it, I thought, as one speaks of somebody who has moved abroad.

There was a hitch over Saskia, who announced that she didn't eat anything with a face. Dame Octavia said that she had been hunting for sixty years and had never yet met a goose with a face worth mentioning, and my Uncle Leo, quite unexpectedly, laid down the carving knife and said that he thought it a perfectly reasonable principle, and that the child was to have whatever she liked. What the child got, in the end, was the vegetables. They had been roasted in the goose's fat. She ate them with every sign of pleasure and I saw no reason to bring it up.

The claret came round. I may not know clocks, but I knew what that was. It was a Léoville-Las-Cases of the 1961 vintage, and I had only ever met it before in catalogues. I said so, to Edmund, across my cousin Imogen. I may have said it with tears in my eyes.

"Have you?" said Edmund. "Good. Then somebody at this table can tell the real thing when he sees it." He raised his glass to me and drank it off as though it were barley water. It was a dreadful thing to watch.

I told a story, I recall, about the Midget and a car ferry at Dartmouth, which went quite well, though it has a longish middle. The vicar told me again that he was glad my father was keeping better. Opposite me the man Cade ate steadily and looked at people. And up the table the nurse cut her goose into small pieces and did not eat them. She kept one eye on my great-uncle and, I could not help observing, a part of the other on my cousin Edmund, on her left, who never once spoke to her. His sleeve touched her wrist when he reached for the salt. I saw her colour.

I wasn't jealous. One can't be, in the first three hours. But I filled my own glass at that point, and didn't taste it.

* * *

And all the time there was my pocket.

I had forgotten to allow for sitting down. When a man sits, in a dinner jacket, the pocket comes forward onto the thigh, and its contents settle. Mine settled with a noise like a bag of nails. Imogen looked round. I coughed. A little later I leaned forward for the bread sauce and something sharp, a wheel I suppose, came through the lining and got me in the leg, and I said "Ah!" in the middle of one of the vicar's remarks. He took it for agreement.

It was the spring that worried me most. I could feel it against my hip, moving. From time to time it gave a faint, melancholy *twang*, like a harp in a distant room. I kept my right hand pressed flat over the pocket to hold it down, and ate my goose with a fork in my left, like an American.

"Have you hurt yourself?" Celia asked.

"No, no. An old wound."

"In the hip?"

"Rugby," I said. I have never played rugby in my life.

I had by now arrived at a plan. It was not a good plan, but it was more of one than I usually have. I should wait until one o'clock, as advised. Then I should make a clean breast of it to the old gentleman, produce the remains, offer to pay for the damage out of my future profits, and move straight on to the subject of the Thracian valley while his mood held. In the meantime nobody need know. Nobody did know. I had only to get through dinner without clanking.

It was at this point that Dame Octavia pushed back her chair and announced that she was going out for a gasper, and my uncle told her to sit down.

He got to his feet. He stood at the head of the table with the candles under his chin and picked up his glass. He turned to the empty chair beside him and bowed to it, very slightly, and said something that I didn't catch. Then he looked at the rest of us.

"To absent time," he said.

We stood and drank it. I've no idea what it meant. I have asked several of those who were there, and they've none either. I said it with great feeling, because of the claret.

We sat. He didn't. He said that it was the twentieth year. He said that he had known that afternoon what he wished to say about it and had now forgotten, and he said it without any fuss, like a man reporting the loss of an umbrella. I thought that rather fine. And then he said that he would tell us something else instead, and that it was about clocks.

I pressed my hand on my pocket. The spring went *twang*.

"One of you," said my Uncle Leo, "has been robbing me."

* * *

Now I have often read, in the sort of book my Aunt Hilary likes, of a man's blood running cold. I had supposed it a figure of speech. It is not. It starts at the back of the neck and it goes down inside the collar.

He said it had been going on for some time. He said clocks, good ones, had been taken out of their places. And he said that it was thought he would not notice, because he was old, and couldn't find his spectacles. I will never forget his next words. He said he could go round that house blindfold and put his hand on any clock in it and say who made it and when.

He knew. That was all I could think. He knew to the inch what had stood on the table in the Blue Room, and it had been a good one. And he had been up there. Or somebody had. They had found the bare table, and a few splinters of glass, perhaps, on the floor under the bed. The alarm had gone round the house while I was in the bath.

I didn't follow the details of what he said. There was something about copies. I took that to be a technical term. I suppose I heard about one word in three. One is not at one's best as a listener when one is sitting on the evidence.

"I don't know which of you it is," he said.

I breathed.

"I've a man finding out for me. A very capable man. He will have told me before the door is opened. I shall know which of you it is by one o'clock."

I looked across the table. I couldn't help it. And the man Cade, with a spoonful of syllabub halfway to his mouth and no expression on his face of any kind, was looking straight at my right-hand pocket.

I knew it then. He wasn't from the insurance. He'd been engaged. He was the very capable man, and he'd been through my room. He must have stood in the doorway of the Blue Room with those tired eyes and reconstructed the entire thing from a dent in the carpet. He probably knew which knee.

My Uncle Leo was still speaking. He was saying something very decent, I gathered afterwards, about how the guilty party might come and tell him why, in the Hour, and nothing more would be said. I have had it repeated to me. At the time I heard a roaring noise, and through it my own voice, in my head, rehearsing. *It was an accident, sir. I was taking off a pullover.*

I will confess that I started to get up.

I had my left hand on the table and my weight coming forward. I had got as far as "I say—" in a sort of croak. My idea was to get it over with there and then, in front of them all, and take what was coming. It was the only time in my life I have ever been about to do the brave thing at the right time.

A hand came down on my knee, under the cloth, and gripped it.

It was my cousin's wife. Celia. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking up the table, at her husband, with a face like a closed door. But her hand had me by the kneecap, with surprising strength, and she said, very low, hardly moving her mouth:

"Whatever it is, Toby. Not now."

I sat down. The spring twanged.

My uncle sat down too, rather abruptly, and the nurse had him by the arm. He looked at his plate and asked what was on it, and whether he had ordered it. Then he turned to the empty chair and asked Margot whether they had ordered a syllabub. The dinner went on. I've been at some sticky ones, but that was the stickiest quarter of an hour I have known.

I did not find out for years what that hand on my knee had been about. I supposed at the time that she had guessed, and was being kind. I now think my cousin's wife had no more idea than the vicar what I had in my pocket. She had seen a foolish young man about to stand up and make a scene, at a moment when she had every reason to want the table quiet. She had troubles of her own that night that made mine look like a parking ticket. But it was a kindness all the same, and I haven't forgotten it. I think of her whenever a spring goes in a sofa.

* * *

The ladies withdrew. I had not known that ladies still did. Saskia said, "Are we being *sent out?*" and was led away by Dame Octavia, who told her it was the only part of the evening when one could get a decent drink and take one's shoes off.

The port came. It came to the vicar first, through some error, and he, bless him, passed it to his right. I watched it travel the wrong way round that table with the feeling one has watching a child wander onto a railway line. The solicitor shut his eyes. My Uncle Leo watched it come and said nothing whatever, but he took it up when it reached him and set it going the proper way again, with one finger, as he might have corrected a pendulum.

Edmund got up and went round and sat next to the man Cade. He turned the chair about and sat astride it, like a subaltern. I couldn't hear what they said. I didn't need to. Edmund was smiling and the detective wasn't, and from time to time each of them glanced, I was nearly sure, down the table at me.

They were discussing my case. That was plain. My cousin Edmund was no doubt pleading for me. *He's young. He's Dorothea's grandson. It was probably only a small one.* And the man Cade would be shaking his head, the way they do. At one point Edmund laughed out loud and hit the other man on the shoulder. Then he got up and limped out.

I took that to be a good sign. I thought Edmund had got me off.

I resolved to thank him. More than that, it came to me that Edmund was the man to advise me. He was a Vane. He had grown up with the old gentleman and would know how these things were best put. He had, by his sister's account, once been handy with clocks himself. For all I knew he could mend it. I had a vision of my cousin Edmund in his shirtsleeves, with a little screwdriver, putting the fern back.

I therefore decided to find Edmund and tell him everything. I should like the reader to mark that decision. All that followed came out of it: the brandy, the vicar, the ice house, and the wheelbarrow.

But first I had a better idea. I have learned to dread those.

* * *

It came to me as I crossed the hall. Everybody had gone through to the drawing room, where my cousin Kit was going to play the piano; I could hear him beginning, something watery. The hall was empty. There were the candles and the fire, and the Font on its table. And round the walls stood about a dozen grandfather clocks.

A dozen clocks, I thought. And every one of them was full of wheels.

I ask the reader to follow my reasoning, for it has a certain beauty. I had in my pocket the works of a clock. The difficulty was that they were in my pocket. But suppose they were to be found in a place where one might expect the works of a clock to be? Suppose I were simply to open the door of one of those tall cases and tip the whole lot in at the bottom? Who would look there? And if, years hence, some man came to clean it and found a few extra wheels and a fern lying in the dust, what would he conclude? That they had dropped off. Clocks must shed things. Everything does.

There would then be no evidence. There would be an empty table in the Blue Room, and a mystery, and the very capable man could reconstruct until he was blue.

I chose my clock with care. Most of them were fancy, with gilt and flowers and little windows. But on the left of the fireplace stood a plain, sober one, tall and narrow, in dark wood, with a long glass door down the front. I thought it had a discreet look. It was the kind of clock that would keep a secret.

I went over to it on tiptoe. I looked both ways. I opened the long door. Inside there was a brass pendulum going to and fro, very slowly, with a bob on it like a soup plate. Beside that, hanging on a cord, about level with my waistcoat, was a weight. It was a plain grey cylinder. I should say it was about the size of a large tin of beans.

I mention the weight because I touched it. I had to move it aside to see the bottom of the case, and I was surprised by it. It was a great deal heavier than it looked. It swung back and knocked on the wood, and I steadied it. I can state, therefore, that at about twenty past ten that night, by the dial above it, the weight of that clock was hanging in its case where it belonged, and that it was clean and dry, and cold to the hand. I was asked about that a long time afterwards, by somebody who had a reason for asking. I had never thought it worth mentioning.

I got my hand into my pocket. I got hold of a good fistful, the dial and some wheels. I had it out, and was bending to drop it into the bottom of the case.

And all the lights went out.

I don't know whether the reader has ever stood in the dark, in a strange house, with his arm inside a grandfather clock and a handful of broken evidence, while somewhere a piano stops in the middle of a note. It concentrates the mind. My first thought was that it was a trap, and that the detective had arranged it. I pictured them rushing in with torches. I snatched my hand back, and barked my knuckles, and the pendulum caught my cuff. The fern got out and went up my sleeve.

Nobody rushed in. The piano started again. It was only the candles I had lost sight of for a moment; my eyes came back, and there was the hall, in the firelight, exactly as before. But I could hear footsteps on the kitchen stairs, and a voice, a woman's, saying that it was that dratted transformer.

I shut the long door. I put the fistful back in my pocket. When Mrs Pask came through the baize door with an oil lamp in each hand, I was standing in the middle of the hall, whistling.

"Was that you at my regulator, Mr Toby?" she said.

"Me? No. Rather not. I was just—it's the fuses, I expect. I'll have a look at the box, shall I? I'm rather good with fuses."

I am not good with fuses. I have never looked at a fuse box in my life. But there was a pair of steps standing against the wall, and I had got up them, and was holding a candle to the ceiling in a knowledgeable way, when the man Cade came out of the drawing-room passage and stood watching me.

Mrs Pask told me to get down. She said it wasn't the fuses, it was the whole Ness, and that she would not have me on her floor with my neck broken and a goose carcass to boil. I got down. The detective asked her for a lantern, and she gave him one. He put his hat on and went out of the side door into the storm, without a word to anyone.

I thought that an odd thing for an insurance man to do. Then I remembered that he was not an insurance man. I supposed he had gone out to search the Midget.

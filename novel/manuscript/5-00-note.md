# PART FIVE {.part}

::: {.parttitle}
Saltcote

*as told by Tom Garrow*
:::

## Editor's Note {.note}

Thomas Garrow never wrote anything down that was longer than a stock return. He left school at fifteen. From 1974 to 1994 he worked cattle and shod horses on the eastern slope of the Rockies in southern Alberta, and from 1994 until he died, in the winter of 2021, he farmed a hundred and forty acres of Essex marsh. He was the least talkative man I have ever known.

But he told this. He told it over a number of years, in pieces, to one listener, usually late and usually out of doors. He told it always in the same words, as people do who have gone over a thing many times in their own heads, and he would stop if he was interrupted and go back to the beginning of the piece. I have set it down in those words as nearly as I am able. I am confident of most of them. I heard them often enough.

Two things about the telling I have kept, because I do not think the account is his without them.

He never said *I*. Not about that night. He would say *I* about anything else: the price of hoggets, what he had for breakfast. But when he came to the Saturday he said *the man*, and went on saying it until the story was done. I asked him once why. He thought about it for some time and said that it hadn't felt like him. I think that he had spent twenty years in a country where a man with a horse and a grievance and a gun is a kind of person that everybody recognises, and that on that one night he found he had become it, and that it was easier to look at from a little way off.

And he did not report speech as other people do. He would not put on a voice, or say *he said* and then the words. He ran it all in, flat, what was said along with what was done, as if talk were only one more thing that happened, like weather. I have left out the quotation marks accordingly. You will find that you do not miss them.

His times are from his own wristwatch, which he had set by the wireless that morning. He was never inside the house and never saw one of its clocks. So he is the only witness of the seven whose midnight is England's midnight. When you set him beside the last account, you will see what that does.

He believed until the end of his life that he had killed Edmund Vane. He did not put that in the story. He said it to me once only, in a hospital, and I could not then tell him what I knew.

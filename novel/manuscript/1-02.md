## 2. Thirteen at Table

They'd given me a room on the first floor at the back, over the kitchen yard. It had a brass bed, a washstand, a view of a wet wall, and two clocks. I timed them against the Tissot out of my sock. One was fifty-eight minutes slow and the other was an hour and two. I split the difference and called it house time, and I sat on the bed and smoked and thought about what I had.

What I had was five clean pieces of brass, a threat with a smile on it, and an old man who'd as good as given me his son and then begged me not to take him. In the job we used to say that when the family hands you somebody in the first hour, you thank them politely and go on looking. The first one they hand you is the one they can spare.

At ten to seven by me I put the watch back in the sock, shaved for the second time that day, and went down.

The hall had been got ready. Somebody had lit a fire in a grate you could have roasted the tractor in and put candles about, though the electric was on as well, and the long-case clocks stood round the walls in the moving light with their brass faces shining like a jury that had already made up its mind. The basin was on its table in the middle. The Font. There were more watches in it now.

I got myself a whisky from a tray and a place by the wall, and I did what I do. I watched them come in.

Fenn first, with a clergyman. Fenn in a dinner jacket looked the same as Fenn in a suit, only more so. He gave me a nod that would have fooled anyone and said, "Ah, the gentleman from Pemberton's," and I said good evening, and that was all we said to each other until one in the morning. He unstrapped a thin gold watch and laid it in the basin like a man putting money in a plate. The clergyman was seventy-odd and soft and pink, with white floss over his ears and the smile of a man who has been deaf for some years and found that it suits him. The Reverend Stiles. He took a hunter out of his waistcoat, looked at it fondly, said, "Well, goodbye for the present," and dropped it in.

Then a noise on the gravel like a tank regiment, and the front door opening, and a gale, and with the gale a woman of about eighty in a mackintosh over a black velvet frock, with a face like a good horse and a voice you could have used to bring ships in.

"Leo! Where is he? Don't tell me he's still in the cellar with his bits. I've come across in the Land Rover with one headlamp and no brakes to speak of, and I want a drink before I want anything." She saw the basin. "Oh, that damn thing." She dragged a man's watch off a wrist like a fence post and threw it in from six feet. "There. Rob me. Edmund, you've put on weight."

"Octavia," said Edmund Vane. He'd changed. In a dinner jacket he looked like what he must have been at thirty, the kind of man other men's wives remember. He'd also kept on drinking, at a steady pace, like a man walking somewhere he didn't want to arrive. "You look magnificent. Like a hearse."

"I look like an old woman who's buried two husbands and means to dine. Who's this?"

This was me.

"Mr Cade," Edmund said. "He's come to see whether we're worth burning down."

"Insurance," I said.

"How perfectly ghastly for you," said Dame Octavia Prowse, and looked me over as if she were thinking of buying me for a child to learn on. "Do you hunt?"

"Only people."

"Ha!" she said, and took the whisky out of my hand, thinking it was hers, and drank it.

The tall young man in the yellow pullover turned up next, minus the pullover, in a dinner jacket that he'd plainly had since school and grown four inches out of at the wrists. He was pink, with a damp fair forelock and a hopeful expression, like a Labrador that has been told it may come in if it's good. Toby something. A nephew, or the nephew of a nephew. He came down the stairs explaining to nobody in particular about the carburettor of an MG, shook hands with me twice, and then stood in front of the Font going red.

"I say, must I really? Only it's a bit—"

He rolled up his cuff. He was wearing a Mickey Mouse watch. The hands were Mickey's arms, in little yellow gloves.

"It was a present," he said. "From a girl. It's ironic."

"What's ironic about it?" said the boy who'd driven the tractor.

"Well. I mean. One wears it ironically."

"Does Mickey know?"

That was the grandson. Christopher, called Kit. Twenty-four, thin as a rail, black hair in his eyes, a pianist's hands, a mouth too old for him. He stood about with a cigarette and watched his father. I noticed that. All evening, whatever else Kit Vane was looking at, he had a line out to where his father was, the way you'd keep an eye on a pan of milk.

And with him the girl off the tractor. Saskia. She had about a quarter of an inch of dark hair all over her head and a ring through one eyebrow and a pair of boots like a navvy's, and above the boots she had on an evening dress. It was silk, sleeveless, cut plain and well, and it was green. A real green, a dark jewel of a green, emerald you'd call it. It made the stubble on her head look like something done on purpose. She'd have been twenty-two.

I'm telling you about the dress for a reason.

Because ten minutes later Celia Vane came down the stairs, with a hand on the rail and the candlelight working for her, and got to the fourth step from the bottom, and stopped.

She was wearing the same dress.

Not near enough. Not something like. The same: the same silk, the same green, the same cut at the neck, the same length. On her it sat differently, because she was forty-eight and had the shoulders for it and the girl was twenty-two and had the boots. But if you'd hung the two on a rail in a shop you couldn't have told them apart, and somewhere, I suppose, somebody had.

There was one of those silences. Even the clocks seemed to hold off.

"Oh, *bloody* hell," said Saskia, cheerfully. "The Liberty sale?"

"The Liberty sale," said Celia Vane.

"It was the only one in the shop that didn't look like upholstery. D'you want me to go and change? I've got a thing with a zip."

I watched Celia Vane decide. It took her a second, and you could see the woman who'd been on a stage. She came down the last four steps smiling.

"Don't you dare," she said. "We'll tell them it's a uniform. Edmund, look, there are two of me. You've always said one wasn't enough."

"I've never said that in my life," said Edmund Vane.

People laughed. The clocks started up again. But I'd seen her face on the fourth step, before she got it under control, and it wasn't the dress she'd minded. A woman like that doesn't mind another woman in her frock. She'd minded standing next to twenty-two.

So there were two green dresses in that house that night. I knew it at seven o'clock. I stood there with a fresh whisky and watched the pair of them, the fair head and the cropped one, the same green twice in the firelight. Six hours later I saw that green in two places at once, in the dark, with a headache, and I didn't put it together for the best part of a year. I'm not offering an excuse. I'm telling you what a detective's worth with a drink in him. You see everything. You could pass an examination on it. Then when it matters you believe your eyes.

* * *

The daughter came down last but one. Imogen. Forty-four, by Fenn's reckoning, and she looked it honestly and didn't seem to mind. Dark hair pinned up, with a streak of dead white running back from the left temple, the kind people are born with. A plain black dress. She came down the stairs looking at the room the way I'd been looking at it, taking it in piece by piece, and when she got to me she stopped and took me in too, and I knew where I'd seen the eyes before. They were up in the long gallery in a gold frame.

"You're the valuer," she said. "My brother says you've been on your knees to the Thorne."

"It seemed the least I could do."

"What did you make of it?"

"It makes me feel poor."

"It makes everybody feel something. I used to think it was watching me. All those faces." She had a quiet voice with Edinburgh laid over the top of Essex. "I clean paintings for a living, Mr Cade. I spend my days telling what's two hundred years old from what somebody put there last Tuesday. If you'd like a second opinion on anything in this house, you might ask me." She said it quite lightly, and she looked across the hall at her brother while she said it, and then she moved on and took a glass of sherry and went to stand by herself in front of the fire.

I thought: that's two of them. The old man, and now the sister. This family's got a way of telling you things by not telling you.

Then the old man came in, and the room turned round to face him the way iron filings do.

He'd been got into evening clothes. They hung on him, they'd been made for a broader man, the man he'd been. But he came across his hall at eighty-one as straight as he could manage with his white hair brushed and his eyeglass on its cord, and he was the only one in that house, I'll say this, who looked as if the clothes belonged to the century they were cut in. On his arm, or rather with her hand under his elbow so that you'd hardly see, came a small plain girl in a dark blue frock with a high neck. Sandy hair. No colour in her face at all. That was the nurse, Miss Quaile.

She looked at everything. He'd been right about that. She came into the hall and her eyes went round it once, the clocks, the fire, the faces, me, and I had the feeling she'd counted us and would know if one went missing. Then she looked at Edmund Vane. It was only for a moment. But I used to sit in court and watch the women in the public gallery, and I know how they look at the man in the dock when they've decided he didn't do it.

Leo Vane stopped at the Font and looked down into it.

"Are we all given up?" he said.

"All present and disarmed, Father," said Edmund.

"Mr Cade doesn't carry one," said Celia. She didn't look at me.

"Doesn't he? Doesn't he. A wise man. A wise man knows he hasn't the time." He put a long forefinger into the basin and stirred the watches gently, like a child with pebbles. "Poor things. They are all wrong, you know. Every one of these is an hour wrong and has been since the last Sunday of March, and their owners have gone about consulting them and catching trains. An Act of Parliament. A man called Willett, a builder, who wanted more light for his golf. And so the whole country agrees to tell the same lie about where the sun is, and tells it for seven months in the year, and is very much surprised when things go amiss." He took the finger out. "Not in my house. You're on Compline time now. It is seven minutes past six. In six hours and fifty-three minutes they'll come to their senses and agree with us, and we shall open the door to them. Until then you will kindly take the hour from me."

"Hear, hear," said the vicar, who hadn't caught a word.

"Is Margot down?" said Leo Vane.

Nobody answered him. The nurse's hand moved a little on his arm. His daughter turned round from the fire.

"She'll be along, Papa," Imogen said. "Come and sit down."

I looked at the faces. I'm a professional and it was the thing to do. Edmund was looking into his glass. Celia was turning her ring. The boy was watching his father. The girl with no hair was whispering to the boy, *who's Margot?* and being shushed. The solicitor was studying a clock. Dame Octavia Prowse was looking at Leo Vane with her big plain face gone quiet and I saw, as you sometimes do see it all at once in old people, that fifty or sixty years ago she'd wanted that man and hadn't got him.

And the pink young man, Toby, was patting his pockets one after another with a look of horror, like somebody who's left the gas on. He'd been doing it on and off since he came down. I filed that too.

* * *

We went in at eight, their time. Nine by the watch in my sock, and the wind getting up outside, so that the long curtains in the dining room breathed in and out.

It was a fine room. Panelling, portraits of people who'd owned the place before the Vanes had, a table that had been polished since the Reform Bill. Candles, again, in silver. There were three clocks: a tall one in the corner, a bracket affair on the sideboard, and a gilt thing on the chimneypiece with a nymph on it. I'd done that room in the afternoon. The nymph was right. The other two were copies.

I counted the places because I count things. There were thirteen.

I counted the people. Leo Vane, Edmund, Celia, the daughter, the boy, his girl. The pink nephew. The vicar, the solicitor, the old woman. The nurse. Me. I made it twelve, and I made it twelve a second time.

The thirteenth place was on the old man's right hand. It was laid like the others, the knives and the forks and the three glasses and a napkin folded like a bishop's hat. The chair was drawn up. Nobody went near it. They went round it, I noticed, even the ones who hadn't been before, the way you step round a wet patch on a floor without looking down.

"I shouldn't, Mr Cade," Celia Vane said in my ear. She'd come up beside me. Close to, she had scent on, something old-fashioned and dark with a peach in it somewhere. "That one's taken."

"Who by?"

"My mother-in-law. She's dined with us every year since she died. She has a very small appetite and no conversation, and she's still the only one at the table my father-in-law listens to." She showed me my own place with a tilt of the fair head. It was the other side, three down, between the vicar and the girl with no hair. "We're thirteen. I hope you aren't superstitious. They say the first to get up is the first to die. Octavia always makes a point of going out for a cigarette before the pudding, so she's due any decade now."

"What about you? Are you superstitious?"

"I'm an actress," she said. "I'm superstitious about everything. I just don't let it stop me."

She went to the foot of the table. Her husband, I noticed, was not at the head. The old man had the head. Edmund Vane sat halfway down on the far side, opposite me, with his back to the windows and the curtains moving behind him, and the sister on one side of him and the nurse on the other. He filled his own glass before the girl could get round with the bottle.

I've eaten worse dinners. I've eaten them off my knee in an Escort, watching a door. There was soup. There was the goose, brought in by the big girl, Gail, who was scarlet with the weight of it, and behind her in the doorway for a second I saw the cook, a square woman of sixty with forearms like a docker's, checking that we'd noticed. The old man carved. He did it standing, with a knife he'd obviously sharpened himself, and it was like watching him at the bench: every slice the same, nothing wasted, not a tremor. The girl Saskia said that she didn't eat anything with a face. Dame Octavia said she'd never met a goose with one worth mentioning. The pink young man told a story about the car that went on for some time and had a ferry in it. The vicar smiled at everybody. The claret came round and it was what Edmund had said, the only thing in the house that matched its label.

And all through it the place on the old man's right. They put soup at it. They took the soup away. They gave it goose. Leo Vane turned to it now and then and said a few words too low to hear, and waited, and nodded. Nobody looked. Everybody knew to the inch where it was.

I've sat in rooms with a body in them, and I've sat in rooms with a man who was going to be a body by morning and didn't know it. I won't say I felt anything that night at the table. I'm not the type and I'd had four whiskies and a good deal of the claret. But I'll say that it was the first dinner I ever ate where the empty chair was doing most of the talking.

The nurse didn't eat much. She cut things up. She kept half an eye on the old man and the other half, which she thought nobody saw, on the big man next to her, on his glass going up and down. Once, reaching for the salt, his sleeve brushed her wrist, and the colour came up her neck over the high blue collar like a tide over mud.

Opposite me, through the candles, the sister was talking quietly to her brother. I couldn't hear. But I saw him say something short, and I saw her say something back, and then I saw her say two words that I can lip-read in any language, because they're a name and I'd heard it once that day. She said *Tom Garrow*.

Edmund Vane put his glass down.

"Where?" he said. That I heard.

She said something. *Thursday*. *The funeral*.

"He's got no business here." He wasn't keeping his voice down now. "That farm's out at Lady Day. Fenn's served the notice. He can go back to his bloody cows."

"Edmund."

"Twenty years. And he comes back to stand at a graveside in a hat and look across at this house like—" He stopped. He'd noticed the table had gone quiet. He picked the glass up again and smiled round it. "A tenant of ours," he said, to me, since I was opposite and a stranger. "Old Garrow, at the farm on the other side. You'll have passed it. Died a fortnight ago. Rather a character. The son's turned up from Canada to see what he can get."

"Lean man? Hat? Grey horse?"

The sister's head came round.

"I saw him this morning," I said. "By the road. He had a good look at me."

"I'll bet he did," said Edmund Vane.

Imogen Vane didn't say anything at all. She looked down at her plate. She had a fork in her hand and she kept on holding it. It's a funny thing, what the blood does. In the nurse it had gone up. In her it went away. That white streak in her hair wasn't the whitest thing about her for a minute.

I thought: so that's how it is. And I thought: it's nothing to do with clocks. I put it down the side of my mind with the nephew patting his pockets and I gave my attention back to the money.

I'd give a good deal to have that minute over. They all would. It turned out that was the theme of the evening.

* * *

They cleared the goose. The big girl put down a dish of something with cream, and Dame Octavia Prowse pushed her chair back with a noise like a gate.

"I'm going out for a gasper," she said. "Somebody's got to be first up, and I've had my money's worth. Don't let him start till I'm back."

"Sit down, Octavia," said Leo Vane.

He hadn't raised his voice. But she sat. He was getting to his feet, pushing himself up with his fists on the cloth, and the nurse half rose behind him and then didn't. He stood at the head of his table with the candles lighting him from underneath, the long face and the white hair, and he picked up his glass.

"We have a custom," he said, "which the strangers among us will forgive. Before the sweet, we drink to someone." He turned to the empty place. He inclined his head to it, an inch. "My dear. With your leave."

Then to the rest of us.

"To absent time."

They got up. I got up with them. There was the scrape of twelve chairs and they said it, not all together, raggedly, like a response in church. *Absent time.* Edmund Vane said it into his glass. The daughter didn't say it; her lips moved. The vicar said "Amen."

We sat. The old man stayed on his feet.

"It's the twentieth," he said. "Twenty years tonight. I thought I should say something about that, and this afternoon I knew what it was to be, and now I don't. I've lost it." He said it quite simply, the way you'd say you had lost a collar stud. "That happens. You'll all have noticed that it happens. You're very kind about it. You say, Never mind, Papa, and Never mind, Leo, and you look at one another. I notice that. I notice a good deal more than you would like."

"Father," said Edmund.

"I'm speaking, Edmund." The glass in his hand didn't move. "I have found that when I lose a thing, it's of no use to hunt for it. I must go on with something else, and it comes back by itself. So I shall tell you the something else. It will interest you. It's about clocks."

His eyes went round the table. They were very clear. They went from face to face and they didn't skip anyone; they stopped on the vicar, and on the girl in the boots, and on me. When they got to me there was nothing in them at all to say we'd met.

"One of you has been robbing me," Leo Vane said.

Nobody moved. That's what happens. In the films there's a gasp and somebody drops a fork. In life they go still, the guilty ones and the others just the same, because the innocent are always sure it means them.

"It has been going on for a year, perhaps two. Not money. I shouldn't notice money. Clocks. One of you has been taking clocks out of this house, good ones, and leaving copies standing in their cases. My copies. I made them with these." He put down the glass at last and held up his two hands, backs toward us, the long fingers apart. "It was thought, I suppose, that I should never know the difference. Because I am old, and I say Margot when I mean somebody else, and I can't always find the room I'm looking for. It was thought I would walk past my own work every day and take it for Tompion's."

He let that sit.

"I can't find my spectacles. I can't tell you the Prime Minister's name. But I'll tell you this. I could go round this house blindfold and put my hand on every clock in it and tell you who made it, and in what year, and whether he was left-handed, and whether on that day he was pleased with himself. There are two in this room that I made. That one. And that." He pointed, without looking, at the long-case in the corner and the bracket clock on the sideboard. He was right both times. "The nymph is Mr Vulliamy's own, and she is no better than she should be."

"Leo," said Fenn, quietly. "Is this quite the—"

"It is exactly the, Lionel. It's the one night in the year on which it can be said." He leaned on his fists. "I don't know which of you it is. I'm telling you that I don't know. I've a man finding out for me, a very capable man. I can't at this moment remember his name, nor where I've put him, but I have every confidence in him, and he'll have told me before the door is opened. I shall know which of you it is by one o'clock."

I kept my face where it was. It wasn't easy. I had the vicar's elbow in my ribs and a spoonful of cream halfway to my mouth, and across the table Edmund Vane was looking straight at me, not at his father, with a little smile, as if he'd just been dealt the card he was expecting.

"And then," said Leo Vane, "it'll be the Hour. And whoever it is may come and tell me why. That's all. I shan't want anything else. Not the police. Not the money. I should like the clocks back, if they are to be had, but I daresay they are not. I should like to know why. Whoever you are, you were taught in this house that what is said in that hour is forgiven when it ends. I taught you that. Your mother taught you that. I have never broken it and I shall not begin tonight." His voice shook, the first time. "I've not got so many hours left that I can afford to waste the odd one. You will kindly not waste it either."

He sat down. He sat down rather suddenly, and the nurse had her hand under his arm before he was in the chair. He looked at the plate in front of him.

"What is this?" he said.

"It's a syllabub, Mr Vane."

"Is it? Did I ask for it?" He turned to the empty place on his right. "Margot, did we order a syllabub?"

* * *

I've said I looked at faces. I looked at them then as hard as I ever have, and I'll give you what I got, for what it's worth, and it was worth nothing, and I've gone over it a thousand times.

Edmund Vane was smiling at me. I've said that.

His wife was looking at him. Not at the old man, not at me. At her husband, down the length of the table, with no expression at all, and her thumb had stopped turning the ring.

Kit Vane was looking at his father too. But the boy's face had gone tight and white round the nose. He looked the way a kid looks who's heard his parents fighting through a wall for years and now somebody's opened the door.

The daughter was looking at the empty chair.

The nurse was looking at her own hands in her lap.

Fenn had his eyes shut. The vicar was eating syllabub. Dame Octavia was watching Leo Vane with her jaw set, like somebody watching a good horse put at a fence it won't get over. The girl Saskia was looking round the table from one to the next with her mouth a little open, quite frankly enjoying herself, as you would at twenty-two.

And the nephew. Toby. The pink young man had gone the colour of the tablecloth. He had one hand flat against the right-hand pocket of his dinner jacket, pressing, the way you'd hold a wound shut. There was sweat on his lip. If I'd been asked there and then to put my hand on the collar of the guiltiest man in the room, on looks alone, I'd have gone round the table and lifted him out of his chair.

Which shows you. He had the works of a ten-pound carriage clock in that pocket, as it turned out. He'd knocked it off a table in his bedroom at half past five and trodden on it. I got that from him in the yard next morning, when it couldn't matter less. All night, that boy thought the old man's speech was about him.

That's the trouble with looking at people. Fenn had said my business was the people. But people don't look guilty of what they've done. They look guilty of what they're thinking about.

* * *

The women went out. They still did it in that house. The port went round, the wrong way, because the vicar had it. Edmund Vane came and sat in the chair next to mine. He turned it and sat astride it.

"A very capable man," he said. "I do hope he remembers where he's put you."

"I'm from Pemberton's, Mr Vane."

"Of course you are. And I'm the Queen of the May." He was drunk, but it was the big man's kind, the kind that's had a lot of practice. It only showed in how carefully he put things down. "Don't worry. I shan't spoil it. I think it's the first thing he's done on his own account in five years; I'm rather proud of him. What's old Fenn paying you?"

"Ask old Fenn."

"I'll give you the same again to go to bed with a book." He said it almost kindly. "I mean it. You've had your dinner. You've found whatever you've found. Tell him at one o'clock that it's me. You'll be right, as far as it goes, and he'll have forgotten by Tuesday, and nobody will be any the worse. But stay indoors tonight, Cade. Stay out of the yard and off the wall. You don't know this place. There's people who do."

"That's twice you've warned me."

"That's twice I've been hospitable."

"What's happening tonight that you don't want me to see?"

He looked at me for a while. Close to, under the drink, he had a tired face. A decent face gone to seed, I'd have said, if I hadn't known better, and I've often thought since that I didn't know better at all.

"You were a policeman," he said. "They tell me you can always tell. So tell me something. You go to a house where there's been a thing done. A long time ago, say. Years. And everybody in that house has got their own idea of who did it. One thinks it was him, and another thinks it was her, and every one of them's been carrying that about for so long that it's worn a groove. And they're all wrong. What do you do?"

"Find the one who knows."

"And if the one who knows would rather go to the devil than say?"

"Then in my experience," I said, "he generally does."

Edmund Vane laughed. It was the only real laugh I heard out of him. He banged me on the shoulder with a hand like a shovel, got up off the chair, and walked out of the room with the hitch in his step. I never spoke to him again. Not a word. The next time I saw him he wasn't in a position to hear one.

* * *

It was Celia Vane who came and got me. I'd gone looking for a place to smoke and found a small room on the garden side, with a desk and a sofa and French windows and a fire that hadn't been lit long. A woman's room. There was a clock in it, naturally. The wind had hold of the French windows and was shaking them. I stood with my back to the fire and lit up with the Ronson, and the door opened, and she came in and shut it behind her and leaned on it.

In the one lamp, the green of that dress was nearly black.

"You don't wear a watch," she said, "you don't know a fusee from a fish-knife, and my father-in-law's capable man is a secret from precisely one person in this house, and that's my father-in-law. Shall we stop?"

"I didn't know we'd started."

"Give me one of those."

I gave her a cigarette and lit it. She held my wrist while I did it. Her fingers were cold. She looked at the pale band on the skin where the Tissot lived, and then she let go.

"Where is it, as a matter of interest?"

"Down my sock."

She laughed. She had a good laugh, low and short, and she looked surprised at it, like somebody who'd found a ten-pound note in an old coat. "God. And I thought you'd swallowed it." She went and sat on the arm of the sofa. "What time is it? Really. Out there."

I got it out. I felt a fool, with my trouser leg up. "Twelve minutes past eleven."

"Then the road's been gone an hour." She turned her head toward the dark glass. You couldn't see anything in it but the lamp and the two of us. "It went during the goose. I always know. I've been here twenty-six years and I feel it shut, like a door in another part of the house. Do you know what my husband did in the first year we were married? He drove me across in July with eight minutes to spare, with the water coming over the road behind us like something poured out of a jug, and he laughed all the way. I thought he was the most alive man I'd ever met. I was twenty-two. I'd been playing maids in Frinton."

I waited. With some people you ask. With her kind you let them work round to it, and they tell you more on the way than they mean to.

"He's the one you want," she said. "You know that already. Leo as good as drew you a picture."

"He said it wants an education."

"Edmund sat at that bench from nine years old. He could cut a wheel before he could shave. He's taken thirty or so in all, Mr Cade, not eleven. Leo hasn't found the half of them. He started four years ago with a little French carriage clock nobody'd looked at since the war, and he was sick afterwards. I mean literally. I held his head."

"Where do they go?"

"A man comes. Or Edmund goes up to town with a suitcase."

"What man?"

"I've never seen him. He telephones, and he says, *Tell Mr Vane that Ronnie rang*, and he rings off."

I'd had the cigarette halfway to my mouth. I finished the journey, slowly.

"Ronnie who?"

"Sears. Is that right? It sounds like a shop."

"It's right," I said.

It was right, all right. I looked at the fire for a bit. I hadn't heard that name spoken aloud by anybody but me since 1988, and I'd mostly spoken it at night, to a ceiling.

I don't want to make a meal of this. It hasn't much to do with clocks. But you'll want to know why I did what I did later, and some of it's here. Ronnie Sears had a car lot on the Southend Arterial and an amusement arcade and a share in two clubs, and behind those he lent money, to the kind of people who can't get it from a bank, at the kind of interest they don't put on a poster. When the people couldn't pay, things happened to them. In 1987 a thing happened to a man called Georgiou who ran a restaurant in Westcliff, and he was found in his own cold store, and I was on the team that looked into it. We knew it was Sears. We couldn't get near him. So my guv'nor got a confession out of a nineteen-year-old kitchen porter with a mental age of about eleven, who'd have confessed to the Great Fire of London for a cup of tea and someone to be nice to him. And I was the one in the room. I didn't write it. I didn't hit him. I sat there and I witnessed it and I signed. Then, a year after, when it was going to appeal and they asked me, I said what had happened in that room. I've never been sure why. I wasn't being brave. I think I just got tired of the kid's face.

The porter came out. My guv'nor took his pension early. And I found that nobody in the Metropolitan Police would sit at the same canteen table with me, and that my wife had been hearing things, and one way and another by the end of that year I was a private citizen with a room in Leigh.

Ronnie Sears sent me a bottle of Scotch. With a card. I've still got the card.

"You know him," Celia Vane said. She'd been watching my face.

"I know of him. How much does your husband owe?"

"A hundred and eighty thousand. That's what he owed in the summer. I don't know how it works, it seems to grow by itself. It started with the sea wall, and then there was the roof, and then there were some horses that were going to make our fortune, and then it was just to pay the last lot. He doesn't tell me. I go through his pockets. That's what twenty-six years comes to, Mr Cade. I go through his pockets while he's in the bath." She tapped ash into the grate, exactly, without looking. "A hundred and eighty thousand pounds. You can't pay that off in carriage clocks."

"No."

"No. So what would you do? If you were Edmund. If there were one thing left in the house that'd clear the whole of it in a night, sitting on a table in the library under a cloth."

"I'd be very tempted," I said.

"He's past tempted. It's tonight."

The French windows banged. The lamp dipped and came back.

"How do you know?"

"Because Ronnie rang. On Thursday. I took the call. He didn't say *tell Mr Vane Ronnie rang*. He said, *Tell him the boat'll be there at the slack.*" She said it in a flat voice, another man's voice; she was good. "I've lived on this island since I was twenty-two. I know what the slack is. It's the top of the tide, when the water stops. It's the only time you can bring a boat into the Hythe on a night like this without losing it. Tonight that's a quarter to one."

"House time."

"A quarter to two by your sock."

"And you're telling me this why? You want him stopped?"

She got up. She came over to the fire. She was a tall woman and in her shoes she could look me in the eye, and she did. That scent again. I found out later what it's called. I can't smell it now without my heart doing something a doctor wouldn't like.

"I want him watched," she said. "That's all. I'm not asking you to do anything to him. You heard Leo. He's told that table there's a man finding out. Edmund knows it's you. Kit knows it's you, and Kit's twenty-four and thinks his father's Lord Byron. And at a quarter to one there'll be a boat at the Hythe with one of this Sears's people in it, expecting to be handed a million pounds in a box. And between here and the Hythe there's half a mile of sea wall in the dark, and my husband, who's been drinking since noon, and who's got a bad knee, and who—" She stopped. She put the back of her hand against her mouth for a second. It was well done. I'm saying that now. At the time I didn't think it was done at all. "They don't need him once they've got it, do they? People like that. What's a hundred and eighty thousand to them against the clock? They could take the box and settle the debt both, with one shove. Nobody'd ever know. He'd have gone off the wall drunk. It's happened here before."

"Mrs Vane—"

"I've been frightened for four years. I'd like to stop. I'd like somebody else to hold it for one night, somebody who knows what those people are. I'll pay you. Whatever Fenn's paying, I'll double it. I've got some money of my own that Edmund hasn't found yet. Stay near him. See that he gets to that boat and hands the bloody thing over and comes back up the wall on his own two feet. That's all I want. I don't care about the clock. Let them have the clock."

"And Mr Vane senior? I'm supposed to tell him at one."

"Tell him. Tell him it's Edmund, he knows it's Edmund. He'll forgive him in the Hour and forget the lot by breakfast. Only the clock'll be gone by then, and so will Mr Sears, and I'll have my husband." She looked at me. "It isn't much of a husband. It's the only one I've got."

Now I'll tell you what I thought, standing there. I was fifty-one and I'd been a detective for twenty years.

I thought: she's lying. Not about the debt, or Sears, or the boat; all that had the feel of the real thing, and it checked with what I'd seen in Edmund Vane's face. But there was something she wanted that she hadn't said, and the fright was laid over the top of it like a coat of paint. I'd watched her decide on the stairs. I'd seen how quickly she could get a face on. A woman who turns her ring like that isn't thinking about how to keep her husband.

And I thought: I don't care. Because of the cold fingers, and the laugh she hadn't expected, and because nobody had come and stood that close to me and asked me for anything in six years. And because of Ronnie Sears. You'll have to believe that part. If somebody came to you and said the man who'd cost you everything was sending a boat, and would you like to be standing there when it tied up, I think you'd go and get your coat.

"I'm already working for one client in this house," I said.

"I know. I shan't tell him if you don't."

"That's not how it's supposed to go."

"Nothing in this house is how it's supposed to go, Mr Cade. It isn't even the right time."

There was a piano going somewhere, down the passage. Somebody had started to play. Slow stuff, rippling, like water going over stones. She turned her head to listen, and I saw the line of her throat above the green.

"That's Kit," she said. "It's the Schubert. He plays it every year. His grandmother liked it." She threw the end of her cigarette into the fire. "We'd better go in. They'll be counting heads."

At the door she stopped.

"What do I call you? I can't keep saying Mr Cade. It sounds like a draper."

"Harry."

"Harry." She tried it. "Find him for me, Harry. Don't lose him. Whatever happens, come and tell me first."

She went out. I stayed and finished my cigarette, and I took the Tissot out of my sock and strapped it on my wrist, under the cuff, where it belonged. I'd had enough of being told the time by other people.

It was twenty past ten. No. I mean it was twenty past eleven. You see how it gets into you.

I went along the passage toward the music with two clients, three whiskies too many, and a feeling at the back of my neck I hadn't had in years, as if somebody'd opened a file on me. I got to the drawing-room door. I had my hand on it.

And every light in the house went out.

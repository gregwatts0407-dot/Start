## 4. In Which I Fetch the Pudding

The wind had gone down a good deal. It was still raining, in a half-hearted way, and there was a moon coming and going behind the clouds. I crossed the kitchen yard with my hand round the candle and found the track behind the stables without difficulty. Mrs Pask had said one couldn't miss it, and I've noticed that people who say that are right about half the time. It was a grassy lump among some trees, with a brick archway in the side of it, and an iron gate.

The gate was shut. I pushed it and it was locked. I recall feeling pleased about that, because it meant I got to use the key. I am fond of keys. I put the saucer down, found the one with the red wool, and turned it, and the gate swung in. I left the ring hanging in the lock so as not to lose it.

There was a short brick passage, and a wooden door standing open, and then cold. I have been in the cellars at Épernay in February. This was colder. I came out on a little landing at the top of a round chamber, all of brick, like the inside of a beehive, and a flight of steps went down the curve of the wall into the dark. They were very steep and they shone.

*Mind them steps*, she had said. *They're greasy, and there's no rail.*

I minded them. I went down sideways, with the saucer held out in front of me in one hand and the spoon in the other, feeling for each step with my foot before I trusted it. I counted nine. On the ninth I put the candle down, on the step, to have both hands for the wall. I looked to see how many were left.

There was a pair of shoes on the bottom three.

They were black evening shoes, large, toes up. They had feet in them. The feet were attached to legs, in black trousers, which ran down the last of the steps onto the floor, and at the end of the legs lay the rest of my cousin Edmund. He was on his back in the straw with one arm flung out. His head was lower than his feet, and tipped back, and his face was toward me. His eyes were open.

I'm not ashamed to say I let out a yell. It went round that brick dome three or four times and came back at me. I sat down on the step. The spoon went out of my hand and I heard it ring on the bricks below and slither.

For a period that I cannot measure, I looked at Edmund and he looked at the ceiling. There was a mark on the left side of his forehead, a dark swelling, with blood on it, and blood had run back into his hair.

I thought: this time it's real. This is the one. They put the stuff in his glass, and he's dead of it, and I stood there and said brandy can't be corked.

And then my cousin Edmund snored.

I want to be exact about that snore, for it's the most important noise in this narrative. It was not loud, or even much of a snore, as I understand the term. It was a long, slow, rasping breath, drawn in through the open mouth, with a sort of click at the top of it, and then a pause so long that I thought it had been the last. Then it was let out again, in a sigh. I counted. Eight seconds, and another. The fourth button of the vicar's waistcoat had gone up and down at just that rate, in the library, half an hour before.

Miss Quaile's voice came back to me as clearly as if she had been standing on the step above. *He's very deeply asleep. He's breathing, eight or nine to the minute. You could fire a gun by his ear.*

Of course. I can't describe the relief. I very nearly laughed. He had drunk half of the same glass. He was a big man, she had said, and it would take longer with him. He had walked about for half an hour, feeling more and more peculiar, no doubt, and had come down here to see about his boat, or whatever it was. And on these steps, which were greasy and had no rail, it had caught up with him all at once, and he had gone from top to bottom on his head.

I got down the last few steps on my seat and bent over him with the candle.

One of his eyes was not open, in fact. It was the left, on the side of the lump, and it had swollen nearly shut. The right was half open, with the eyeball rolled up, so that there was a good deal of white. I have seen a spaniel sleep like that, in front of a fire, and it alarms one in a spaniel too. I spoke his name. I patted his cheek. I took him by the lapels and gave him the cocktail treatment, as I had the vicar. His head went to and fro and he made a sound.

"Mmf," said Edmund.

"Edmund! Wake up, old man. You've had a fall."

"Ma," he said, quite distinctly. Then: "Sorry." Then he snored again.

Well, there it was. He was alive, he was asleep, and he was talking about his mother. I reviewed my instructions. The patient was not to be left on his back with his head tipped; he could choke. He was to be laid on his side. The left.

I got hold of his far shoulder and his hip and rolled him toward me. He was a tremendous weight. He came over all of a piece, like a log, and settled on his left side in the straw with his cheek on his arm. That did bring the bad side of his head underneath. I saw that as soon as I'd done it, and I didn't care for it. But she had been most particular about the left. I got some of the dry straw and pushed it under his temple for a pillow. He groaned a bit, and then the breathing went on as before.

I sat back on my heels. I was rather pleased with myself. It was the second casualty of the night that I had dealt with on sound medical lines.

Then I remembered the pudding.

* * *

The ice was at the back of the chamber, a long heap of it under sacking and straw, sweating, with the wet running out from underneath in a shallow pool. I held the candle up. I had expected the box to be sitting on top, where I supposed Gail would have put it.

It wasn't on top. It was on the floor, at the edge of the ice, a yard from my cousin's outflung hand. It lay partly on its side in the wet. It was a wooden box with tin corners, about two feet each way, and tied round with a length of cord. *Wooden. So big.* Mrs Pask had shown me with her hands, and that was it to the inch.

I tried the weight. It was just as she'd warned me: heavy, as with ice packed round it. And when I set it upright, something inside shifted and gave a faint metallic chime. That would be the mould. They are made of copper.

I worked out afterwards how it had come to be where it was, and I may as well give the reader the benefit. Gail had put it down on the edge of the heap, in a hurry, in the dark. Edmund, coming down the steps on his head, had fetched up against the ice and knocked it off. This seemed to me at the time a complete and satisfying explanation. I still think it a good one, although I have since been told that it was wrong in every particular.

I looked for the spoon. I was supposed to lift the lid and try the edge. But the spoon had gone into the straw somewhere, by the ice, and the lid was corded down with a knot of a kind I had never seen before and could not begin to undo. I am, as I've said, no good with knots. I decided that Mrs Pask could do her own testing. A pudding that had been lying against a ton of ice was not likely to have melted.

So now I had a problem in logistics, of the kind they set at staff colleges. I had one unconscious cousin of sixteen stone and one bombe in a box. It was nearly one o'clock. I had twelve steps and no rail. I could carry the box. I could not, on any reckoning, carry Edmund.

And I could not leave him. That was quite clear. It was like a tomb down there; one could see one's breath. A man asleep on wet straw in his evening clothes, with a drug in him, would be dead of cold by morning.

I left the candle where it was, on the ninth step, to keep him company. I seem to have spent the night leaving candles with people. I went up the steps and out through the gate and back along the track to the kitchen, as fast as I could go.

* * *

"He's what?" said Mrs Pask.

"At the bottom of the steps. Asleep. He's hit his head. He's had a sleeping draught, you see, in his brandy, Miss Quaile says so, the same as the vicar. I've put him on his left side."

She didn't exclaim. She stood with her hands flat on the kitchen table and her head down, like a woman counting to ten. Then she said a thing I didn't expect.

"Every year," said Mrs Pask.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Every year, on the night. Since she went. He can't abide it, Mr Edmund can't, he never could. He's gone by eleven, and the Lord knows where he'll fetch up. I've found him in the stable. I've found him in the boathouse, in the punt, with a bottle. The year Miss Imogen didn't come I found him sat on the Wade in the dark with his feet in the water, crying like a child, and I walked him home and put him to bed and never told a soul." She looked up. "Is he bad?"

"He's got a lump. Here. It's bled a bit. But he's breathing quite steadily, and he spoke to me."

"What did he say?"

"He said 'Ma.' And then 'Sorry.'"

Her face did something. It was a good, plain, sensible face, and for a moment it looked like a girl's.

"He would," she said. Then she straightened her back, and it was gone. "Right. Mr Vane's not to know. Not tonight. Not after what he stood up and said at that table, with the whole lot of them looking at Mr Edmund sideways. If he sees him carried in dead drunk with his head broke at one in the morning, that'll be the finish. Nor madam's not to know neither. She's enough to bear."

"He isn't drunk. I mean, he is, but it's the draught, somebody put—"

"I don't care what it is. I don't want to know what it is. There's things go on upstairs in this house that I've made it my business for forty years not to know, and I'm too old to start." She was untying her apron. "And we'll not have that nurse to him. She'd be bound to tell the old gentleman; that's what she's paid for. He wants putting somewhere dry, with a rug over him, where he can sleep it off and come in at breakfast on his own feet like he always does. There's the end loose box. There's clean straw in it. He's slept there before."

"But Mrs Pask, I can't lift him. He's enormous. There's those steps."

"There's two of us," said Mrs Pask. "And there's the barrow."

It was a gardener's wheelbarrow. She had it out of a shed in the kitchen yard before I had got my breath. It was an old wooden one, long and deep, with a pair of handles like the shafts of a cart and a single iron wheel in front. She threw a couple of sacks into it. She had put on a man's cap, and over her carpet slippers she had pulled a pair of rubber galoshes.

"I've moved bigger than him," she said. "I moved a pig once, on my own, that'd died in the night. Come on."

I have told many people that the cook and I carried Edmund up the ice-house steps, and they have nodded. I would like the reader to really consider it.

He was six foot two and weighed, on my estimate, sixteen stone, and he was as limp as a wet coat. There were twelve brick steps, each about nine inches deep, covered with a kind of green grease, going up round a curve, with a wall on one side and on the other a drop. There was one candle. There was me, who has never been strong. And there was a woman of sixty-three in galoshes.

We began by dragging him to the foot of the steps. I took him under the arms from behind, and locked my hands on his chest, and walked backwards. His heels ploughed through the straw. Mrs Pask came after with the candle and told me not to bang his head.

Then we tried it with me at the top end and her at the legs. I got up three steps, backwards, hauling. She got his knees over her shoulders somehow, like a fireman, and pushed. On the fourth step my foot went from under me and I sat down. Edmund sat down on me. Mrs Pask, below, received the whole of us in a sort of avalanche and held it. I do not know how. I heard her say, through her teeth, "That pig was nothing to this."

"I can't," I said. "I'm most frightfully sorry. I can't feel my arms."

"You can. Shift your backside up one. Now pull. Now the next. Don't look down."

We did it a step at a time. I went up on my seat, one step, and dragged him up after me into my lap, and went up another. It was not dignified. Each time his head lolled back against my shirt front, and each time I heard that long rasping breath and the click at the top of it. I have never been so glad of a noise. Around the seventh step he said something else. He said, "Is it over?" in a thick voice, like a child woken in a car.

"Nearly, Mr Edmund," said Mrs Pask from below. "You go back to sleep."

And he did.

At the top we rested, with him laid out along the landing. I thought my heart would come out through my collar. Mrs Pask leaned on the wall and got her wind. She had lost her cap.

"Now the barrow," she said.

We got him into it. I shall not go into that; the principle was the same as with the steps but in reverse. He went in on his back, on the sacks, with his head toward the handles and his legs hanging out over the front, on either side of the wheel. She tucked his arms in.

"I'll take him," she said. "I know the ground. You fetch the ice."

I went down once more for the box. I looked for the spoon, without much hope, and didn't find it. I blew the candle out and left it in its saucer on the step, since I had no hand to carry it. I got the box up in my arms, like a man with a suitcase and no handle, and climbed, and came out through the gate. I didn't shut it. Nor did I take Mrs Pask's keys out of the lock. I had altogether forgotten that they were there. She was very short with me about it afterwards. It is the one thing, in the whole of this, that she ever held against me.

She was waiting on the track, between the shafts.

"Where'll I put this?" I said. The box was pulling my arms out.

Mrs Pask looked at the box, and at the barrow.

"He'll not mind," she said.

So I put the bombe on Edmund. There was nowhere else. I set it on his stomach, square, and folded his hands on top of it to keep it steady. He looked like one of those crusaders who are carved holding a model of the church they have founded.

I include that detail knowing that it does not show any of us in a dignified light. But I said I would tell this truthfully.

* * *

It is about seventy yards from the ice house to the stable yard, slightly uphill, on mud. Mrs Pask wheeled him the whole way. I walked beside, with one hand on the box. Neither of us spoke. The iron wheel cut into the soft ground and she had to lean her whole weight on it, and I could hear her breathing as well as his.

Once I thought I saw somebody. We were coming up to the end of the stable block, where the track turned into the yard. The moon had come out. And at the corner of the wall, low down, about the height of my waist, there was a face.

It was a small face, quite white, with dark hair, and it was looking round the corner at us with enormous interest. It was there for perhaps two seconds. Then it wasn't.

I said nothing to Mrs Pask. I'd already had one conversation with her that evening about things I had seen. I put it with the lift.

We came round into the yard. It was cobbled. I had sat there on the mounting block that afternoon and emptied my shoes. The tower with the clock on it stood up over us, and I looked up, as one does, and saw the two wooden figures at the top, under their little roof. Kit and the girl had dressed them. The man had on a dinner jacket and a white front. I thought he looked a good deal smarter than I did.

"Which is the end box?" I whispered.

"There. Past the block. I'll go round it. Mind, there's a drain."

I must now describe exactly what happened, because for nine years, in the telling, I have not.

In the telling it goes like this. We wheel my cousin across the yard by moonlight, with a pudding on his chest. I say that he looked like a crusader, and people laugh. And then I say: "And we tucked him up in the straw with a horse blanket, and I never saw a man sleep sounder." And we go on to the Odd Hour.

There is a piece missing. It is about four seconds long.

The yard was cobbled, as I say, and the cobbles were wet, and they were the old round kind, like the backs of loaves. A wheelbarrow with an iron wheel does not go over those. It goes between them. Mrs Pask knew it. She was taking the barrow in a wide curve round the mounting block, slowly, picking her line.

And I thought she was tired. She was sixty-three and had been on her feet since six that morning, and I was twenty-nine. I had been walking alongside with one hand on a box, like a passenger. I was ashamed. I said, "Here, let me, do let me," and before she could answer I had stepped in between the shafts beside her and got hold of the right-hand handle.

"Leave go, Mr Toby," she said. "I've got him."

"No, really, I insist."

I pushed. I am told that with a wheelbarrow one does not push one handle. The barrow slewed. The wheel came out of the line she had been keeping and dropped into the gap between two cobbles, and stopped dead, and the whole weight came forward against it and then went over sideways. It was like a boat going over. It took perhaps a second. I had the handle and I couldn't hold it. She had the other and she couldn't either.

I saved the pudding. That's the thing I have to live with. The box started to slide, and I let go of the barrow and grabbed for it with both arms, and caught it. I caught it beautifully.

Edmund went out on the other side. He went out head first.

The mounting block was just there. It was a block of stone, the height of a table, with a square edge. He was on his back, and turning, and it was the back of his head that met the corner. I heard it. I have heard a melon dropped on a kitchen floor, and it was that sound exactly.

He lay on the cobbles at the foot of the block. The barrow lay beside him with its wheel going round.

I stood there with the box in my arms. Mrs Pask had both hands over her mouth.

And then Edmund said, "Ow."

He said it quite loudly, and in an aggrieved tone. He put a hand up to the back of his head, slowly. He said a word beginning with B. Then he opened his eyes, both of them as far as they would go, and looked straight up at me, standing over him against the moon.

"Who's that?" he said.

"It's Toby," I said. My voice had gone up about an octave. "It's only Toby. Dorothea's grandson."

"What are you holding?"

"The pudding."

He thought about it. "Good," he said. "Well done." And he shut his eyes, and turned over on the wet stones onto his side, the left one, of his own accord, and drew his knees up like a boy, and began again to snore.

Mrs Pask had gone down on her knees by him. She had his head in her hands and was feeling over the back of it with her fingers, under the hair. She took them away and held them to the moon. They were dark.

"It's cut," she said. "Not deep. Heads bleed. He's his father's skull, thick as a church door; I've seen him come off a horse at the point-to-point onto a road and get up and ask who'd won." She was talking to herself as much as to me. She wiped her fingers on a sack. "He spoke. You heard him. He spoke quite sensible, and knew to ask who it was."

"He said 'well done.'"

"He did."

"Mrs Pask, I'm so sorry. I'm so terribly sorry. I only wanted to help."

She looked up at me. I had expected her to be angry. I'd have preferred it.

"I know you did, my duck," she said. "You've not the build for it, that's all. Put that box down and take his feet."

* * *

We got him into the end loose box. It was dry in there and smelled of hay. We laid him down in deep straw, on his left side, and she fetched a horse rug from the place next door and spread it over him and tucked it in at the neck. She went back for a second one and rolled it up and put it under his head. Her hands were as gentle about it as if he had been four years old. I held the lamp she had lit, a stable lantern, and I could see her face, and I understood something I hadn't before. Mrs Pask had been at Compline since 1952. Edmund would have been ten.

He was breathing in the same way, slow, with the click. There was a little blood on the rug where the back of his head rested, and the lump on his forehead had not got any smaller.

"Oughtn't we to fetch Miss Quaile?" I said. "Really? Just to look at him?"

"And have her tell Mr Vane? No. I've said. He'll do." She got to her feet, with a hand on the manger. "I'll come out to him myself, after the ice is served, with a basin and a bit of lint. And again before I go to bed. He'll have a head on him in the morning that he'll remember. That's all it'll be."

"You're sure."

"I've known him forty-two years, Mr Toby. He's fallen off or out of or into everything on this island. He bounces." She took the lantern from me. In the doorway she stopped and looked back at the shape under the rug. "You've not to go telling this. Not to anyone. Not to that Mr Cade, nor Miss Imogen, nor your mother. He'd never hold his head up. A grown man, wheeled about in a barrow by the cook."

"I shan't say a word."

"See you don't."

I kept that promise for nine years. I told the story, as I've said. I told the barrow, and the pudding on his chest, because by then everyone concerned was past minding, and it got a laugh. I never told the mounting block. I never told anyone that I had taken the handle.

There was one other thing in the stables that night that I should mention. When she went into the next-door place for the rug, I followed her to the door with the lamp. It was a harness room. There was a wooden stair in the corner going up into the tower, and you could hear the big clock working overhead, very slowly, like someone sawing. And there had been some sort of a rough-house in there. A hurricane lamp was lying on the floor, smashed, in a pool of paraffin. There was a strong smell of whisky. On one of the rugs in the heap, when Mrs Pask lifted it, there was a patch of something dark and still wet, and I saw her look at it, and then look quickly at me, and take the rug underneath instead.

And leaning against the wall just inside the door was a cricket bat.

I thought, Edmund has been here already. I thought he had come here first, earlier in the evening, with a bottle, and knocked a lamp over and cut himself. Then he'd gone wandering off to the ice house and done it properly. I was sure of it. I could see the whole thing. It didn't occur to me that there might be more than one person bleeding in a stable at Compline on the same night. One hardly expects it.

* * *

We went back across the yard. She wheeled the empty barrow. I carried the box. The moon was fully out now, and the gravel was quite bright, and the house stood at the far side of it with the hall windows lit. I looked up at the tower again as we passed under it. The wooden man in his dinner jacket lifted his hammer, and the little bell went, three times over. *Ting-tang, ting-tang, ting-tang.* It made me jump.

"Quarter to," said Mrs Pask. "Lord, and I've not got the dish out."

In the kitchen, the clock on the wall said seven minutes to one. I've since been told that it was fast, and that Mrs Pask kept it so. I can only say what I saw. She washed her hands at the sink, for a long time, with a brush. I set the box down on the table.

"Not there," she said. "The slab, in the larder. It wants to be kept cold till the minute. And don't you go undoing it; you'll let the warm in. Where's my spoon?"

I told her about the spoon.

"And my saucer?"

I told her about the saucer.

"And my keys?"

I was silent.

"You've left my keys in that gate," said Mrs Pask. "My kitchen keys, that's never been off that nail in forty years but in my own hand." She breathed in. I waited. But she only dried her hands, and looked at the ceiling, as though she had heard something up there. It was a look I'd seen on Miss Quaile on the stairs. They all seemed to be listening for something above them in that house.

"I've a thing to see to," she said. "Upstairs. I'll not be five minutes. You put that in the larder and you sit down there and you don't touch nothing. When I come down I'll turn it out on the dish, and Gail can carry it in. And then I'm going out for my keys, and you're coming with me to hold the lamp."

She took a candle and went up the back stairs. I heard her going up, flight after flight. She was going a long way. The treads creaked right up to the top of the house.

I carried the box through to the pantry and put it on the marble slab, next to a ham. I shut the door on it. I gave the hatch of the dumb waiter a civil nod in passing. It didn't say anything.

Then I sat down at the kitchen table, as told, among thirteen glass plates. I looked at my hands. They were green from the steps, and there was straw on my trousers. On the front of my dress shirt, where his head had rested all the way up, there was a brown stain the size of a saucer.

I thought about my cousin Edmund. He was asleep under a horse rug, and snoring, with a lump on the front of his head that he had given himself, and a cut on the back that I had given him. I thought, well, at any rate he spoke. He said "well done."

I had no business to feel as cheerful as I did. But I'd been useful. There were two people lying down in comfort who might have been sitting up choking if I had not been by. And I had the pudding.

I should perhaps have wondered why it ticked.

It did, a little. I'd noticed it as I set the box down on the marble: a faint, irregular tick from inside, like a beetle in a wall. It went on for a few seconds and stopped. I assumed it was the ice settling.

## 4. The Fixed Point

I saw Toby Pargeter go into the ice house with a candle in a saucer. I heard him yell. I heard a spoon ring on brick.

I was in the trees. I waited for him to come out and shout for help. He did not come out. I waited what seemed a very long time, and by my watch was four minutes. Then he came through the gate at a run, without his candle, and went past me up the track toward the kitchen with his long legs going like a foal's.

He had left the gate open. The cook's keys were hanging in it.

I went in.

I did not want to. I had heard that fall. I stood on the landing with the torch in both hands, and it was shaking so that the light went all over the dome. Then I made it go down the steps.

He was at the bottom. He was on his side now, with his cheek on his arm, and somebody had pushed straw under his head. There was a candle burning on one of the steps. Beside him, tipped over in the wet, was the box.

He was making a noise. It was a long slow rasping breath with a click at the top.

I have told you about my mother. She had made that noise. Not at the end. Before the end, in the weeks when they gave her the strong medicine, she would sleep in the afternoons with her mouth open and breathe like that, and I would sit on the bed and read to her. I knew what it was. It was a person a long way down.

I went down the steps on my seat. I got my watch off. I held the glass under his nose.

It misted.

*1.26. Suspect No. 1 is NOT DEAD. I did the test. He is breathing slow like Mom did. He has a big lump on his head and its bleeding. The Old Man DID hit him. But not dead.*

*The Pink Man found him and put him on his side. That is the right thing. They did that with Mom.*

*I am staying with him till somebody comes.*

I sat on the bottom step. I did not touch him. I looked at his face. It was the first time I had seen it close. I had been calling him the villain since half past one that afternoon.

He did not look like one. He looked like a big tired man who had been hit. There was grey in his hair. He had long eyelashes, like a girl's, and one of his eyes was half open with the white showing, and the other was swelling shut.

He said something. I bent down.

"Ma," he said.

I said, "She isn't here."

I do not know why I answered him. He could not hear. But I had said the same word in my sleep for two years, and been told the same thing, and I thought that somebody ought to tell him.

There were voices outside, and a squeaking. I got behind the ice.

* * *

I watched them take him up. I was crouched behind a heap of wet sacking with my torch off.

It was the cook and the pink young man. I have read his account of it and I can tell you that he has left nothing out and added nothing. He went up those steps backwards on his seat, one at a time, with sixteen stone in his lap, and Mrs Pask pushed from below with her shoulder. On the seventh step the man they were carrying spoke. He said, "Is it over?" She said, "Nearly, Mr Edmund. You go back to sleep."

Toby came down again for the box. He looked for his spoon. He passed within a yard of me. He blew out his candle and left it on the step.

When they had gone I came out. I picked up the spoon. I do not know why. I put it down again in the straw. Mr Cade found it there twenty minutes later and held it up to a torch, and it made him think of a moneylender.

I followed the barrow.

It went up the track with the cook between the shafts, in a man's cap, leaning. Edmund lay in it on his back with his legs hanging over the front. He had the box on his stomach. His hands had been folded on top. I walked behind, in the dark, along the wall.

At the corner of the stables I put my head round. That is when Toby saw me. He has written it down: a small white face, at the height of his waist, there for two seconds. He put it with the lift.

I saw what happened at the mounting block.

He has told you. I can only confirm it. Mrs Pask was taking the barrow in a wide curve, slowly. He said, "Here, let me, do let me." He stepped in and took hold of one handle. The wheel went down between two stones.

I saw the barrow go over. I saw him let go and catch the box. He caught it beautifully.

I saw Edmund Vane go out on the other side, head first, and I heard the back of his head meet the corner of the stone.

I put my hand over my own mouth. I had made one noise that night already.

And he said, "Ow."

I want to tell Toby this, and I have told him. I was ten yards away. I heard what the man said, and how. It was loud, and cross. He put his hand up to his head. He said a bad word. He opened both his eyes and looked at the young man standing over him against the moon and asked who it was, and what he was holding. When he was told that it was the pudding, he said, "Good. Well done." Then he turned over on the cobbles of his own accord and went back to sleep.

A person who has been killed by a blow to the head does not do that. I am not a doctor. But I have since asked two, with the pathologist's report in front of them, and they both say the same. It was a contusion. It bled. It did not break the bone. The fracture was on the other side, at the front, and it had been there for forty-five minutes already when the barrow tipped.

Toby Pargeter has believed for thirty years that he gave his cousin the injury that sent him dizzy into the sea. He called a book after it. He did give him a cut on the back of the head, by trying to help. He also heard him snore, and put him on his side, and carried him up twelve steps out of a place that would have killed him by three. If you set those two things in a balance, I do not think that it comes down on the side he supposes.

* * *

They put him in the end loose box and covered him. Mrs Pask tucked the rug in at his neck. I watched her through the gap in the door. I had not known that her hands could be like that.

They went. I heard the little bell on the tower ring three times as they crossed the yard. It was a quarter to two by my watch.

I went in.

It was warm in there, after the ice house, and it smelled of hay. They had left no light. I sat down in the straw beside him with the torch between my knees, pointing at the wall.

I do not know what I thought I was doing. I had said that I would stay with him till somebody came, and somebody had come, and gone again. Mrs Pask had told me twice to get into the nursery bed. I could not have gone back up to that room for anything in the world.

I think that it was only that he was asleep, and hurt, and by himself. I had sat by somebody like that before.

I got out the notebook.

*1.48. They have put him in a stable in the straw. The cook and the Pink Man. In a WHEELBARROW. The Pink Man dropped him on his head by accident but he woke up and said ow so its OK.*

*I am with him. I am gaurding.*

*CASE NOTES. I have to think.*

*The Old Man hit him. But the Old Man is not bad. He looked lost.*

*The Big Man took the clock. But he said sorry Ma. And he was sad in the libary.*

*The fair lady*

That is as far as I got. He moved.

He drew his knees up, and groaned, and put a hand to his forehead. He lay still. Then he opened his eyes, both of them as far as they would go, and looked straight up into the dark.

"Where the devil am I?" he said.

I had the torch on the wall. He turned his head toward the light. He saw me.

He looked for quite a long time. I sat perfectly still, with the notebook on my knee.

"Am I dead?" he said.

"No."

"Are you sure?"

"I did a test. With my watch. Twice."

"Did you?" He seemed to think that over. His voice was thick and slow. "That was thorough. What are you? Are you a ghost?"

"No."

"You're in the nursery. I've seen you there. You were on Dapple."

"I was this afternoon. How did you see?"

"I didn't. I mean a long time ago." He shut his eyes and opened them. "No. That was Immy. You're not Imogen."

"I'm Wren."

"Wren." He said it carefully. "That's a bird."

"It's after my mom's mom."

"Is it? I'm after nobody. I was supposed to be after my father, and it didn't take." He was trying to get up on his elbow. He got halfway and stopped, and went grey. "Oh, Lord. Somebody's hit me."

"The old man did. With a thing on a string. I saw."

He looked at me.

"You saw that."

"I was outside. I heard you fall. I counted the steps."

"How many?"

"Twelve."

"That's right," said Edmund Vane. "There are twelve. I counted them too, on the way down. I remember thinking, that's the lot." He put his fingers to the back of his head and looked at them. "And I've another at the back. Did he hit me twice?"

"No. That was the Pink Man. He tipped you out of the wheelbarrow. He didn't mean to. He caught the pudding instead."

He stared. Then his face did something. It began at the corners of his mouth. I saw that he was laughing, without any sound, and that it hurt him.

"The pudding," he said. "Oh, my God. Toby. With the—I was holding a church." He wiped his good eye. "Whose are you, Wren? You're not one of ours. You talk like the pictures."

"I'm not allowed to say."

"Aren't you? Who says so?"

"I'm not allowed to say that either."

"I see. You've been well trained. You'd do for this family." He had got himself sitting, with his back against the manger. He was shivering. "What time is it? Have you really a watch? Nobody's got a watch in this house."

I held it up with the light on.

"Ten to two."

"Is that by us, or by the world?"

"It's the real time. I set it by the radio. All your clocks are an hour wrong."

"So they are," he said. "So they are. You've noticed that. It's taken some people years." He shut his eyes. I thought that he was going to sleep again. Then they came open, quite wide. "Ten to two. The real time. *God.* The boat."

* * *

He got up. I do not know how. He hauled himself up the manger hand over hand and stood holding it, with his head hanging and his breath coming hard.

"I've got to tell the boat to go," he said. "It's waiting. I was to bring the box. If I don't come they'll walk up to the house, and my father's standing at the door." He took a step, and his knee went, and he caught the wall. "A light. From the tower. That was the arrangement, if it was off. Three swings."

"You can't. You're hurt. You've had medicine in your drink; the nurse said."

"How do you know what the nurse said?"

"I was in the lift."

"Of course you were." He was working his way along the wall to the door. "Listen, Wren-after-your-grandmother. I've done a great many stupid things tonight, and I'm about to do another, and I shall very likely fall off the ladder. You needn't watch. Go back to wherever it is that you're not allowed to say."

I went after him. I had said that I would.

He got into the harness room. He stopped. There was the broken lamp, and the smell.

"Somebody's had a party in here," he said.

"The piano boy hit the detective with a bat. He's not dead either. I did the test."

"Kit did?" He turned and looked at me in the torchlight. "Kit hit that policeman? What for?"

"He thought he'd come to hurt you."

He stood there. I could not see his face well.

"Did he," he said. It was very low. "The young fool. For me." He took a lantern down off a nail, and got a match to it, with hands that would hardly do it. "I must speak to that boy. I've not spoken to him properly since he was ten."

The ladder goes up in the corner. He put his foot on the bottom rung and his hands on the sides, and nothing happened. He could not make the other foot come.

I got underneath. I took hold of his ankle with both hands and lifted it onto the next rung.

"What are you doing?"

"Helping."

"I'm sixteen stone."

"I'm only doing your foot."

We went up like that. He pulled with his arms and I moved his feet, one and then the other, fourteen times. He says, in what he told Toby afterward, that he got up those stairs on his hands and knees. That is nearly right. He had forgotten whose hands.

* * *

The clock room is a wooden box at the top of the tower, with a great iron frame in the middle and a pendulum as long as a man swinging through a slot in the floor. It was bitterly cold. The moon came in through the slats.

*It goes TOCK. Then nothing for ages. Then TOCK. It is the biggest clock I have ever seen. You can see all the wheels.*

*There is a cubbord in the corner with 2 gold rods coming out of the side going to the big clock. The door is not quite shut. I looked.*

*THERE IS ANOTHER ONE.*

*The same as the one he put in the box. The same faces. The same moon. EXACTLY.*

*There are 2 big clocks. Like there are 2 green dresses. He has stolen one and the other is UP HERE.*

*Which is the real one?*

I wrote that by the light of his lantern, sitting on the floor, while he got his breath. It is the last thing in the notebook that is written in sentences.

I did not tell him. I have gone over that. He was six feet from the Thorne Sidereal, the real one, with its door ajar, and he never turned his head. He had not looked at a clock in twenty years. I could have said, *There's another one in that cupboard.* He would have gone and put his glass on it, and seen the thorn. He would have known that the box in the ice house was not worth the price of the boat.

I was nine, and I was not sure what I was looking at, and he had other things on his mind.

He opened the small door in the wall. The night came in.

There is a ledge out there, a few feet wide, with a low parapet, and a little roof over. The bell hangs in the middle. On the far side of it stood the wooden lady, as tall as he was, grey, with her arm up. On the near side, close to the door, with his back to us, was the wooden man.

Somebody had dressed him. He had a black jacket on, buttoned, and a white front, and a bow. He had a hammer in his fist.

Edmund went out past him with the lantern. He held on to the wooden man's shoulder to do it, as you would hold on to a friend. He stood at the parapet, between the Jack and the bell, and lifted the light, and swung it.

I stayed in the doorway. I could see over the edge. A long way off, down by the water, there was a small white light, and two people standing.

"There," he said. "That's Ostler. He's seen it. Who's that with him?" He leaned out. "That's not—who the devil is that?" He swung the lantern again. "Go on, man. Go home. It's off."

I looked at my watch. It was two minutes to two.

And a voice came up out of the yard.

* * *

"Vane."

It was right underneath. It was not loud. Then it was.

"VANE."

I knew it before the second one had finished. I would have known it at the bottom of the sea.

It was my dad.

I cannot tell you what that was like. I had left him at Saltcote at half past seven in the morning, shoeing a mare. There was six feet of water between that farm and this tower. I had seen it out of the nursery window. He could not be there. He had made me promise about the road, and he never broke a rule that he made.

I very nearly shouted. I had my mouth open. I was going to lean over that parapet and yell, *Dad, I'm up here.*

I did not. I have asked myself why a thousand times. I think it was two things. One was the promise. I was in the last place on earth that I was supposed to be, and he was underneath, and I knew how he would look at me. And the other was his voice. I had never heard it like that. He had never in my life spoken above the level of a man talking to a horse. I did not know that he could make that sound. There was something in it that kept me where I was.

Edmund had turned. He brought the lantern round, and the light fell down the tower.

"Who's that?"

"Garrow."

"Who?"

"Tom Garrow. From Saltcote. You looked at me Thursday through a glass."

I heard Edmund laugh. It was short and broken. He asked how he had got over. My father said that he had swum it.

"You swam the Rill? At the flood? Then you're a bigger fool than I am." He was holding on to the wooden man's arm. He turned his head, and he was not looking at me, or at the Jack. He was looking across the bell at the grey lady. "He's swum the Rill, Ma. Did you hear that? Old Amos's boy. He's come for his rent."

He was talking to her. He was drugged, and had been hit twice on the head, and he had climbed forty feet. He looked at a figure of painted oak in a wet shawl, and he saw his mother.

My father has told you what he said next, and I can tell you that he has it word for word. He said that he had a thing to ask. He said that it was from his father, who was dead, and had held on a week to give it him.

"Not now," Edmund said. "Not *now*, man. Can't you see I'm busy? I've a boat." And then, louder, in a voice that frightened me: "Get off my land. You've no land. Fenn's sent the paper. Go home to your bloody sheep."

He laughed. My father says that it rang down the tower. It did. I was beside him. I could see his face, in the lantern, and I can tell you what my father could not see from the yard. He was not laughing at anyone. He was hanging on to a wooden man with one hand, forty feet up, with his eyes rolling. I do not think that he knew what he was saying. He was frightened out of his wits and he made the noise that he always made.

Then my father asked his question.

"Was she coming or was she going?"

I heard it. I did not understand it. I heard him say that my grandfather had stood on our wall with a lamp, and seen her light come three parts over, and another go after. That he had stood an hour. That hers had turned back.

"Did she turn of herself? Or did you turn her?"

Edmund stopped laughing.

I was looking straight at him. I shall never forget it. Everything went out of his face. The drink, and the drug, and the fear. He looked down into that yard at a man standing in the light with a gun in his hands, and I saw him understand who it was, and what he was being asked, and that he had been waiting twenty years for it.

"You tell me," my father said. "You'll tell me now. I've come through the sea for it."

Edmund's mouth opened.

He was going to answer. I am as sure of that as I am of anything. He had it. He had told a nurse that afternoon that he would say it in the morning. Somebody had come across the water at night and asked him to his face, which no one had ever done, and he was going to tell a Garrow, there, from the top of his own tower, what had become of Marguerite Vane.

He said one word. I was a yard from him. I heard it. Nobody else could have.

He said, "Both."

And the clock struck.

* * *

There was a whirring in the room behind me, and a clank. The floor shook. Over my head a rod moved.

The wooden man lifted his arm.

I had not known that he could. I had drawn him that afternoon from a window, hitting a bell, and it had not come into my mind that he would do it while I was standing next to him. His whole arm came up from the shoulder, stiff, with the hammer in the fist. The black sleeve rode back from a white cuff. It came up, and over, and down.

*Ting.* And on the other side, a smaller hammer on a wire. *Tang.*

He swung out as he struck. That is how he is made. He stands on an iron pin, and when the rod pulls he turns a little toward the bell, and leans, and his arm comes down past the edge of the roof. From the yard, with a lantern behind him, he would have looked like a man stepping forward to the parapet and levelling something at whoever stood below.

He did it four times. Edmund had let go of him and was holding the bell frame. They were shoulder to shoulder, the two of them, in their dinner jackets. The light was swinging.

Then the lady moved.

She was on the far side. I saw her arm go back. It went a long way back, slowly. It came down.

The big bell went off like the end of the world.

I had my hands over my ears. I did not hear the gun. I saw it. There was a red flash down in the yard, and another, so close together that they were nearly one.

The wooden man jumped. That is the only way I can put it. Something hit him in the chest, and his white front burst open, and there were splinters. He rocked on his pin. There was a crack. He went over backward, quite slowly, with his arm still up, and his black jacket, and he fell off the far side of the tower into the dark.

I heard him hit the water.

In the same instant Edmund cried out and clapped his hand to his neck. The lantern flew out of his fingers. It went over the parapet. He came backward through the little door as if he had been thrown, and landed on me.

We went down together on the floor of the clock room. Something whined over us and smacked into the cupboard in the corner, a lot of small things at once, like hail on a tin roof. There was a twang. One of the gold rods jumped and hung loose.

Then it was quiet. The pendulum went on. *Tock.*

* * *

He was lying across my legs. He was very heavy.

"Are you hit?" he said.

"No."

"Are you sure? Feel. All over."

I felt. "I'm not."

"Thank Christ." He rolled off me. He was holding the side of his neck, and something dark was coming between his fingers. "I am, I think. A little. He's given me the edge of it." He took his hand away and looked at it by the moon. "Oh, well done, Garrow. That's what I get for standing next to a better-dressed man."

I got my handkerchief out. It was one that my mother had bought me, in Lethbridge, with red maple leaves on it. I had been keeping it clean. I folded it and put it against his neck, where the wet was, and held it there.

"Press," I said. "You have to press. For ten minutes."

"Who taught you that?"

"The nurse at the hospital. When Mom had the tube out."

He put his own hand over mine. It was a big hand, and cold, and it was shaking.

"You're a useful sort of person to have about," he said. "Wren." He lay back on the boards. "Did you see what he shot?"

"The wooden man."

"The Jack. In my father's dinner jacket. Both barrels. He's gone into the fleet." He began to laugh, and stopped, because of his neck. "He thinks it was me. He'll have seen it go over. He's standing down there now thinking he's done murder."

I said nothing.

He turned his head on the floor and looked at me. The moon was in bars across his face. One eye was shut tight and the other was very bright.

"Who is that, down there?" he said. "You know. I saw you, when he called. You nearly went over the edge."

I did not answer.

"You're not allowed to say."

"No."

"You talk like the pictures. And Tom Garrow's been twenty years in Canada." He was quiet. "Dolly Pask's been taking plates up the back stairs all day for a cat. We haven't got a cat."

I held the handkerchief.

"You're Garrow's child," Edmund Vane said. "You're old Amos's granddaughter. In my nursery. On my rocking horse." He shut his eye. "Oh, Dolly. You old fox."

"She'll lose her place."

"She will not. Not while I've breath." He was silent for a moment. "That's your father."

"Yes."

"Does he know you're here?"

"No. He made me promise. He'll kill me."

"He's just had a fair try at me." He was nearly smiling. "Don't cry. He won't. I know that sort. He carried a ewe on his back once, when he was a boy, a mile and a half, with a broken leg; I watched him from the wall. I thought, that's a better man than I'll ever be." He moved his hand on mine. "Listen. I ought to put my head over that parapet and shout down that I'm alive. But I can't stand, just at present. And if he sees a face come up over the edge, after what he thinks he's done, he'll either drop dead or let off the other barrel. And you're up here."

"He's only got two."

"So he has. You do count things." He breathed. "Let him think it for an hour. It'll do him no lasting harm. It'll keep him out of the house, which is the main thing; my father's in there, and my sister. I'll go down directly and find him. I owe him an answer. He asked me a fair question and I'd got as far as one word."

"You said both."

"Did I? Did you hear that?" He looked at me with something like respect. "Yes. Both. She was coming, and she was going. I'll tell him. I'll tell him the whole of it, as soon as I can walk. I've said that to three people today. It gets easier."

We stayed there. I held his neck. I did not have a watch on; I had put it in my pocket after the test. I counted instead. I had got to four hundred and something when I heard the music.

It came up from the house, very thin. It was a waltz.

"That's my father," Edmund said. "He's dancing. He does, in the Hour. With nobody." He listened. "No. Not with nobody. Somebody's come down for him. Who'd have the nerve?"

He did not know. I did not either. I know now. My father was standing in the yard, underneath us, at that minute, with an empty gun, waiting to be taken. In the hall a woman in grey silk was being told the tide. And forty feet up, a man with shot in his neck lay on the floor with his head in a child's lap and listened to "Gold and Silver."

I must have slept. I was nine, and it was half past two in the morning. I do not remember lying down.

* * *

I was woken by his hand over my mouth.

"Hush. Somebody's on the ladder."

He had me up, and across the room. There is a closet there where the weights go down, with a slatted door. He put me in it. There was just room, standing, beside a thing like a sash-weight as big as I was.

"Not a sound," he whispered. "Whatever you hear. It's my father. He's not to see you; he'd think that he'd gone mad, and he's near enough already." He shut the door.

I looked through the slats.

A candle came up through the floor. Under it there was white hair, and a long face, and a magnifying glass swinging on a string. It was the old man. He climbed up into the room as slowly as a spider. He stood, and held the candle out, and looked at the cupboard with its broken rod.

Then Edmund said, "Who's that?"

I am not going to give you what was said in that room. The old man would not set it down, and he was right. It was said in their Hour, and it was theirs. I heard all of it. I was standing two yards from them, in the dark, with my hand over my own mouth. I understood perhaps a third.

I can tell you what I saw.

I saw a man of eighty-one go down on his knees in front of his son, and put a hand on his face, and keep it there. I saw him count something at the angle of the jaw, with his lips moving.

I heard him explain about an hour that had been given back. I heard him say that he had killed his son and then had not. And I watched Edmund's face while he listened. He did not laugh. He looked as I think I must have looked when my father told me that the prairie was like the sea, only it didn't move. He knew that it was not so, and he saw that it was being said to him by someone who loved him, in the only words that person had.

He said, "All right, Father. I was dead, and now I'm not. I'll not argue."

Then he told the old man what had happened on the Wade.

It took about two minutes. I have said that I understood a third. I understood the box on legs, since my father had shown it me. I understood that somebody had been put up the ladder, and that somebody had not. I did not then understand who.

I heard the old man say, "Then she didn't leave me." He said it six times. I counted.

And I heard him say the thing about the wheels. He said that the ones Edmund had made were better than the ones the first man had made. He said that he had known it on the day, and had said that they would serve.

Edmund put his arm over his eyes.

That is when I sneezed. There was twenty years of dust in that closet. I had been holding it for a long time.

The old man said, "What is that?"

"Rats," said Edmund.

He looked straight at the slats when he said it. I saw his one eye. I have never before or since been lied for so well.

The old man gave him something small, out of his waistcoat, on a ribbon. He told him to put it in his coat pocket and not the waistcoat. I saw Edmund put it away. It was a key.

There was a voice from below then, a woman's, calling up. Edmund put his finger to his lips. He would not let his father say that he was there. I thought it strange. I had watched that nurse all day; I knew how she looked at him. I thought that she would have been glad.

He told me why, afterward, on the ladder. He said, "She'd have had me in bed. And she'd have found you. And then there'd have been questions, and Dolly out of a place, and your father brought up to the house with every Vane in it looking at him. I'd one night left to do some things. I couldn't spend it having my temperature taken."

The old man went down. I heard her take him away across the yard.

And right beside my head, on the other side of the wall, the lady brought down her arm.

It struck twice. I thought that my skull would split. There was no little bell before it. There was nobody now on the left of the bell to ring one.

When the sound had died, the slatted door opened.

"Come out, rat," Edmund said.

I came out. I had dust all over me. He was standing, holding on to the iron frame, and his face was wet.

"You heard that," he said.

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Yes."

"Did you understand it?"

"Some."

"That's about what I'd have said myself." He let out his breath. "Well. Keep hold of the part you did. I may want you to remind me. I've told it once now, and I didn't die of it, which surprises me." He put his hand in his coat pocket and felt the key there. "He said they were better than Thorne's. Did you hear him?"

"I heard."

"Thirty-nine years I've waited for that. Since I was thirteen, and played the Schubert." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "Come on. Help me with my feet. There's cold goose in the kitchen, and I've a sister to find. And you, madam, are on the wrong side of six feet of water from your bed."

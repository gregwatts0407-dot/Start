## II. The Watches

I had three weeks of that life before the night came, and I am to set them down, Dr Imrie says, although nothing happened in them. I have told him that a great deal happened. It was only that none of it could be seen.

My days went like this. I left Mr Vane at six, when Gail came up with his tea, and went to my bed in the little room next door and slept with the curtains open until the light was strong. At noon I got up. In the afternoons I sat in the morning room on the garden side of the house, at a desk that had been his wife's, and sorted her papers into heaps. At ten I gave him his draught and took my chair by the fire. Between one and two in the morning, on most nights, he woke, and then we walked.

I came to know the house in the dark better than I ever knew it by day. He went where he pleased and I went with him, a pace behind, with the lamp. We went down to the Shop, where he would stand at his bench and pick up a tool and look at it, and lay it down exactly where it had been. We went along the long gallery. We went across to the tower every night to wind the great clock, and I learned to stand with my back to the door that opened on the leads.

He talked. I think that he had not been listened to for some years, except as one listens to a child, and when he found that I answered what he had said and not what was convenient, he opened to me like a book that has been long shut. He told me about the going of clocks. I did not understand a tenth of it. But I understood, after a time, what it was to him. He believed that there was a true time, which was the sun's, and that a good clock was a kind of honest witness to it, and that a man who kept one in order was doing a small right thing in the world, every day, by which others might set themselves.

"And then they passed the Act," he said to me, one night, on the stairs. "In the year 'sixteen, when I was three years old. They said the clocks should all be moved an hour forward in the spring, and so they were, and so they have been ever since. Do you understand what that is? It is a lie agreed upon. The sun stands where he stood. Noon is when he crosses the meridian of the place; that is what the word means. And forty million people look at their watches and say it is one o'clock."

"It gives them lighter evenings, Mr Vane."

"It gives them nothing. There is exactly as much light as before. They have only chosen to get up earlier and to call it something else, so as not to notice." He was quite calm about it; he had been angry for so long that it had worn smooth. "I put up with it for fifty-eight years. I changed the clocks in this house every spring and every autumn, like a good citizen, and I made a ceremony of the autumn for my wife's sake, because she liked a party. And on the night of the autumn change in 1974 it killed her."

"How did it kill her?"

"She went by the clock in the hall. She thought she had an hour more than she had." He stopped, with his hand on the rail. "I had put them back early. Before dinner. It is a long business, a hundred and eleven clocks, and I had always done it at the Hour, with the family, and that year I was tired, and I thought, I will get it over. So that when she looked at the hall clock and it said two, the world's clocks said three, and the tide goes by the world. She walked out onto the Wade with a quarter of an hour in hand, believing that she had an hour and a quarter. And I had done it. With these hands. To save myself a scramble."

"You couldn't have known that she would go out."

"No. I did not know that. I didn't know why she went, for a long time. They kept it from me. And then I did know, and then I forgot again, and now I'm not perfectly sure." He looked down at me, and his face was puzzled and gentle. "I think that she was going to someone. I think that is what I knew. Was she?"

"I can't tell you, Mr Vane. I never heard."

"No. You weren't here. I must ask Edmund. Edmund will know; he was always about, on that night." He went on down the stairs. "I have never changed a clock in this house since. Not one. I told them that the Act might do as it liked on the other side of the water, but that at Compline we should keep the hour that killed her, since it was the true one. And I've owed her the other ever since."

"Owed her?"

"The hour. The one I took. A man who takes an hour from his wife owes it to her; that is simple bookkeeping. I've been twenty years thinking how it was to be paid." He nodded to himself, in the dark. "I believe that I've found a way. I shall show you, one night. It wants only the occasion."

I thought it was his illness speaking. I had been told that he was confused. I was to learn that he was never less confused than when he talked like that, and that he meant every word precisely.

* * *

It was in the long gallery that he liked best to walk, and it was there, on my second night, that he took me to see her.

The gallery runs the whole length of the first floor on the west side, a hundred feet or more, with windows along one wall that look toward the Wade. It is panelled, and the floor is of wide boards that have sunk in the middle, so that one walks in a kind of shallow trough. There are clocks at intervals. At the far end, on the end wall, facing down the length of it, hangs the portrait.

I had seen it by day and had thought it a good picture of a handsome woman. I had not seen it by a single lamp, coming toward it from a hundred feet away, with an old man on my arm who was saying, "She will be so glad that you have come."

One sees the dress first. It is grey silk, and whoever painted it loved to paint silk; it has a light of its own. It comes out of the dark before the face does. Then the bare shoulders, and one hand resting on a chair-back, and last of all the face, which is not beautiful and is very much alive. The mouth is a little open. The eyes are grey, like the gown, and they are looking at you. I know that it is said of every portrait. I can only report that I moved from one side of the gallery to the other, on the pretext of trimming the lamp, and that I did not get away from them.

"There," said Mr Vane. "Margot, this is Miss Quaile. I told you of her."

He stood before the picture with his hands behind him. I stood a little to one side. Beneath the frame a tall clock was going, the one with the cracked voice. It was the only sound.

"She is laughing at me," he said presently. "You see it? She had just said something. I was in the room, and I had told the painter that he was not to make her look like a duchess, and she said, 'Leo wants me to look like something that keeps good time.' And the fellow caught it. I have never known how. He was not a clever man in any other way."

"She looks very kind."

"She was not especially kind. She was amused. It is a different thing, and wears better." He put out a hand and touched the bottom of the frame, lightly. "She was amused by me for thirty years. I don't think that I ever once amused anybody else. I used to hear her laugh in another part of the house and know that I had done something, or had been reported as doing it. I didn't mind. I found that I rather missed it, when it stopped."

"When did it stop?"

He considered. "Some while before the end. A year. Two. She was still kind to me then. That was how I knew."

I looked up at the face. I have said that I do not hold with the kind of book in which the heroine feels things. But I was a nurse, and I had been trained to look at faces for what they would not say. I thought, looking at Marguerite Vane, that she was a woman who had been waiting for a long time for someone to ask her a question. Nobody had. She had gone on being amusing in the meantime.

I thought too, and I am ashamed of it, that she was looking at me as one looks at a girl who has come to take a place in the household, to see how she would do.

We went back along the gallery. At the door that was shut he stopped, as he did every night, and laid his palm flat against the wood.

"She is asleep," he would say. Or, "She's not come up yet." Or once, on a bad night, "Why has she locked it? What have I done?" And on that night I said, "Mr Vane, she is dead," and he leaned his forehead on the panel and wept, and afterwards thanked me.

I never passed that door, in those three weeks, without feeling that there was someone on the other side of it who knew I was there.

* * *

I must write about Mr Edmund Vane. Dr Imrie says I have been going round it.

I have said that he looked at me across the table on my first evening, and that I was angry at being seen. I told myself that it was nothing, and that he was a man who had looked at a great many women. I daresay that was so. He was fifty-two. He had a wife. He drank from the middle of the morning and was never, in the time that I knew him, what could be called sober, though he was also never what could be called drunk. He carried it as he carried his bad leg, with a kind of impatient ease. Everyone in the house except his father spoke of him in the same tone, half exasperated and half protective, as one speaks of a large dog that has killed a hen.

He came into the morning room on the fourth or fifth afternoon. I was at the desk with a box of letters. He stood in the doorway with his glass.

"So you're the archivist," he said. "What are you finding?"

"Bills, mostly. Seed catalogues. Mrs Vane kept everything."

"She did. She kept every bus ticket. She said she liked to know where she'd been." He came across and looked over my shoulder. I could smell the whisky, and tobacco, and under those something clean, like cold air. He picked a photograph out of the heap. It was of a small boy on a pony, scowling. "Good God. That's me. I was eight. That animal bit me a minute later, and she laughed so much she dropped the camera."

"You look very fierce."

"I was frightened out of my wits. I've always looked fierce when I'm frightened; it has got me a reputation I haven't earned." He put it down. He looked at the heaps I had made, each with its label, and at my hands. "You're a neat creature. You do that the way my father does a movement. Everything laid out in the order it came off."

"It's how I was taught to do a dressing trolley."

"You were a hospital nurse, Fenn says. Why did you stop?"

I had been asked that by Mr Fenn, and by the agency, and I had an answer that I gave, about wishing for a change. I opened my mouth to give it. I do not know what happened. I think it was only that he had asked, and stood waiting, as though he would be interested in whatever I said.

"A patient died," I said. "In the night. I'd fallen asleep."

He did not say that he was sorry, or that I must not blame myself. He put his glass down on the corner of the desk.

"How long were you asleep?"

"An hour and twenty minutes."

"And she'd rung for you."

"She'd tried to."

"Did you tell them? That you'd slept?"

"I told them all. I told the night sister, and the matron, and the inquiry. I told her daughter."

"What did the daughter say?"

"She said, 'You poor girl.'" I had never told anyone that part. "I think I could have borne it if she had hit me."

He was silent. He had gone to the window. It looks out on what was once a garden, and beyond it the old glasshouse they call the orangery, with half its panes gone and a vine grown through the roof.

"Then you're a better man than I am, Miss Quaile," he said. "You told them."

I did not understand him. I said, "What did you do?"

He laughed. It was not a happy sound. "I? I woke up. That was all I ever did. I woke up, and it was getting light, and I walked home." He turned round and took up his glass again, and he was the large careless person that everyone knew. "Don't mind me. I talk nonsense in October. It's the equinox. Go on with your bus tickets. And Miss Quaile."

"Yes, Mr Vane?"

"Don't get fond of us. We're not a good investment. Ask my wife."

He went out. I sat with my hands in my lap. After a time I found that I had been looking at the photograph of the scowling child for some minutes. I put it in the heap that was labelled *Photographs, family, before 1955*.

I did get fond. I shall say it once and plainly and then leave it. I had not meant to. I was twenty-six, and plain, and had never been much noticed by anyone, and he had asked me a true question and listened to a true answer. I do not think it takes more than that. He never touched me, except once, by accident, at the dinner table on the last night, when his sleeve brushed my wrist. He never said a word to me that his wife might not have heard. But when he came into a room I knew it with my back turned. I found that I was listening, all day, among the hundred and eleven clocks, for the sound of an uneven step.

I have wondered whether she knew. The woman in the gallery. I used to feel, going past, that the amusement in her face had a different quality on some nights. I thought that she was saying to me, *Yes. I know. He was mine first, and I did him no good either.*

* * *

There was a second time, and it was at night.

It would have been the twelfth or thirteenth of the month. Mr Vane had slept through, as he did perhaps one night in four, and at three in the morning I went down to the kitchen to make myself tea. I was coming back through the hall with the cup when I saw that the library door was open. There was no light inside. But there was the smell of a cigarette.

I should have gone on up the stairs. I stood at the door instead.

He was sitting in a chair that he had drawn up to the table in the window. There was a little moon. On the table stood the great clock under its glass, the one they call the Thorne, which is the chief treasure of the house; I had been shown it and told what it was worth. Someone had taken off the cloth that covered it at night. The moon was on its face. All its small hands were going about, and he was sitting in front of it with his elbows on his knees, watching, as a man might sit and watch a fire.

"Come in, Miss Quaile," he said, without turning. "I knew your step. You walk like someone trying not to wake a ward."

I came in a little way. "I didn't know anyone was up."

"I'm always up, this month. I haven't slept through an October night in twenty years. I walk about. I sit. Sometimes I come and look at this." He nodded at the clock. "Do you know what it does?"

"It tells the tide."

"It tells everything. That's what he was after, old Thorne. He wanted one machine that would say where the sun was, and where the stars were, and the moon, and how high the water stood on the bar out there, all at once and for ever, with no one to touch it. And he very nearly did it. It's the cleverest thing I've ever seen made by a man. I helped my father put it together, the last two years. I was sixteen. I cut the moon's train." He drew on his cigarette, and I saw his face for a moment in the glow. "I've hated it ever since I was thirty-two."

"Why?"

"Because it knew. That night. It was standing here in this window, saying the tide to the minute. High water such a time. The Wade shut from such a time. It had it right. It always has it right. And nobody came and looked at it." He let the smoke go. "She did, I think. Mother. She was the only one who ever really used it; she said it was the one clock in the house that told you something you needed to know. She'd have looked. She knew exactly how long she had."

I stood very still. I was holding the cup in both hands.

"Your father thinks that she was misled by the hall clock," I said. "He thinks that he had put it back too soon and that she was an hour out."

"I know what my father thinks."

"Is it not true?"

"He did put them back early. That part's true." He was quiet for so long that I thought he would say no more. "It's a comfortable thing to believe, isn't it? That she made a mistake about the time. It's nobody's fault but a clock's. I've let him believe it for twenty years. He's built his whole life on it since; he's turned this house into a monument to it. And every one of these damned things that ticks is telling him, all day, it was the hour, Leo, it was the hour."

"And it wasn't?"

He turned his head then. I could not see his eyes.

"You're a truthful girl," he said. "My father says so. He says you won't lie to him. It's rather hard on the rest of us; we've got out of the way of it." He stubbed out the cigarette. "I'll tell you this much, since you told me yours. My mother never made a mistake about a tide in her life. She was born on this coast. She could tell you the state of the water by the smell. Whatever happened to her on the Wade that night, it wasn't arithmetic."

"Then what was it?"

"I woke up," Edmund Vane said. "I told you. And it was light."

He stood, heavily, and took the green cloth from the chair-back and laid it over the glass case. The little moving hands went out. At the door he paused beside me. He was a head and a half taller than I am. He looked down, and for a moment I thought that he was going to say something else. I do not know what I should have done if he had.

"Go and drink your tea, Miss Quaile," he said. "It'll be cold. Everything in this house gets cold while one's thinking about it."

He went away across the hall. I heard his step on the stairs, the one and then the other.

I did not sleep that day at all. I lay on my bed with the curtains open and watched the light move on the ceiling. He had told me that he had hidden something for twenty years, and that it was to do with his mother's death, and that he had been asleep when he should have been awake. He had told me as one tells a fellow sufferer. I should have felt the weight of it. What I felt, God forgive me, was that there were now two of us in the house.

* * *

On the night of the fifteenth, Mr Vane showed me how the debt was to be paid.

He woke at one. He was very clear that night, and brisk, and in a hurry. He would not walk in the gallery. He said that we must go across at once, since there was work to do and only a week to do it in, and he had me carry a canvas roll of tools that he took from the Shop.

We climbed to the clock chamber in the tower. It was a still night, with frost. He did not go to the winding handle. He went to a cupboard built into the corner of the chamber, a tall narrow one of old deal that I had taken for part of the wall, and he opened it with a key from his dressing-gown pocket.

"Hold the light there," he said.

There was something standing in the cupboard, on a shelf at the height of his chest, with a cloth over it. He drew the cloth away.

It was a clock. I thought for an instant that it was the Thorne itself, and that he had carried it across in the night. It had the same broad white dial and the same ring of small ones, and the same painted moon. But I had seen the Thorne under its glass in the library not six hours before, when I went through to fetch a book. So I knew that this must be the other. I had been told that there were copies. Gail had said that he had made a twin to the great clock, years ago, to teach himself how it went, and that it lived in a cupboard somewhere.

This one was going. I could hear it, under the slow saw of the tower works. And it was joined to them. From its back there ran two thin rods of bright new brass, through holes bored in the side of the cupboard, across a yard of air, to the iron frame of the great clock, where they ended in a little cage of wheels that had been clamped to one of the arbors. The brass was clean and yellow. Everything else in that chamber was two hundred years old and black. It looked like new growth on a dead tree.

"There," he said. He was breathing quickly. "I began it in September. I've done the most of it at night, since they think I am asleep. It's nearly complete. I have only to cut the last pinion, and set her to the year."

"What is it, Mr Vane?"

"I call it the Reconciler. It is a differential, of a kind. You'll not follow the train, and you needn't." He touched one of the rods with a fingertip, and it sang, faintly. "You know what happens on the night. At one o'clock by this house, the world puts its clocks back. It has run on for seven months an hour ahead of the truth, and it comes back, all at once, and agrees with us. It is two o'clock out there, and then it is one. Do you see? There's an hour that they have had, and now they are to have it again."

"Yes."

"Where does it come from?" He looked at me as a teacher looks at a slow pupil. "An hour is a real thing. It cannot be made from nothing. In March they take one, and say that it did not happen. In October they are given one back. It must be kept somewhere in between. I have thought about this for twenty years. It is kept, Miss Quaile. It is held over. And on the night, at the stroke, it is paid out again to everyone in England, and they sleep through it. They waste it. They turn over in their beds."

I said nothing. The frost was coming through the louvres. Out on the leads, a yard from the cupboard, behind the shut door, I knew that the grey woman was standing.

"I do not intend to waste mine," he said. "This house has never taken the false hour. So when the true one is paid back, we are not owed it. It goes past us. It has gone past us for twenty years, on this night, like a train through a station at which it does not stop. But I believe that it may be caught. That is what this is for. The great clock keeps our time, and the Thorne keeps the sun's, and the stars', and at the instant that the world's time falls back upon ours there is a difference between them of one hour exactly, and the Reconciler will take it up. It will take up the slack. Like a fusee." His hands moved, showing me. "And then I shall have it. I shall have an hour that is not on any clock. I shall go back through it. And I shall give her the tide properly."

"Give her—"

"I shall tell her the right time. I shall say, 'Margot, the clock in the hall is wrong; I put it back before dinner. It is three, not two. You have only a quarter of an hour.' And she'll not go. Or she will go by the punt. Or she'll run." He was smiling. His face was lit from below by my lantern, and it was the face of a man who has found, after long calculation, that the sum comes out. "She will get across. I have only to tell her. It is such a small thing. I have had the words ready for twenty years."

I have nursed people who believed that they were being poisoned by their relations, and one who believed that she was the Queen. I had been taught that one does not argue. I stood in that freezing chamber and looked at an old man's beautiful new brasswork, which had been made with more skill than I shall ever have at anything, to do a thing that could not be done. I found that I was not thinking of what I had been taught. I was thinking of a hand stretched out along a hospital floor. I was thinking that if someone had come to me with a machine and said, *You may have that hour and twenty minutes again*, I should not have asked how it worked.

"Mr Vane," I said. "I don't believe that it can be done."

"No," he said kindly. "You are a truthful girl, and you say what you think. I should be disappointed if you didn't. But you'll hold the lamp for me all the same, while I cut the pinion."

I held the lamp. He worked for two hours, with a file no thicker than a needle, on a wheel that I could have hidden under my thumbnail. His hands were perfectly steady. Once, behind us, the works gave their whirr and the quarter struck outside, and I felt the grey woman listen to it.

When he had finished he covered the clock with its cloth and locked the cupboard.

"You will not speak of this," he said. "Edmund would take it down. He means well. He doesn't understand what is owed."

I gave him my word. I have kept it until now.

* * *

I come to the diary.

I had worked through the desk by the nineteenth. It is a pretty desk, of rosewood, with a great many small drawers, and I had emptied them one by one and found nothing that could not have been read aloud in a drawing room. There were her household books. There were letters from her children at school. There was a bundle of tide tables, one for each year from 1946, which she had bought at the chandler's in Maldon; she had marked them in pencil, in a small upright hand, with rings round certain nights and now and then a single letter. I did not know then what the letter stood for. It was an A.

On top of the desk there stood a clock. It was a little French thing of gilt metal, in the shape of a drum held up by two cupids, such as one sees in the windows of antique shops. I had dusted round it for a fortnight. It did not go. It was the only clock in the whole of that house which did not, and I had come to be almost fond of it for that.

I asked Mr Vane about it, one night, on the stairs.

"That is Margot's," he said. "It was her mother's. It stopped in the year we were married and she would never let me touch it. She said that one clock in this house should be allowed to be wrong." He smiled. "She said it was a comfort to her. She said that when she was tired of being told the time, she went and looked at the one that had given up."

On the afternoon of the nineteenth I lifted it down to polish the desk beneath. It was heavier than I expected, and it rattled.

I turned it over. There is a round door in the back of such clocks, of brass, with a little catch, through which one reaches the bell. I do not know why I opened it. I think it was only that everything else in that desk had been opened, and I am a tidy person.

There was no bell. There was no movement of any kind; the drum was empty. It had been taken out, long ago, and the case kept. And pushed into the hollow, curled round to fit, there was a small book.

It was a pocket diary, bound in red cloth, such as one buys at a stationer's. The year was stamped on the cover in gilt. It was 1974.

I sat down in her chair. I held it shut on my knee for some time. The room was quite silent; there is a clock in the corner, but I did not hear it. Through the window I could see the orangery, with its broken panes, and the vine.

I knew that I ought not to read it. I had been engaged to put the papers of a dead woman in order. That does not give one leave to read what she had hidden inside a clock. And I knew whom I ought to take it to. It belonged to her husband.

But I thought of the old man in the tower with his file and his brass rods, building a machine to carry a message to his wife. *You have only a quarter of an hour.* And I thought of his son in the dark library, saying that it was not arithmetic.

One of them was wrong about how she had died. I had in my hand, in all likelihood, the means of knowing which. And whichever it was, I should have to go on telling the old man the truth when he asked me for it, at one in the morning. I had promised. I did not see how I could promise that and choose not to know.

That is what I told myself. I have since thought of another reason, which does me less credit. I wanted to know what Edmund had done. I wanted to know whether it was worse than what I had.

I looked up, before I opened it. I had the feeling that I was observed. There was no one in the doorway. There was no one in the garden. But the morning room is directly beneath the south end of the long gallery, and I knew very well what hung on the wall above my head.

I opened the book at the ribbon.

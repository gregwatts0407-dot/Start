## 5. The Wade

The water was cold enough to stop a man's heart. It came over his boots at the foot of the ramp and to his knees before the mare had gone her own length.

He let her have her head. He sat down in the saddle and kept his hands low and did nothing. There was nothing he could do. He could not see the road. He could see the lantern, and in its light a few yards of black water going by from left to right, fast, with rain driving into it. Now and then a withy came up out of the dark on one hand or the other, and passed, and was gone.

She was walking. He could feel the stones under her. She put each foot down as if she had counted them.

The wind was on his right cheek and the sea was against his left leg. It leaned on the mare like a man leaning on a gate. She leaned back. Where the road had been built up on its bank the water was at her belly. Where the bank had sunk she went in to the shoulder, and he felt her float for a stride and find it again.

He looked back once. There was nothing there. The farm was gone. The wall was gone. The whole of England had been put out like the lamps of the village.

He looked ahead. He had steered by the porch lamp at Compline as a boy, coming home late from the island side with a message. It should have been dead in front of him, over her ears.

A squall had come across. He could see rain and the lantern and the mare's white neck.

He took out his watch. He held it to the light. He does not know why he wanted the time. A man in that place does a thing because his hands are used to it.

At midnight the man was in the Wade with the sea to the mare's shoulder and no house in sight.

He put the watch away. He remembers thinking that it was a strange hour to be anywhere, and that in the house they would be at their port, with the candles lit.

* * *

He knew when they came to the Rill by the sound.

It had been a mutter and then a roar, and now it was all round him. The mare stopped. She stopped of her own will, with her forefeet together, and stood. The water was at her chest and it was different water. It did not lean. It tore. He could feel her whole body quivering against it.

He held up the lantern.

In front of them the road ended. He could see where it went down. The surface of the sea dipped there, in a long smooth slope, like the lip of a weir, and beyond the lip it was running north with a hump on it, and streaks of white. It was thirty yards across. He knew what was under it. There were the planks of the Clapper, and under the planks seven feet of tide going through a gut no wider than a barn.

On the other side, somewhere, the road came up again.

He sat her on the edge of it. He does not know how long. The rain had eased. He could see a little further, and to his right, downstream, black against the black, there was something standing in the water on legs.

It was the Perch.

He looked at it. He had looked at it from the wall the day before with the child. *The lady didn't get in the box. Why not? It was right there.*

She had been out here. On this road, in the dark, with the tide coming. Not in a gale; it had been a still night, his father said, with a moon. But the same water. She had stood somewhere near where he sat now, a small quick woman of fifty-four in her stockings, with a lantern, and her son had been with her, drunk, on the ground.

And she had been past it. She had been a hundred and fifty yards from his father's hand.

He thought, sitting there, that he did not want to know. That was the truth. His father had asked which it was, and he had put on his coat and taken the gun and come. But with the Rill going by in front of him he found that he was afraid of the answer. If she had been dragged, then Edmund Vane had killed her. And if she had not, then she had stood in this place and looked at Amos Garrow's light, and turned her back.

He did not know which he could better carry home.

The mare shifted. She put her head down to the water and blew at it.

No, he said. That's enough. I'll not ask it of you. We'll go back.

He meant it. He had his weight in the off stirrup and the rein against her neck to turn her. It was too much. She was twenty. He would sit on the wall at Saltcote and watch the boat's light go down the river. He would tell the child, when she was grown, that there were some things a man could not get an answer to.

The mare took the bit.

He felt it go out of his hands. She had it in her teeth; she pulled the rein through his fingers like a rope running out. She gathered herself under him, all at once, the way a young horse does at a fence.

And she went in.

* * *

It took them both under.

He had been in rivers. He had swum cattle over the Oldman in the June rise with the snow water coming down brown and trees in it. He had thought he knew what water could do.

This was not like a river. A river goes one way. This went north and down and round, all together. The cold shut on his chest like a door. He had the horn in one hand and a fistful of mane in the other and the sea was in his eyes and his mouth. He felt the mare go from under him. For a moment there was nothing of her but the mane.

He thought, that's it. That is the finish.

He has said what he thought of, then. He said it once to the woman, and once more, a long time after, to the one who has set this down. He did not think of Vane. He did not think of his father. He thought of a girl of twenty-four kneeling on the saltings with her skirt in the mud, holding a lamp. He thought, I'll not have seen her.

That was all. It was not a long thought. But it had been waiting twenty years, and it came up through him like a cork.

The lantern had gone out. It was still on the horn; he could feel it bang against his knee. They were in the dark, in the sea, being carried.

Something stood over him.

It was there and gone. Four black legs, crossed with bracing, and above them a box, and a ladder. They went under it, or beside it. It was so close that the mare's shoulder struck one of the posts. He put out his hand. He did not mean to. His palm met tarred wood, cold and rough, and slid along it, and it was behind them.

He had touched the post. He had done it all his life, going by on the road. He did not know why he did it then, with the water closing over his head. But he remembers the feel of it. He remembers thinking that it was solid, and that everything else in that place was moving.

The mare screamed.

It was not pain. She had got her head up. She was swimming. He felt her come alive between his knees, the big shoulders driving, and he let go of the horn and lay along her neck to give her her back. She was not fighting the set. She was going with it, and across. She knew. She was letting it carry her down, and all the time working over, a yard and a yard, toward where the bottom shelved.

Her off fore struck something. She lurched. She struck again.

She stood.

She stood up out of the sea with the water pouring off her and her legs spread and her head between her knees. He was half off. He hung on her neck. Under her feet there was shingle, hard, ribbed, going up.

She had found the road.

They were forty yards north of it. She had come out on the tail of the bank, where it ran out into the mud, the one place below the Clapper where a horse could land. A length either side and she would have been in the soft. He does not believe that she saw it. He believes that she had it in her feet.

He slid down. The water was at his waist. He stood with his arms round her neck and his face in her mane, and he could not speak.

She turned her head and looked at him. Her nostrils were red. She blew out, long, and shook herself, so that the saddle rattled.

Then she began to walk. She did not wait for him. He had to wade beside her with his hand on the stirrup leather. She went up the slope of the bank and onto the crown of the road, and turned her face to the island, and went on.

* * *

The rest was a walk. The water shoaled. The rain had gone over and there was a moon somewhere, behind cloud. He could see the withies again, and after a while the porch lamp, small and steady, where it had always been.

He got up on her when it was to her knees. He had no strength left to walk.

They came up the ramp onto the Ness at twenty past twelve. He looked at his watch on the dry ground. He had been forty minutes in the sea.

There was an orchard. He remembered it. He slid off under the first trees and stood holding the saddle. He was shaking, in long hard spasms. His teeth would not stay shut. He had lost the feeling in his hands.

He sat down with his back to an apple tree and the reins round his wrist. He does not know for how long. He has said that it was the cold, that he could not have stood if the Vanes had come down through the orchard with dogs. When he could feel his fingers he tried to relight the lantern. It took nine matches. He had them in an oilskin pouch, inside his shirt, with the packet. When he looked at his watch by the flame it was twenty to one.

Then he led her through the orchard and along under the garden wall. He knew the way. He had come it a hundred times, twenty years ago, on summer nights, with his heart going like a hammer. He had not thought that his feet would remember. They did. They took him along the brick path, past the water butt, to the wide door at the end of the glasshouse where the gardeners had brought the tubs through.

It was there. It leaned on its hinges. The glass above it was mostly gone.

He put his shoulder to the door and it gave.

He backed the mare in. He was talking to her. He was telling her that it was done. He did not look round until he had her under the roof and had turned to shut out the wind.

There was a candle burning.

It stood on an upturned pot in the far corner. He knew that pot. Beside it there was a woman.

* * *

He has never told what he saw, in so many words. He would say, there was a woman there, in a grey dress. Then he would stop. The one who listened learned not to ask.

But once he said more. It was late, and he was old. He said that she was standing with her hands down at her sides, and bare arms, and her hair up, in a long gown the colour of rainwater. The candle was behind her. He could not see her face.

He said that he thought he had drowned.

He had heard of it. Men who had come near it in the big rivers told how it went easy, at the last, and you saw things. He thought that he had gone down in the Rill with the mare, and that this was what there was. It was a glasshouse with a vine in it, and Mrs Vane, who had died on that road twenty years ago trying to come to his father. She had been waiting, and had lit a candle.

He said her name. He said, Mrs Vane.

No, the woman said. Tom. It's me.

She came forward. The light came round onto her face. There was a streak of white in her hair that had not been there. There were lines at her eyes. She was thin.

It was the girl who had held the lamp.

He does not tell what was said. He has been asked. He says that it was between the two of them and that it was a good while ago.

He says that she told him a thing she had carried for twenty years. It was the reason for a curtain. It was not the reason he had thought. He had gone to the other side of the world on account of the wrong one.

He says that he told her why he had come. He did not dress it. He had a gun on the saddle and she had seen it. She asked what it was for.

Vane, he said.

That is the word he gives. He will say that part. He says, I told her straight, I came for Vane. He says it as a thing that he wants known. He would not have her think afterwards that he had crossed that water for her and brought a gun along by chance.

What he said after that he does not say.

The one who set this down has heard another account of that hour. It was written by the woman, a long while later, in a book. In her account the man says something else as well. He says it at the end, after she has told him her part. She gives the words. They are not words that he would use, telling this. He was asked about it once, with the book open on the table.

He looked at the page for some time.

She's put it better than I said it, he said. I never had that many words.

But did you say it?

I'd not have crossed that water for Edmund Vane, he said. Not when the mare took the bit. I'd turned her. I've told you. I was going home.

Then why do you always say you came for him?

Because I did. I got on her for that. I rode down that ramp for that. A man ought to say what he set out to do. What he found when he got there is another thing.

That was all he would give. He shut the book and went out to look at a ewe.

* * *

He will tell this much more.

She would not go with him then. He had asked her. He had said the mare could do it again at twenty to four, with the ebb, and he would have her at Saltcote by four. She said that she must go into the house first. There was a thing she had to say to her father, in front of the rest of them, at one o'clock by their clocks. She had sat in that glasshouse twenty years ago when she should have been in the hall. She would not go to him from behind a curtain a second time.

He understood that. He had come across the sea on much the same errand.

She asked him to leave the gun.

She asked it twice. She said that her brother laughed when he was frightened, and that the man did not know what he would do if he were laughed at. She said that he had a child.

He looked at it. It was on the saddle, where he had set the saddle down, on the edge of a stone trough. The case had fallen open. The candle was on the barrels.

He did not say that he would leave it. He would not say a thing he was not sure of. She saw that he did not.

He said that he would be there at two by her father's clocks. That was three by his. He said if he was not, she was to get up on the mare and let her go home.

She went in. He watched her to the door. She went along the path in that grey gown with the wet of his coat on her shoulders, and she did not look round.

Then he was by himself. There was the mare, and the candle, and the rain on what was left of the glass.

He looked at his watch. It was a quarter to two.

He had been on the Ness an hour and twenty-five minutes. He had not once thought of the boat.

It came back to him like a blow. A quarter to two. That was the slack. That was the hour Ostler had named, in the public house, with four glasses in front of him. *Top of the tide. A box and a passenger.* At this minute, at the other end of the island, a launch would be lying against the Hythe with its engine turning. A big man with a hitch in his walk would be coming along the sea wall with a box.

He had come through seven feet of water to stand in front of that man. He had lost his hat and near enough his life. And then he had stood for an hour and a half in a glasshouse, holding a woman's hands, and forgotten what he came for.

He was not sorry. He would not say that he was. But his father had held on six days in a chair for it.

He went to the trough. He stood over the saddle.

*You'd not know what you were until you'd done it. And then you'd have to carry it.*

She was right. He knew that she was right. He was a man who had carried a sheep half a mile that night rather than leave it.

He thought, I will go without. I'll go along the wall with my hands empty and stand in his road and ask.

And he saw how it would be. He saw Vane stop, with the box in his arms, and look at him. He was hatless, and soaked, and smeared with marsh clay to the hair. He was the tenant's son. *Garrow. What the devil are you doing on the Ness? Get out of my way, man. I've a boat to catch.* And he would shoulder by. He would go down the staging, and the engine would open up. And there would be nothing to do but stand and watch him go, as his father had stood on the ramp.

The man found that he could not do it. He could take the answer, whichever it was. He could not take being walked past.

He drew the gun out of its case. It was wet. He broke it and wiped the chambers with his thumb. He took the two cartridges out of his pocket. They were dry; he had had them in the oilskin with the matches.

He stood with them in his palm.

For her sake he ought to leave them. He could carry it empty. A man forty yards off cannot tell.

He thought of Vane, who had been to a school, looking at a gun held by a Garrow and knowing by something in the way it was held that there was nothing in it. He thought of the laugh.

He put them in. He closed the breech. It made a small sound, like a gate latch.

He was not going to use it. He said that to the mare. She had turned her head and was watching him with one dark eye, and the lantern in it.

I'll be back, he said. You stand.

He blew out the candle on the pot. He left the lantern burning low for her. He went out of the wide door with the gun under his arm, into the wind, and turned north along the wall.

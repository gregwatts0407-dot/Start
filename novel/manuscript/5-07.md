## 7. Riding Out

She came at half past two by his watch. He heard her on the brick path. She was early.

He did not get up. He sat on the pot with the gun across his knees and the two cases at his feet. She stood in the door. She was still in the grey gown. Her face was wet, and it was shining, the way a face does that has been crying for a good reason.

He saw her look at the cases.

You've been in the house, he said. You've said your piece?

I've been in the house.

That's good. I want you to have done it. He looked down at the gun. There's a thing I've to tell you. You'd best hear it before you come any nearer.

She asked if he had found him. He said that he had. She asked if he had been told what his father wanted to know. He said no.

He began to tell her. He got as far as the tower. *He was up—*

She would not have it. She came across the floor and knelt in the leaves and put her hand over his mouth.

He will not say much of that. He says that she asked him not to tell her, not then, not in that house. She said she would hear it on the other side of the water, in daylight. She said that if she knew it she would not be able to get up on the horse, and she had waited twenty years to cross that road.

He told her it was a thing they would come for him about. She said, then they will come to Saltcote, and I shall be there.

He has turned that over since, more than any other part. She did not know what he had done. She would not let him say. But she knew that it was bad. She had seen the cases, and his face. And she said that she would come.

He did not think that he had a right to it. He took it. A man who has just gone through what he had will take a thing he has no right to.

She asked was there anything to be done. Anyone to be fetched.

He thought of the sluice, and the water going out.

No, he said. There's nothing there to do anything for.

She shut her eyes. He saw her make up her mind not to know. It was a hard thing to watch. He has thought since that it was the bravest thing he ever saw anyone do, and that it was also wrong.

She told him then what the old man had said. She had danced with her father, in the hall, and he had given her the hour of the tide. *A horse at twenty to three, by the house. Three feet in the Rill, and a set to the north.*

That was twenty to four. It was the hour he had reckoned himself, by the feel of the water, standing at the orchard gate. The old man had never left his house. He was eighty-one and they said his mind was gone. He had got it to the minute.

How'd he know there was a horse, the man said.

She did not know. She said that her father had taken her for her mother. He had thought he was sending her to Amos Garrow. *He said, I don't ask you to stay.*

The man put the gun down on the bricks and pushed it away with his boot.

He had come over that night with his father's gun, to settle for his father. He had settled nothing. He had shot a man who might have told him. And across the yard, in a hall full of candles, the old man he had never spoken to had stood up and given him his daughter and the time of the tide.

He did not know then about the land. That came after.

* * *

She went to the house for a coat. He saddled the mare.

He was a long time about it. His hands would not work. He dropped the cinch twice. The mare stood and let him. She had eaten some of the dead vine leaves and seemed none the worse.

He left the gun where it lay. He could not bring himself to touch it.

He thought about what he would do. He would get the woman across. That first. He would put her in his father's chair by the range. Then, when it was light, he would walk up to the village and knock at the constable's door. He would say, I'm Tom Garrow of Saltcote. I shot Mr Edmund Vane off his stable tower at two this morning with a twelve-bore. You'll find him on the saltings north of the Ness, or down the river.

He would ask to see the child first. He thought they would let him.

He did not think beyond that. He had seen the inside of a gaol once, in Fort Macleod, fetching out a hand who had been drinking. He thought of the smell.

He heard her feet on the path again. She was running.

She came in at the door. She had on an old tweed coat, a woman's, too wide in the shoulder, and a pair of gumboots. The grey silk showed between. She had something shut in her left hand. She stopped in front of him. She was out of breath, and she was laughing, and the tears were running into her mouth.

I've seen Edmund, she said.

He stood with his hand on the bridle. He did not take her meaning.

He's in the kitchen. He's eaten half a goose. He's a lump on his head the size of an egg, and a few of your pellets in his neck. He says to tell you that you're a rotten shot.

He heard the words. He could not make them into anything.

He's on his feet, he said.

He's on his feet and making jokes.

I saw him go off the back of that tower. I saw him fall. With his arms out. Into the fleet.

I don't know what you saw. I know what I've just spoken to. You didn't kill anyone. Do you hear me? You didn't.

He felt behind him for the stone trough. He sat down on the edge of it. His legs had gone.

He will not say what that was like. He says that there is no way to say it. He had been a dead man for an hour and a half. He had stood in a yard and waited for them to come. He had planned what he would say to the constable. And she had walked in out of the dark in her mother's coat and told him it was not so.

He put his face in his hands. She held his head against her.

Later he asked himself what he had seen. He has asked it ever since. He had looked up that tower and seen a white face with a wound on it. He had seen a black coat with its arm coming down. He had fired at the coat, and the coat had gone over into the fleet and out through the sluice. He had stood on the bank and watched a white cuff go under the arch.

And Edmund Vane was in his kitchen with a goose bone.

He learned on the Sunday what it was. They dug it out of the outfall and carried it up to the house on a hurdle. It was a thing of oak, two hundred years old. It struck the quarters. The young people had put a dinner jacket on it for the party.

He had shot the Jack. He had come through seven feet of sea with his father's gun, and stood in a yard, and with both barrels at thirty yards he had killed a clock.

He laughed when he was told. He laughed until the woman came in from the dairy to see what ailed him. And then he stopped. He had remembered the second barrel. It had gone left and high. He had been told about the pellets in the neck.

* * *

She had asked her brother for him. In the scullery, with a candle. She told him so in the glasshouse while he sat on the trough.

She had said to Vane what she saw, twenty years ago, from behind the vine. Two lights on the Wade. One man walking up the lawn at dawn. She had said, you went after her. You kept her there.

And he had said yes.

That was the answer. The man had come for it, and it had been got for him by a woman in a passage, in three minutes, without a gun.

*I kept her standing on that road an hour and a quarter with the tide making.* She gave him the words. Vane had said them straight out. He had not laughed.

Then she never turned of herself, the man said.

He said there was more. He wanted to tell me. I wouldn't hear it. She shut her eyes. I was too angry. He said, another day.

The man thought about that. He thought about his father, in the chair, with the wet sound in his chest. *I'd like one Garrow to know if she was coming.*

She had been coming. Vane had said it. He had kept her on the road. So she had been dragged, as near as made no matter; she had been held there by a drunk son till the water was round them. She had not stood and looked at Amos Garrow's lamp and chosen the house.

It was what the old man had prayed for. God forgive me, he had said, I've hoped it.

But there was more. Vane had said so. *It's worse than what you think. And it's better.* And the woman opened her left hand. There was a ring in it, plain gold, worn thin. Her mother had taken it off on the Wade that night and given it to her son. *Give that to your father.*

The man looked at it on her palm.

That's a queer thing for a woman to do, he said. That's about to leave a man. To send him back her ring by her son.

Yes.

That's not a woman being dragged.

No.

He did not know what it meant. He turned it over the whole of that winter. A woman who is being hauled back toward a house by force does not stop to take off her wedding ring and send a message. That is a thing done by somebody with her mind made up, and a little time.

So there was more. And he would go for it. He said so to the woman. He would go across by the road on the Sunday or the Monday, in daylight, with his hat in his hand, and ask Edmund Vane civil for the rest. He had done enough on his father's account with a gun.

He did not go. On the Monday they found Vane on the Ray Sand.

He never asked. He had one answer, got by someone else, and it was half. He has had to make do with it. He would say to the one who listened, when he came to this place: That's all I ever had. I had, he kept her. And a ring.

He would look at the range for a while.

I'd have liked the rest, he would say. For Dad.

* * *

She told him about the ground as he was strapping the gun under the saddle flap. He had picked it up after all. He would not leave it in the old man's glasshouse.

Papa's given you the farm, she said.

He did not understand her.

Saltcote. Freehold. He dictated it to Fenn in the hall, in the Hour. *To Amos Garrow or his heirs.* Edmund told me. There'll be no caravans.

He stood with the buckle in his fingers.

He had the lawyer's paper inside his shirt. It had gone through the Rill with him. *Vacant possession. The twenty-fifth day of March.* He had read it standing, in a kitchen, with the child watching. He had thought about three hundred ewes going up a ramp.

A hundred and eighty years they had paid rent for that ground. They had gone up a stair twice a year with their hats off. And the old man had given it away in a night, to a dead tenant, between a speech and a dance. He did not know that the tenant's son was eighty yards off in his stable yard with a loaded gun.

The man does not know why he did it. He has heard what was said in that hall. *There is something I owe across the water. I can't pay it to the man. I'll pay it to his name.* He thinks that the old man knew more than they gave him credit for. He thinks he had known for twenty years where his wife was walking to, and had never said. And on the last night that he kept, he paid.

The paper came in April. It was from the same firm, on the same blue sheet, signed by the same hand. It conveyed the freehold of the house, buildings and land known as Saltcote Farm to Thomas Garrow, son and heir of Amos Garrow deceased, in consideration of one peppercorn. The lawyer had written a line at the foot, in ink. *I am glad to have lived to draw this.*

The man put it in the biscuit tin, on top of the rent books.

He has a way of telling that. The one who set this down has heard it many times, and has argued with him.

He says: He took his father's ground back that night with a gun.

He says it flat. It is how he ends that piece of the story. He will say, I rode over there with Dad's twelve-bore under the saddle, and I rode home with the farm.

He has been told it was not the gun. It was an old man with a pen. The gun did nothing but spoil a carving and put eleven pellets in a man's shoulder. He would have had the farm if he had stopped at home that night by the range.

He knows it. He will hear you out. Then he will say it again the same way.

I asked him once why. He was a long time answering.

Because I'd have took it, he said. That's what I can't get by. I never knew the old man had give it. I stood in that yard and I'd a loaded gun, and Vane up there laughing, saying I'd no land. If he'd come down them stairs, I don't know what I'd have done to get it. I think I'd have done anything. I found that out. He was looking at his hands. So I'll not say it was give me. It was give me, and I'd have took it, both. I went over to take it. That's what I carried over, and the farm's what I carried back. A man ought to say what he set out to do.

* * *

He does not tell the crossing at any length. He says that it was a still night by then, with a moon, and the ebb running. He put the woman up in the saddle and led the mare in at twenty to four.

He swam the Rill at her head. There was three and a half feet on it and a set to the north that carried them down past the Perch. He had hold of the cheek strap. The mare went through it like a ship. She came up on the bank thirty yards down, where she had landed the first time, and stood, and he got up out of the water on his hands and knees.

That was the last time he asked it of her. She lived six years more and never went into salt water again above the fetlock.

He will tell one thing about it.

When they were over the deep, and he was up behind the woman with his arms round her, she turned in the saddle and looked back. She said that her brother had come down to the gate with a candle to see them off. She lifted her arm and waved.

The man looked.

There was the porch lamp. And there was another light, at the orchard gate, where the Wade came ashore. It was small and white. It was very low down.

He has said that he did not like it. He did not know why. A candle is yellow, and a man holds it at his chest. This was thin and pale, like a glow-worm, and it was not three feet off the ground. And it moved. It came down to the water, and it seemed to him that it came on, out along the road.

That's low down for a man, he said.

She said that Edmund was carrying it in a saucer.

It looks to be coming this way.

She said he would not. Not on foot. He knew the tide better than anyone.

That was so. There were three feet in the Rill. Nobody who knew that water would walk into it. The man told himself that it was the moon on a wet stone, or a lamp set down on the gatepost.

A cloud came over. When it had gone by he looked again and could not find it.

He turned the mare's head to the shore. He had a woman in front of him who had not slept, and was wet to the waist, and shaking. He had a horse of twenty who had swum the Rill twice. He had a lamp in a kitchen window to steer by.

He did not look back again. He rode up out of the sea at Saltcote at five minutes past four by his watch, on the Sunday morning, with Imogen Vane before him on the saddle.

That is where he stops.

* * *

He will go on, if he is asked. He does not count it as part of the telling.

He carried her in and set her in the chair. He saw to the mare. He rubbed her down for an hour, and gave her a warm mash, and stood with her until she had stopped shivering.

At seven he went across the yard to the cottage for the child.

The old sister opened to him. She looked ill. She had her hand at her throat. She said, Tom, I've a thing I ought to tell you, and then Dolly Pask came round the end of the cottage at a run, with her skirt hitched. She had left her van up on the wall with the door hanging open. She had come off the island as soon as the road was dry. She was grey in the face and there were cobwebs in her hair.

She stopped. She looked at the old woman, and at him.

Is she here, she said.

Is who here?

The child.

She's abed. Vi's just this minute telling me.

Dolly Pask shut her eyes. She stood with her hand on the wall. Then she pushed past him, and past her sister, and went up the stairs. He heard her boots overhead, and a door.

She came down slowly. She sat on the bottom stair.

She's asleep, she said. She's in her bed, asleep. Her voice was not steady. You'd best take her home, Tom. She'll have had a bad night. There was a wind.

He went up. It was a slip of a room under the thatch, with one window. The child lay on her side with her knees drawn up and the blanket over her head. He turned it back.

She was sound asleep. Her face was white. Her hair was soaking.

He thought that she had sweated. She did, sometimes, since her mother. She would wake in the night crying and wet through.

He lifted her. She did not stir. She was in her vest and drawers. Her clothes were not on the chair. He saw her shoes by the bed. They were the canvas ones with stars on the soles that he had bought her in Lethbridge. They were dark with wet, and there was weed in the laces.

He thought, the dew. She has been out in the yard at first light looking for me.

He did not think more than that. He has said so, when he was asked. He had not slept. He had been in the sea twice. He had killed a man and been told that he had not. He had a woman in his kitchen that he had waited twenty years for. He looked at a pair of wet shoes on a cottage floor and he thought, I must tell her not to go out without her coat.

There was something pushed in under the bed. It was a bundle, black, rolled tight. He saw the edge of it. He took it for one of old Vi's shawls.

He carried the child down, and across the yard, and into the house. She woke as he came through the door. She looked up at him, and then round the kitchen. She saw the woman in the chair by the range, in his shirt, with her hair down.

She looked for a long time.

You're the lady, she said.

Which lady, the woman said.

From the house. You were on the road, Friday. You touched the box.

Yes.

Did you come on Mab?

Yes.

In the dark?

Yes.

I'd be scared.

I was, the woman said.

The child considered it. She had her head on one side. Then she put her face against the man's neck. He felt her go heavy. She said one thing more before she slept. She said it into his collar, so low that he hardly had it.

Is the man all right?

What man?

She did not answer. She was gone.

He thought she had been dreaming. He put her in his own bed and she slept till four in the afternoon.

* * *

They found Vane on the Monday. A policeman came to the farm on the Tuesday. He was a young man, an inspector, and civil.

He asked if the man had a shotgun. The man said every farm on that shore had one. He asked to see it. He looked through the barrels. He said that the deceased had some pellets in him, number five, fired from below.

The man said nothing.

The inspector asked where he had been on the Saturday night.

He would have told. He had it ready. He was not a man who lied, and he had made up his mind in the glasshouse to walk to the constable's door. He had only been stopped by being told there was nothing to confess.

There was something now. Edmund Vane was dead. He had been drowned. But he had gone into the sea with shot in his neck and shoulder, and the man knew whose.

He opened his mouth.

He was here, the woman said.

She was standing at the range with her back to it. She had on a skirt of Dolly Pask's and the iron ring that she had sent to Scotland for.

He was not on the Ness that night, Inspector, until twenty to four in the morning. I can tell you so, because I was standing at the orchard gate, and I watched him come out of the sea on that mare to fetch me. He never got down from the saddle. He turned her round and brought me straight back. I'm Imogen Vane. We are going to be married.

The inspector looked from one to the other. He wrote it down.

It was the only lie the man ever heard her tell. She told it without a tremor, looking the law in the eye, and she never spoke of it afterwards. Nor did he.

He let her. That is the part he does not forgive himself. He stood in his father's kitchen with his mouth open, and let a woman lie for him. He let it go. He had the child. He told himself that.

There was an inquest. He sat through it beside her with his hat on his knee. A doctor read out how Vane had died. He said drowning. He said a blow to the head, hours before. He said a second blow. He said chloral. And he said eleven pellets of number five shot in the left shoulder, the neck and the ear, from a distance, from below.

The coroner said that he hoped somebody's conscience would one day supply what the evidence did not. He looked along the benches.

The man looked at his hat.

He has done the sum since. He does it most nights. Vane was hit on the head by somebody; he does not know who. He was doped; he does not know why. Then he climbed a tower, hurt, and stood in a lamp, and the man put a charge of duck shot into the side of his neck from thirty yards. An hour and a half later he was sitting in a kitchen making jokes. Two hours after that he was in the sea.

A man bleeds from the neck. He may not feel it. He may sit and eat and say that it is nothing, and all the while it is running down inside his shirt. Then he stands up, and walks down to the water to see his sister off, and he is light in the head. He goes dizzy. It takes him at the edge of the tide.

The man saw a light at the orchard gate. It was low down, for a man.

He has thought, he was on his knees.

That is what he believes. He does not tell it. It is not in the story; the story stops when the mare comes out of the sea. But he said it once to the one who set this down, a few weeks before the end, in a white room, with a tube in his arm. He said, I never killed him on the tower. I've known that near thirty year. But I put the shot in him that took him down to the water. He'd have been alive in the morning, but for me. He'd have told Imogen the rest. He'd have told me.

He said, I've had the farm. I've had her. I've had the girl. I got the whole of it, that night, everything a man could ask, and I got it with his blood on it.

He said, I never asked him the question.

He was told that it was not so. He was told there was another account.

He did not hear. They had given him something for the pain.

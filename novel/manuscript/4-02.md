## Chapter Two

It had begun with a foal.

That was in the May of 1974. She was home from London, where she had been two years at the Institute learning to take varnish off dead men's pictures. She was twenty-four, and clever, and bored. She had a small green car and a way of looking at people, and she thought that the Ness was the dullest ninety acres in England.

She had driven across on the evening ebb with the windows down. It was one of those long light evenings that the marsh has in spring, when the whole sky seems to rest on the ground and the larks will not stop. She was halfway over the Wade when she saw the horse.

It was a grey mare, out on the saltings to the north of the road, a couple of hundred yards off, where the Garrows ran their stock. It was lying down. There was a man kneeling beside it.

She would have driven on. A tenant's mare was nothing to her. But the man got up, and turned, and waved his arm. It was not a greeting. It was the way one waves at a boat.

She stopped the car. She got out and stood on the road in her London shoes.

"Can you hold a lamp?" he shouted.

She had never been spoken to in that way by anyone on the estate. She was Miss Imogen. Men touched their caps to her.

"I beg your pardon?"

"A lamp! Can you hold one? She's foaling, and it's coming wrong, and I'll lose the light. I can't hold it and get my arm in."

She looked at the saltings. They were green and wet and seamed with little creeks, and she knew what they would do to her shoes. She took the shoes off. She left them on the bonnet of the car and went down off the road in her stockings, with her skirt held up, across two hundred yards of sea lavender and mud.

It was the first thing she ever did for him. She thought afterwards that it had settled the rest.

He was a year younger than she. She knew him by sight, as one knows the children of the farm; she had seen him at harvest suppers, a silent brown boy at the end of a bench. He had been away at an agricultural college and come back with shoulders. He had his shirt off and his right arm was red to the elbow. He did not look at her. He put a storm lantern into her hands.

"There. Down by her tail. Hold it steady. Don't mind what you see."

She held it. She held it for an hour and ten minutes, kneeling in the wet, while the light went out of the sky and the mare groaned. Imogen had never seen anything born. She saw his arm go in to the shoulder. She saw his face, turned away, with the eyes shut, as though he were listening with his fingers. He talked the whole time. He did not talk to her. He talked to the mare, low and steadily, a string of words with no gaps, the way one would talk to a child in a fever. *There, my old girl. There. I've got him. He's got his leg back, the fool, I've got to turn him. Wait now. Wait for me. That's it. That's my beauty.*

She had never heard anybody speak with so much tenderness. She had not known that it could be done.

It came all at once, in the end. There was a rush, and something long and dark and shining lay on the grass in a bag of its own, and he tore the bag with his hands. It was a foal. It was black and wet and all legs. It lay with its sides heaving. The mare swung her great head round and breathed on it.

He sat back on his heels. He was shaking. He wiped his face with his forearm and left a streak across it.

"It's a filly," he said. He was grinning like a boy. "Look at her. Look at the length of her. She'll be grey, like her dam; they're born dark." Then he looked at Imogen for the first time, properly, in the lantern light. She saw him see who she was. The grin went. "Miss Vane. I never—I'd not have called you down, only I didn't know the car. I'm sorry. Your stockings."

She looked down. She was mud to the thighs. There was blood on her skirt, and something worse.

She began to laugh. She laughed until she had to put the lantern down. After a moment, uncertainly, he laughed too.

"What will you call her?" she said.

"I don't know. Dad names them."

"Call her something from a poem. She was born in a salt marsh by lamplight. She ought to be out of a poem."

"I don't know any poems."

"I'll find you one."

She found it that night, in the library, in a book that nobody had opened since her grandfather. She copied out two lines on a sheet of her mother's writing paper. In the morning she walked across the Wade, on her own feet, and put it into his hand in the yard at Saltcote, with his father looking on. *Queen Mab.* He read it slowly, moving his lips. He folded it small and put it inside his shirt.

"Mab," he said. "That'll do. That's a name you could call across a field."

It was the name of the mare that stood in that yard tonight, under a rug. She was twenty years old.

* * *

Imogen lay in her narrow bed on the Saturday morning and watched the ceiling lighten. She had not slept. She had gone through it all in the night, every piece, as one goes through a drawer that has been shut for years.

It had lasted from May to October. Five months. She had been at Compline for all of them. She had told her tutors that there was a picture in the house that wanted treatment. It was true; she had spent the summer in the morning room with the portrait of a Georgian Vane face down on a table, relining it. In the evenings, when the tide served, she had gone out.

They had met in the orangery.

It was her idea. He would not come into the house. He said so flatly, the first time she asked him. *I'll not. Not by the kitchen, and I'd not be let in by the front.* It was the only thing she ever heard him say with anger in it. So she showed him the old glasshouse on the south side, with its broken panes and its vine. Nobody went there. Her grandmother had grown oranges in it, in tubs, and there was still a smell, in warm weather, of earth and something sweet. The vine had got through the roof and filled the whole place with green. In August there were grapes, small and sour and black.

He would come across at dusk on the ebb, walking, and round by the sea wall to the garden side. She would be waiting among the leaves. They sat on two upturned pots. They hardly touched, at first. He was shy of her, and of her voice, and her hands. He held his own hands between his knees as though they were something he had brought by mistake.

She talked. She had never talked so much in her life. She told him about London, and the pictures, and what it was to take a brown fog of old varnish off a sky and find that the painter had meant it blue. He listened with his head down. She thought at first that he did not follow. Then one evening he said:

"It's like the land."

"How?"

"You take off what's been put on. Bad drainage. Wrong crop. A man that didn't know it. And underneath it's what it always was. You don't make it. You just clear off and let it be seen." He went dark red. "I can't say things. I'd sooner show you."

So he showed her. He showed her the marsh. She had lived in the middle of it for twenty-four years and never looked. He took her out on the saltings at five in the morning and made her lie on her front to watch a redshank on its nest. He showed her where the samphire grew, and how the creeks filled from the far end first. He stood her on the sea wall at the top of a spring tide and said, *Now. Watch. It'll stop,* and it did, the whole sea, as if someone had laid a hand on it.

"It's the slack," he said. "It only lasts a few minutes. Dad says it's the tide thinking better of it."

He knew to the minute when the Wade would open and when it would close. He did not look at a table. He looked at the sky, and smelled the air, and said, "Half past nine," and was right.

"How do you do that?"

"I don't know. It's like knowing when you're hungry."

She fell in love with him as one falls asleep, without marking the moment. She knew only that some time in June she had stopped thinking of him as the Garrow boy. She listened all day for the tide.

He kissed her on the tenth of July. She had the date by heart. There had been a thunderstorm, and the rain was coming through the broken roof in ropes, and they had backed under the thickest part of the vine. A drop ran down her neck. He put up his hand to brush it away, and did not take the hand back.

It was not practised. He had kissed nobody, she thought, or nobody much. He did it as he did everything, slowly and completely. When he stopped, he looked at her with a kind of dread.

"I've no right," he said.

"I gave you the right in May. I held your lamp."

"You're Miss Vane."

"I'm Imogen. Say it."

He could not, for a week. He called her nothing at all. Then one night, leaving, with his hand on the door frame, he said it, very low, like a man trying a word in a foreign language. *Imogen.* He made it sound like the name of a place.

At the end of August he gave her the ring.

He had made it himself. He was learning the smith's work from the man at Thorney, to shoe his father's horses. It was a horseshoe nail, bent round into a circle, with the head of the nail beaten flat and square for a stone. He had filed it smooth, and it shone like silver.

"It's only iron," he said. "It'll rust if you let it. You'd have to keep it oiled."

She put it on. It was a little large.

"I can't offer you anything," he said. "You know that. I'm a tenant's son. I'll have Saltcote after Dad, if your father lets me, and that's a hundred and forty acres of grazing and a house with the damp in. You've been to London. You could have anyone."

"I don't want anyone. I want a man who can tell the time by the smell."

"Your people won't have it."

"My mother will."

She had believed that. It was the thing that made what came afterwards so hard. She had been certain of her mother. She had been saving it up, to tell her. She had pictured the amused grey eyes, and the laugh. *Imogen. A Garrow? How perfectly sensible of you.*

And then she had walked into the Grey Room in October and found the jewel case on the bed.

* * *

She got up. It was seven by the house. She washed in cold water and dressed in a grey jersey and a tweed skirt, and went down the back stairs. The kitchen was empty; Dolly had not yet come over. She made tea and drank it standing.

She would not think about the rest. She had been over it enough. The quarrel with Edmund. The orangery, in the dark, with her mother's voice. *Come over in the morning.* The two lights on the Wade, and the grey dawn, and the man walking up the lawn with the sea running off him.

But the mind does not ask leave. It went on by itself.

Tom had come three times that week.

The first time was the Monday, the day after. He had walked over at low water and knocked at the kitchen door. Dolly came up to say so. Imogen was lying on her bed with her face to the wall. She said, "Tell him I can't."

The second was the day of the inquest. He had waited an hour on the gravel.

The third was the Saturday. The funeral had been the day before. It rained. He stood in the kitchen yard, bareheaded, with his cap in his two hands, and looked up at her window. He stood there from ten in the morning until past noon. She watched from behind the curtain. He did not move at all. The rain ran down his face and he let it. She had seen him stand like that beside a sick ewe, for a whole night, waiting to see which way it would go.

She had wanted to run down to him. Every part of her had wanted it. She had put on her coat twice.

And each time she had seen her mother's lantern going away across the lawn. She had heard her own voice at the tea table. *She's going to him tonight. To Amos Garrow.* She had done that. She had told, out of spite, because she was jealous of her own mother; and her brother had gone after her; and now her mother lay in St Botolph's churchyard with the earth still raw.

If she went down to that yard, she would have to tell him. He would look at her, with those steady eyes, and she would have to say, *It was me. I told Edmund. And I hid, and let her go alone.* And then she would see his face change. She thought that she could bear anything but that.

So she had stayed behind the curtain. At a quarter past twelve he put his cap on, and turned, and walked out of the yard.

He left a letter with Dolly. It was one sheet, in pencil, in a round careful hand like a schoolboy's.

*I don't know what I done. If it is on account of my father and your mother I never knew of it till after. I would not have kept it from you. I will be at Saltcote till the 14th of November. Then I have took a passage. If you will come I will stay. If you send word I will stay. I will not come to the house again as I think it troubles you. T. G.*

She had read it standing at the scullery window. She had it still. It was in Edinburgh, in the lid of her workbox, with an iron ring that she had kept oiled for twenty years.

She did not send word. On the fourteenth she sat in her room all day with the door locked. On the fifteenth she packed, and went back to London, and did not come home again in October.

There had been one more. It came to the Institute, in the spring of 1976, forwarded from Compline. It had a Canadian stamp. Inside was a photograph, taken with a cheap camera. It showed a plain of yellow grass running away to the edge of the picture, and a line of mountains, very small, with snow on them. There was nothing else. No house, no figure. On the back he had written the name of a place, and then:

*It is all sky here. You would like the colour. T.*

She had looked at that photograph for a long time. She was a person whose business it was to look. She saw that he had stood in the middle of an empty country and tried to find something in it that he could give her, and that this was what he had found.

She had not answered. She had not known what to say that would be true and not unbearable. She told herself that she would write when she had found the words. She was still looking for them a year later. After that it was too late; one cannot answer a letter three years old.

Somebody had told her, in the 'eighties, that he had married. Dolly, she thought. It had come in a list of village news, between a wedding and a flood. *Young Tom Garrow's wed out there, his father says. A girl off a ranch.* Imogen had said, "I'm glad," and found, to her surprise, that it was true. She had been glad for him, and gone back to her flat, and taken off a decent man's ring.

* * *

Her father was in the Shop. She went down the stone steps at a quarter to eight and found him at his bench, under the window, with a thing in his hand no bigger than a sixpence.

"Papa."

He looked up. For an instant his eyes did not know her. Then they cleared, and he smiled, the slow sweet smile that he so seldom gave.

"Imogen. They said you were coming. I said, she never comes in October. Fenn must have written." He held up his cheek for her to kiss, like a child. His skin was as dry as paper. "You're thin. Are you working?"

"A Raeburn. A man in a red coat. Somebody cleaned him with soda in 1890 and took his eyebrows off."

"Can you put them back?"

"I can put back what I'm sure was there. Not more."

"That is honest. I'm glad to hear it. So few people will stop at what they are sure of." He turned the little wheel in his fingers, and she saw that it was newly cut, and bright. "I have been doing something of the kind myself. Not restoring. Making good. There was a thing I got wrong, a long time ago, and I've found a way to put it right. I shan't tell you. You would say that it couldn't be done. But you will see, tonight."

"What did you get wrong, Papa?"

"The time," he said simply. "I gave your mother the wrong time."

She stood beside him. She put her hand on his shoulder. It was all bone under the cardigan.

She had heard him say it for twenty years. Every time she came. *I put the clocks back early. She went by the hall clock. It was my doing.* And every time she had stood, as she stood now, with the truth in her mouth like a stone.

*It wasn't the clock, Papa. Edmund went after her. He kept her out there. I saw the lights. I saw him come back.*

She had never said it. To say it, she would have had to say the rest. *And he went because I told him.* She had kept silent to shield her brother, she told herself. It was a noble thing; one did not give one's brother to one's father. But she knew that it was not Edmund she was shielding.

So she let her father go on believing that he had killed his wife with a clock. She had let him turn the house into a shrine to it. She came twice a year, and kissed his cheek, and went away.

"You look like her this morning," he said. He was peering at her through his glass. "About the eyes. You have always had her eyes. I used to find it a comfort and now I find that I can't always tell." He touched her face, lightly. "Don't mind me if I call you by her name tonight. I do sometimes. Miss Quaile corrects me. It is very good of her, but one gets tired of being corrected."

"I shan't mind, Papa."

"No. You never minded things. You were the quiet one. Edmund made all the noise." He turned back to his bench. "He is unhappy, you know. He has been unhappy since he was thirty-two, and I've never found out why. I am not a man that people tell things to. Your mother said so. She said that I could hear a watch losing two seconds a week and couldn't hear my own children."

Imogen went up the steps. At the top she had to stop, and hold the rail.

* * *

She had meant to keep indoors. She had the whole day planned. She would look at the pictures in the dining room, which needed it. She would help Dolly with the silver. She would stay on the east side of the house, where the windows looked at the stables and not across the water.

At a quarter past eight she was standing at a window of the long gallery with her grandfather's telescope.

It was a ship's glass, of brass, in three draws, with the leather gone from the barrel. It had lain in that window seat since before she was born. As children she and Edmund had fought for it, to watch the shipping. She had not gone to look for it. She had been walking past, and her hand had lifted the seat, and there it was.

She told herself that she wanted to see the birds.

The tide was making. It had filled the creeks and was coming in over the flats from the north, grey and wrinkled, and the Wade lay across it like a road in a flood, with half an hour left to it at most. Across half a mile of mud and water the far shore stood up very clear in the cold light. She found the sea wall. She moved the glass along it, slowly, to the left. A tarred barn. Another. A black gable, with a chimney, and smoke.

The yard came into the circle, small and bright and shaking a little with her pulse.

There was the grey mare. She had her rug off, and she stood square and patient on the stones, with her head down. She was white now with age, white as a swan. And beside her, with his back to the glass, bent double, with one of her hind feet between his knees, was a man in a canvas coat.

Imogen stopped breathing.

She could see nothing of his face. She saw a hat, a wide one with a curled brim, pushed back. A lean back, with the coat tight across the shoulders. Long legs, in faded blue. He was working at the hoof with a rasp. She could see his elbow going. Every few strokes he stopped, and ran his thumb along what he had done, and bent again.

She would have known him among ten thousand, by that. He did it as he had done everything, slowly and completely, with his whole attention. He had drawn a foal so, with his eyes shut. He had kissed her so.

He let the foot down. He straightened, with a hand to the small of his back, the way a man does who is no longer twenty-three. He took off his hat and wiped his forehead on his sleeve. And he turned round.

She had been told that he had gone grey. She had not been told the rest. The round brown boy's face that she had carried about with her was gone. This was a man's face, long and spare and weathered, with lines cut deep on either side of the mouth, as though by wind. The brows had come down. It was a face that had spent twenty years looking at a great distance in a hard light.

But the eyes had not changed. She could not see their colour through the glass; she did not need to. She saw how they rested on things. He looked at the mare. He looked at the sky, a long measuring look, and she knew that he was reading the weather, and that there would be wind by dark.

And then he lifted his head and looked across the water at the house.

He looked straight at it. He stood with his hat in his hand and his eyes on Compline, and his face was perfectly still. It was not the way one looks at a view. She had seen that look on him once before, in a yard, in the rain, turned up toward a curtain.

She knew that he could not see her. She was a quarter of an inch of dark glass in a wall of windows, half a mile away. But the telescope brought him so near that she could have counted the buttons on his coat, and it seemed to her that his eyes came down the length of the brass tube and found her.

She stepped back from the window so sharply that the glass struck the frame.

Her heart was going like a bird's. She stood against the panelling with the telescope held to her chest. She was forty-four years old. She was a senior conservator, with a pension, and a flat, and a white streak in her hair. She was hiding from a man behind a wall for the second time in her life.

When she dared to look again, he had put his hat on. He was standing with his back to her. A car had come along the road on top of the sea wall, a big dull one, and stopped at the head of the ramp. A heavy man in a raincoat had got out and was reading the tide board. Tom was watching him.

She saw the stranger get back into his car and drive down onto the Wade, with the sea already at the stones. She saw Tom turn his head and follow the car with his eyes, the whole way across, until it was hidden by the point. Then he looked at the house again.

He stood a moment longer. He put his hand inside his coat, to his chest, as though to feel that something was there. He took the mare by the halter and led her into the barn.

Imogen closed the telescope. She laid it in the window seat and shut the lid.

She thought: he has not come home to bury his father and go away again. A man does not look at a house like that who means to leave it alone.

She did not know what he meant to do. She thought of a notice to quit, folded small, inside a shirt. She thought of her brother, who would be drunk by noon.

And she thought, with a clearness that frightened her, that whatever Tom Garrow had come back to settle with the Vanes, he had a right to settle it. The first account on the list was hers.

Behind her, at the end of the gallery, the cracked clock struck the half hour. Under the portrait the lamp had burned out. Her mother looked down the length of the floor at her, with her lips parted, as though she had just asked a question and would wait as long as it took for the answer.

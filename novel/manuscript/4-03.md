## Chapter Three

She found Edmund in the gunroom at noon. He was sitting on the bench where they had sat as children to have their boots pulled off, with a tumbler beside him and an open letter in his hand. He put the letter in his pocket when she came in.

"Immy," he said. "The prodigal. I didn't believe Fenn when he said you'd come."

"I didn't believe Dolly when she said what you'd done."

"I've done a number of things. You'll have to narrow it down."

"Saltcote. A notice to quit. On the morning they buried him."

He looked at her blankly. Then she saw it reach him. He shut his eyes.

"Was it," he said. "Was that the day? Christ." He rubbed his face with his big hand. "I told Fenn the end of the week. I never looked at a calendar. I haven't looked at one since September. Was the boy there? Did he get it at the church?"

"He got it in Dolly's kitchen. He read it twice and put it in his shirt."

"Yes. He would. They never say anything, the Garrows. They just put it away somewhere and wait." He reached for the tumbler. "Don't look at me like that. It's a hundred and forty acres of grazing below the level of the sea, and the wall wants twenty thousand spent on it, and the old man paid the same rent in 1994 that his father paid in 1950 because Father never thought to put it up. There's a man who'll give me a hundred and ten thousand for it, for a holiday park. I owe more than that."

"How much more?"

"Enough."

"Edmund."

"Don't, Immy." He said it quite gently. "You've been in Edinburgh for twenty years, cleaning other people's pictures. You come down at Easter and in August and you look round at the damp and the dry rot, and you look at me. Then you go back. I don't blame you. I'd have gone if I could. But don't come home in October and ask me how much."

She sat down on the other end of the bench. There was a row of pegs opposite, with old coats on them, and under the coats a rack of guns. She remembered her brother teaching her to shoot, when she was ten and he was eighteen, out on the wall. He had put his arms round her from behind to hold the stock to her shoulder. He had been patient with her. He had been the kindest and funniest person she knew.

She looked at him now, sideways. He was fifty-two. He was heavy and grey and his hand was not quite steady on the glass. But he still had the long lashes, and the trick of looking up from under them, that had made every girl in the county stupid in 1962.

"The clocks," she said.

He went still.

"I looked at the Tompion in the hall this morning. The month-going one. It's Papa's copy. I knew it the moment I saw the dial; the silvering's too even. It hasn't had three hundred winters. And the bracket clock in the dining room. And the Knibb on the stairs." She kept her voice low. "I didn't look at the rest. I didn't want to know how many."

"When did you first see it?"

"Two years ago. At Easter. The little East in the morning room."

"And you said nothing."

"No."

"Why not?"

She could not answer him. She had asked herself the same thing. She had stood in front of that small clock for a quarter of an hour, knowing what she was looking at, and then she had gone in to luncheon and passed him the salt.

It was the habit of the house. That was all she could say for herself. One saw, at Compline, and one did not speak. She had seen her mother's lantern, and her brother's. She had seen him come up the lawn. Having kept silent about that, she had found that she had lost the right to open her mouth about anything else.

"I suppose I thought you must need it very badly," she said.

Edmund laughed, shortly. "That's what he'll say. You watch. If it ever comes out, that's what the old man will say. *I expect he wanted it more than I did.* It's the worst thing about this family. Nobody ever has the decency to be angry." He drank. "Well. It comes out tonight, or near enough. He's got a man in the house. A fat policeman in a blue suit, going round with photographs. He'll have the lot by dinner."

"What will you do?"

"I've made arrangements."

"What kind of arrangements?"

He turned his head and looked at her. She had the oddest impression that he was about to tell her. She thought that he wanted to, and that it was not only the clocks. Something else was just behind his teeth, something much older. His mouth opened.

"Immy," he said. "Do you ever—"

He stopped. She waited. She found that her hands had gone cold.

It was there. It was in the gunroom with them, as it had been in every room they had shared for twenty years. *I saw you come up the lawn.* She could have said it then, at noon, on a bench, with the coats looking on. She had only to open her mouth.

"Do I ever what?"

"Nothing." He looked away. The moment went, as it always had. "Do you ever think of getting married. That's all. You ought to. You're wasting yourself on dead Scotsmen."

"I'm forty-four."

"Mother was fifty-four." He said it very quietly, and then seemed to hear what he had said, and got up, too quickly, catching at the gun rack. "I hear young Lochinvar's come out of the west. Is that why you're here?"

"I came because Papa asked."

"Of course you did." He stood over her, swaying a little. He put out his hand and touched the white streak in her hair, just once, with one finger, as he had done when it first came, when she was fourteen and cried about it. "Go across, Immy. I mean it. Tomorrow, when the road dries. Go and knock at his door. One of us ought to have something out of that damned farm."

He went out. She heard his uneven step go away down the stone passage.

She sat on the bench. She thought: he knows that I know. He has always known. And he has just told me to go to Tom, as if he were giving me something back.

She thought that she had hated him for twenty years. She found, sitting there, that it was not so simple, and that she was crying.

* * *

The afternoon went. She kept away from the west windows.

She did what she had promised herself. She looked at the portraits in the dining room and found bloom on two of them. She cleaned her mother's silver brushes, on the kitchen table, with Dolly's whiting. Dolly herself was short and distracted and twice went up the back stairs with a covered plate and came down without it.

"Who's that for?"

"The cat," said Dolly.

"We haven't got a cat."

"We've one today."

At three she met Celia in the hall, arranging late roses in a bowl. Her sister-in-law looked up, and smiled, and the smile did not reach her eyes. She had been lovely once. She was still handsome, in a tired, guarded way, like a house with the shutters up.

"You look as though you had seen a ghost," Celia said.

"I've been looking through Grandfather's telescope."

"At Saltcote. Yes. Mrs Pask told me who was home." Celia snipped a stem. "I shouldn't, if I were you. This family's had quite enough out of that farm. Your mother. Now Edmund, with his caravans. You'd think there was nowhere else in England."

"Do you know what Edmund's arranged? For tonight?"

The scissors stopped.

"Why should he have arranged anything?"

"He told me he had."

"Did he." Celia put the rose into the bowl, very exactly. "Edmund arranges a great many things, darling. He arranged to make our fortune with a racehorse. Then he arranged it with a sea wall. I've given up asking." But her hand had begun to turn her wedding ring, round and round. "If he tells you any more, I should be glad to hear it. One likes to be kept informed. I am only his wife."

Imogen went up the stairs. On the first landing a girl in a dark blue dress was coming down with a tray. She was small and colourless and had a still, attentive face.

This would be the nurse.

"You're Miss Quaile," Imogen said. "You're the one who tells my father the truth."

The girl started so violently that the spoon rang on the tray. She stared. All the blood went out of her face.

"I'm sorry. I didn't mean to frighten you. I'm his daughter."

"I know," Miss Quaile said. Her voice was hardly above a whisper. "I know who you are. I beg your pardon. It was your eyes."

She went on down, holding the tray very level.

Imogen stood looking after her. *She tells him. He asks, and she tells him. Straight out.* The girl had been in the house three weeks. She had done every night what Imogen had failed to do in twenty years. It had cost her something, too, by the look of her.

She thought: I could go down to the Shop now. He is at his bench. I could say it in six words.

She went to her room instead, and lay on her bed, and watched the light go.

* * *

She put on the black dress. It was plain and well cut and she had owned it for ten years. She pinned up her hair. She did not look at herself for longer than it took.

The hall was full of firelight and people. She came down into it slowly. There was Fenn, who bowed. There was the vicar. There was old Octavia Prowse, enormous in black velvet, who took her by both shoulders and said, "You've your mother's bones. Why don't you dress like her? You look like a governess."

There was a tall pink young man, a cousin, who told her about a motor car. There was Kit, who kissed her cheek and said, "Aunt Immy. Thank God. Somebody sane." He had a girl with him, with a shaved head and a green silk dress, who looked round the hall at the clocks with frank delight. And there was Celia, coming down the stairs in the same green, and stopping, and going on.

There was a stranger by the wall with a glass. He was heavy and grey and watchful. This was the policeman. She went and spoke to him, since nobody else would. He told her that the Thorne made him feel poor.

"It makes everybody feel something," she said. And then, because Edmund was standing across the hall, and because she was so tired of saying nothing: "I clean paintings for a living, Mr Cade. I spend my days telling what's two hundred years old from what somebody put there last Tuesday. If you'd like a second opinion on anything in this house, you might ask me."

She saw him take it in. He had small, patient eyes.

It was the nearest she had come to speaking in twenty years. She did not know why she had done it. She thought that perhaps a thing which had been shut a long time must be opened a little way first, to see if the hinge would bear.

Then her father came in, on the nurse's arm, and stood at the Font, and stirred the watches with his finger. He made his old speech about the Act of Parliament. And at the end of it he looked round the hall with a puzzled face.

"Is Margot down?" he said.

Imogen was by the fire. She saw them all go still. She saw Edmund look into his glass.

She saw the nurse's lips part. The girl was going to tell him. She was going to say it, here, in front of the guests, in that clear low voice: *Mr Vane, your wife is dead.*

"She'll be along, Papa," Imogen said. "Come and sit down."

She did not know that she was going to. It came out of her mouth, the old lie, as easily as breathing. She saw Miss Quaile's eyes turn to her. There was no reproach in them. There was only a kind of recognition, as of one person noting which side another is on.

Her father nodded, satisfied, and let himself be led to a chair.

* * *

At dinner they put her beside Edmund.

She did not look at the empty place. She had sat at this table with that chair for twenty years of Easters and Augusts, and had learned to keep it at the edge of her eye. But tonight it was laid for the Hour, with the three glasses. Tonight she knew what dress hung in the wardrobe overhead, and she felt the chair on her skin all through the soup.

The talk went on round her. The pink cousin, on her left, told a story with a ferry in it. He was a nice boy. He had something in his pocket that rattled, and he kept one hand pressed over it. When she asked him gently how the wine trade was, he said, "Not awfully," and looked astonished at himself.

Her father carved the goose. She watched his hands.

"He could do that blindfold," she said to the cousin. "He used to, for us, at Christmas. Edmund could too. Edmund had the best hands of any of us."

She heard herself say *had*. On her right her brother filled his glass.

It was during the goose that she told him. She had not planned it. But she had seen a man through a telescope that morning who looked at this house as one looks at a debt. Edmund had made arrangements, and had been drinking since noon. She thought that he ought at least to know.

"Tom Garrow is at Saltcote," she said, very low.

His fork stopped.

"What?"

"He's been home a fortnight. For his father. I saw him this morning, in the yard."

"Where?" He had not lowered his voice. "Saw him where? Here?"

"Across the water. Through the glass. He buried Amos on Thursday. The funeral, Edmund. The day your notice came."

"He's got no business here." His face had gone dark. She could smell the whisky on him. "That farm's out at Lady Day. Fenn's served the notice. He can go back to his bloody cows."

"Edmund."

"Twenty years. And he comes back to stand at a graveside in a hat and look across at this house like—"

He stopped. The table had gone quiet. Even her father had looked up from his plate. Edmund picked up his glass and smiled round at them, the easy smile, the one he had always been able to find.

"A tenant of ours," he said, across the table, to the policeman. "Old Garrow, at the farm on the other side. Died a fortnight ago. The son's turned up from Canada to see what he can get."

"Lean man? Hat? Grey horse?" the policeman said. "I saw him this morning. By the road. He had a good look at me."

"I'll bet he did," said Edmund.

Imogen looked at her plate. She had a fork in her hand, and she kept hold of it.

*Like what?* She wanted to turn to her brother and ask it. *He looks across at this house like what, Edmund? What did you see in his face on Thursday, at the churchyard wall, that frightened you so?*

But she knew. Edmund had gone out onto the Wade twenty years ago after a woman who was walking to that farm, and the woman had not arrived. And an old man had waited at the far end with a lantern until the tide came. The old man was in the ground now. But the son had held his hand through the last night. *I don't know what was said.*

She thought: Tom knows something. Amos told him something, at the end. And Edmund is afraid of what it is.

She felt the cold go down her back.

* * *

Her father rose for the toast. He bowed to the empty chair. He said, "To absent time."

They stood. Imogen stood with them. She lifted her glass. She found that she could not say the words. She had said them every year of her childhood, in this room, with her mother laughing at the foot of the table. They had been a joke then. *Absent time, darlings; the hour we shan't get back till spring.*

Her lips moved. What she said, without sound, was *To Mother.*

Then her father said the other thing.

She heard it as one hears a sentence passed on someone else. *One of you has been robbing me.* She did not look at Edmund. She could feel him beside her, quite motionless, like a man standing in a river.

Her father's eyes went round the table. They came to her and rested, pale and clear. She looked back at him. She thought: he does not know me from Mother tonight, and he does not know his own son is a thief. And she had known both, for two years, and had passed the salt.

*I shall know which of you it is by one o'clock.*

She turned her head then. She looked at the place on his right, at the folded napkin, the three glasses with the candles in them, and the chair drawn a little back.

*You started this,* she said to it, silently. *You made the Hour. You stood in the hall every year and made us say what we thought. And the one year that it mattered, you were the only one who was going to tell the truth, and you never got the chance. We've none of us said a true word in this house since.*

*What am I to do?*

The chair did not answer. Her father sat down, and asked what was on his plate.

* * *

In the drawing room she went to the window.

She could not have sat with them. She pulled the curtain back and got into the window seat, with her knees up, as she had as a girl, and put her forehead to the glass.

This was the west side. The wind had risen with the dark, as she had seen him read that it would. It came across the flats in long gusts and shook the sash. There was no moon yet. There was nothing out there but the black. The tide had been over the Wade for more than an hour. Somewhere in front of her, under five feet of sea, was the road, and the Rill, and the Perch on its legs with the water going by.

Behind her Kit began to play.

It was the Schubert. He played it every year. Her mother had liked it, and had made Edmund learn it as a boy, and Edmund had taught it to his son. It was the only thing she had ever known Edmund to hand on. It went round and round, the same sad easy tune, like water going over a weir.

She looked for the light at Saltcote. There was none. The whole shore was dark. Then she remembered that the lamps in the room behind her had gone out, a while since, and that someone had said the whole of Thorney was down.

So he was sitting in the dark, too. With a child asleep, somewhere. With a folded paper in his shirt.

The music stopped, and she heard her father's voice.

"Thank you, Kit. She has gone up. She always goes up after the Schubert, and takes her shoes off, and comes down for the Hour. Imogen, did you see her go?"

She had her face to the glass. She was not thinking of what he asked. She was thinking of grey satin shoes on a cold floor, side by side.

"Yes, Papa," she said.

"Was she tired?"

"A little."

It was true. She had seen her go. She had watched her mother's lantern cross that lawn and go down to the Wade, twenty years ago tonight, from behind a vine. Her mother had been tired. She had been fifty-four, and had waited ten years.

Nobody spoke. The fire ticked. After a while people moved about, and somebody brought lamps. She did not turn round.

She did not know how long she sat there. An hour, perhaps. She heard Edmund come in, and go out, and come in again. She heard him yawn. She heard the clocks tell the half hour after ten.

It was a few minutes after that that she saw the light.

It was very small. It was on the far shore, low down, where the farm was. For a moment she thought that the electricity had come back. But it was the wrong colour. It was yellow, and it shook.

It moved. It came out from where the buildings must be, and along, and stopped. It stayed still for perhaps a minute. She could see it tremble in the wind.

Then it came down the ramp.

She knew that ramp. She knew to the foot where the land ended. The light came down the slope off the sea wall, steadily, and it reached the bottom, where the Wade began. It did not stop there.

It came on. It came straight out from the shore, toward her, along the line where the road lay. It was a yard or two above the water. It rose and dipped, slowly, with a rhythm that she knew.

Imogen put both her hands flat on the glass.

It was a lantern, on a horse. Someone was riding a horse out along the Wade.

She could not get her breath. The tide was at the three-quarter flood. There would be four feet of water on the road already, and a current setting north through the Rill that would take a man off his legs. No one crossed at that state. It had never been done. The Garrows themselves, who had farmed that shore since the wars with Napoleon, had a saying about it. *An hour and a half before, the Wade belongs to God.*

He knew that. He had known the tide all his life as other men know when they are hungry. He could not have made a mistake. He had looked at that water, in the dark, and smelled the wind, and known exactly how deep it was, and he had put the mare into it.

"Oh, you fool," she whispered. "Oh, my dear. Go back."

The light came on. She watched it as she had watched nothing in her life. It went under, once, behind a wave, and she heard herself cry out, and it rose again. It was a third of the way. It was nearly half.

It reached the place where the Rill must be, and it stopped.

She knew what he was doing. He was sitting the mare at the edge of the deep channel, with the sea up to her chest, letting her feel the ground go. There were six feet in the Rill at this state, and it ran like a mill race. The Clapper would be under. A horse could not walk it. She would have to swim, and be carried down, and find her feet again on the other side, if there was another side. If she was a hand's breadth out, she would miss the road, and there was nothing beyond but soft mud that would hold a horse until the water closed over her.

The mare was twenty years old.

The light stood still. It stood for so long that Imogen thought he had turned back, or was gone. Her nails were digging into her palms.

Then it dropped. It went down almost to the water. It moved sideways, quickly, to the right, as though something had taken hold of it and were sweeping it away toward the sea. It went thirty yards. It went forty.

And it steadied. It rose, slowly, a foot, and another. It began to come on again, lower now, and slower, slanting back toward the line.

He was through. Mab had found the road.

Imogen leaned her forehead on the glass and wept. She did not make a sound. The tears ran down and she let them.

She understood, watching that small stubborn light come up out of the sea, what she had been refusing to understand all day. He was not coming to deliver a letter. A man does not swim a horse through the Rill at night to speak to a solicitor. He was coming to the house where her brother was. He was coming with twenty years in him, and whatever his father had said. A Garrow put things away somewhere and waited, and then one day he came for them.

And she understood the other thing. She would go down to him. Nothing that he could say to her would be worse than the twenty years she had spent at a curtain.

The light went out of sight behind the point of the island, where the Wade came ashore below the orchard. The clock on the mantel behind her told twenty minutes past eleven.

She did not move at once. She could not have stood. She sat with her hands in her lap and got her breath, while the room went on behind her as if nothing had happened. She heard her brother say that he would have a look at the generator. She heard his step go out. It was half past.

Overhead, along the gallery, there was a sudden sound of running. It was light and quick, like a child's feet. She raised her eyes to the ceiling. It ran the whole length of the floor and stopped at the far end, above her head, where the portrait hung.

She thought, without surprise: yes. I hear you. I'm coming.

She turned from the window. The nurse was standing in front of her, very white, saying something. Would she sit by her father for five minutes. He was asleep; he ought not to be left.

"Yes," Imogen said. "Of course. Go."

She did not hear what she had promised. She saw the girl take a candle and go out. She saw her father asleep in the wing chair, with the rug to his chin. She saw Edmund's empty place by the fire, and Celia standing at the door like a woman listening at a keyhole.

She had the key in the pocket of her dress. She had put it there when she changed, not knowing why. Her fingers closed on it.

She went out of the drawing room quickly, without looking back. In the hall a tall young man came running and all but knocked her down. She hardly saw him. She went up the stairs with a candle in one hand and the old iron key in the other, and turned along the gallery, toward the lamp at the end.

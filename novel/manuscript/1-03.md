## 3. Lights Out

The piano stopped in the middle of a bar. Then it went on. I've thought since that that told you most of what you needed to know about Kit Vane. The lights die on him, and he stops for the length of a breath, and then he finishes the piece in the dark with nobody able to see his hands.

I stood in the passage with the door handle in my fist and waited for my eyes. There's dark and there's dark. In a town it's never the real thing; there's always a street lamp somewhere, or a glow in the sky. This was the real thing, a hundred yards of sea on every side and a gale, and it came down on that house like a lid. All I had was the noise. The wind. The piano. And the clocks, which I'd managed to stop hearing for the best part of an hour, coming back up out of the walls, every one of them, to let me know they didn't run on electricity and never had.

I opened the door.

The drawing room had a fire, and that was all it had. It was a big room and the fire lit the front half of the people in it and left the rest to guess. I picked them out. The old man in a wing chair with a rug over his knees and the nurse on a stool beside him. Dame Octavia asleep, or dead, on a sofa with her mouth open. The vicar next to her, beating time on his knee, and not the time the boy was playing. Fenn standing. The daughter in the window seat, turned away from all of them, with the curtain pulled back in one hand, looking out at nothing. The cropped girl sitting on the floor against the piano's leg, her green dress pooled round her boots. And the boy at the keyboard, a thin dark shape, with his head down.

No Edmund Vane. No Celia. No pink nephew.

"That's the third time since Michaelmas," Fenn said, to nobody. "There's a transformer at Thorney that comes down if a gull sits on it."

"It's of no consequence," said Leo Vane. "Let him finish."

So the boy finished. It took another two or three minutes. I don't know music; my wife used to say I'd got a tin ear to go with the tin heart. But I stood against the wall at the back of that room, and I'll say that it got to me, that tune. It keeps coming round to the same place and it's a little sadder every time it gets there. Somebody had liked it. Somebody who'd stood for her picture in grey silk about to say a funny thing. He played it as though she were sitting where the firelight stopped.

When it was over nobody clapped. The old man said, "Thank you, Kit," and then, in the quiet, quite clearly: "She has gone up. She always goes up after the Schubert, and takes her shoes off, and comes down for the Hour. Imogen, did you see her go?"

The daughter didn't turn round from the window. "Yes, Papa."

"Was she tired?"

"A little."

I got out. I'd come to that house for money and I'd take money for most things. But I wasn't paid to stand and listen to that.

* * *

In the hall they were doing what people do. The big girl, Gail, was going round with a taper lighting more candles, with her tongue between her teeth. The cook had come up from the kitchen in her apron with an oil lamp in each fist and was setting them down, one on the table by the Font and one at the foot of the stairs, and telling the girl she was dripping wax on a floor that had been there since Henry the Eighth. The nephew was halfway up a stepladder for no reason I could see. He had a candle in his hand and he was explaining about fuses.

"It isn't the fuses, Mr Toby," the cook said. "It's the whole Ness. It's the whole of Thorney, come to that, you've only to look out the scullery. Get down off my steps."

"I only thought, if one had a look at the box—"

"You'll have a look at the box and I'll have you on my floor with your neck broke, and a goose carcass to boil. Down."

He got down. I liked that woman. I never exchanged ten words with her, but I liked her. She'd a face like a loaf and she ran that house the way a sergeant runs a company whose officers are all drunk.

I asked her for a light of my own. She looked me over, shoes last, the same as the old man had, and reached into a cupboard under the stairs and brought out a hurricane lamp, the kind with the wire cage, and lit it, and gave it me.

"You'll want that if you're going out," she said. "And you'll want a coat. It's blowing a hooley."

"What makes you think I'm going out?"

"You're not the sort that goes to bed," said the cook, and went back down her stairs. Mrs Pask, she was called. I've sometimes thought that if I'd spent the evening in her kitchen I'd have known more by midnight than I found out all night in the rain. But she wasn't on Fenn's list, and I've told you about lists.

I stood the lamp on the floor and thought about where a man would go.

He hadn't been in the drawing room for the music. Nor had she. I didn't know how long either of them had been gone; I'd been in that little room with her until a couple of minutes before the lights went, and I'd given her a start down the passage. So she could be anywhere. Powdering her nose. But him. The boat was for a quarter to two. It wasn't half past eleven yet. If I had a box to carry half a mile along a sea wall in a gale, with a bad knee and a skinful, and wanted to do it without the family at the windows, I wouldn't leave it till a quarter past one, with the old man already in the hall for his ceremony and everybody up and about. I'd do it early. I'd do it when I had the chance, and leave the thing somewhere handy, out of the house, and come back in and be seen.

And somebody had just handed him the chance. The lights were out, and everyone he was afraid of was in one room, listening to Schubert.

I picked up the lamp and went down the hall to the library.

The fire in there had burned low. It gave enough to see the bay of the window and the mahogany table and the square shape on the table with the green baize cover over it. Just as he'd left it at four. Just as the old man had it every night, he'd said, like a budgerigar.

I listened to the room. You get so you can. A room with a hundred thousand books in it is a quiet place, they soak it up. I could hear the fire settle, and the rain on the glass, and one clock, a slow one, somewhere over by the door.

One clock.

I went over to the table and put my ear down by the baize. Then I took hold of the corner of the cloth, between finger and thumb, and lifted it.

The glass case was there. The brass frame, the lock with its key. There was nothing in it. There was a square of darker wood on the tabletop where something had stood for years and kept the light off, and four small dents.

I put the cloth back the way it had been. I did it carefully. I don't know why. I think it was respect for a neat job.

* * *

It was no night to be out in, and I'll skip how I felt about it. I got my raincoat from the room upstairs and went out through a side door by the gunroom, into a yard, and the wind took me and stood me against the wall and went through my pockets. The rain wasn't falling. It was going past, level, like something thrown. I had the lamp turned down to a bead and held low against my leg.

I hadn't walked the island. I should have done that first, before the clocks; I'd have done it in the old days. But I'd looked at it from the library window for a while, and from my own, and I'd a fair idea. The house faced east. In front of it there was a sweep of gravel and then a long block of stables and outhouses with an arch in the middle and the clock tower over the arch. Behind the stables the ground fell to a creek. They called that the fleet. North of everything, on the far side of a spinney of bent trees, was the landing place, the Hythe, and you got to it along the top of the sea wall. The spinney was between. If I was carrying a box from the house to the Hythe, the spinney was where I'd break the journey.

I went along the side of the house and stopped at the corner.

There was a light. A long way off it seemed, though it can't have been above eighty yards: a little yellow light, low to the ground, moving along the front of the stable block with a hitch in it. It went up and came down, up and came down, like a boat on a swell. I've tailed a lot of men. You get to know a walk. I didn't need to see anything but the lamp to know which leg was bad.

He was carrying something in front of him with both arms, and the lamp was hung off his wrist or his little finger, and banged against the load. He went the length of the stable block and round the end of it and was gone.

I gave him a count of twenty. Then I crossed the gravel after him, on the grass edge where it didn't crunch, with my lamp shut right down and my free hand on my hat.

I'd been in the open ten seconds when the clock in the tower went off over my head. There was a whirr first, and a clank, and then a bell. It was a small bell and a quick one, two notes, *ting-tang*, and then again, *ting-tang*. Half past the hour. I looked up. There was a bit of a moon somewhere behind the cloud, enough to make the sky a shade less black than the tower. I saw the clock face, and above the clock face, under a little pitched roof, there were two people standing.

I nearly dropped the lamp. I'm not ashamed of it. You come round a building in the dark at half past eleven on the trail of a man with a million pounds, and you look up and there are two figures forty feet over your head, a man and a woman, as big as life, looking down at you. One of them moved. The man's arm came down and the bell went *tang*, and I understood. They were the things the boy and girl had been dressing. Wooden. A man with a hammer who did the quarters. A woman on the other side of the bell, bigger, with her hammer held up by her shoulder, waiting for the hour. Somebody had hung a black jacket on him, and a white shirt-front, and a bow. On her there was a shawl that the wind had got hold of and was streaming out sideways like hair.

I didn't like them. I'll say that now and get it over. I had no reason. But I didn't care for the pair of them, stood up there in the weather in somebody's cast-off clothes, hitting a bell every fifteen minutes for two hundred years whether there was anyone to hear it or not. The woman especially. She had a painted face, I could just make out, and a long painted gown under the shawl, grey. She looked patient.

The little man finished. The whirring ran down. I went on round the end of the stables with the back of my neck feeling like a dartboard.

Behind the stable block there was a track going down between a wall and the trees. I could see his light again. It had gone in among the trunks, and it wasn't moving now; it was sitting still, about thirty yards in, and throwing the shadows of branches out across the track. I got in under a holly and waited. I had the rain down my collar and a twig in my ear. I've spent some of the best years of my life like that.

He was four or five minutes. I heard iron: a gate, a hinge that wanted oil. Then nothing, and the light got dim, as if it had gone down a hole. Then it came back. The gate again. And he came out of the trees onto the track with the lamp in his hand and his arms empty.

He passed within six feet of me. He was breathing hard and he was talking to himself, low, the way a drunk does who's concentrating. I caught some of it. He said, "Right. Right. That's that, then." He stopped and stood, swaying a bit, looking back up at the black bulk of the house with its few lit windows. He stood there a good half minute in the rain, without a hat, a big man in a dinner jacket with his hair plastered down, and then he said something else. He said it quite distinctly.

He said, "Sorry, Ma."

Then he went off up the track toward the house, with his limp.

I've given that to you as I got it. I didn't know what to make of it then and I couldn't make anything of it for a long time. He'd just put his father's clock in a hole in the ground for a moneylender's boat. If he'd said *sorry, Dad*, I'd have understood it. I put it down to the drink. I put a lot of things down to the drink that night, his and mine.

* * *

When his light had gone I turned mine up and went into the spinney.

It wasn't a hole in the ground. It was a hump in it: a mound about as high as my shoulder, grown over with ivy and old elder, and in the side of the mound a brick arch, and in the arch a gate. Iron bars, close together, the kind they have on a vault. It was standing a few inches open. Behind it there was a short passage, brick, with a second door of wood at the end that stood wide. I put my hand on the gate and it swung in like a bank's. It had a lock on it that was half a lock and half a latch: a tongue with a spring behind it, bevelled, that would ride over the catch when you pulled the gate to and drop home on its own. I worked it with my thumb. You could open it from outside with a key and from inside not at all. I've seen the same thing on a cell.

I took note of that. I'd have done better to take more.

I went in. It was cold the moment I got past the wooden door, a cold that had nothing to do with the night; that was October, and this was January. The passage came out on a little brick landing, and my lamp showed me a round room like the inside of an egg, domed over, all brick, sweating. I was standing at the top of it. A flight of steps went down the curve of the wall, steep, without a rail, maybe a dozen of them, to a floor I couldn't properly see. There was straw down there, and sacking, and something that gave back the light, wet and grey, heaped. Ice. They had an ice house. In 1994. I suppose if you live where the electricity goes off whenever a seagull sits down, you keep your options open.

On the landing, just inside, against the wall, there was a wooden box.

It was a tea chest, more or less, a plywood crate with tin edges, the lid laid on loose and a length of sash cord round it. He hadn't even nailed it. I set the lamp down and got on my knees for the second time that day. I undid his knot. It was a fisherman's knot of some kind, done drunk, in the dark, by a man with a bad leg, and it was a perfect knot. I'd been told he had the hands.

I lifted the lid.

He'd packed it in a blanket, a grey army one, and under the blanket in a pillowcase. I folded the stuff back. The lamp shone on brass, and on glass, and on a white dial about as wide as a saucer with a painted moon looking out of a slot.

It was still going. He hadn't stopped it. I knelt there on the wet bricks with the cold coming up off the ice, and I listened to one million two hundred thousand pounds ticking in a tea chest, and I'll admit that for the space of one breath a thing went through my head that had no business there. I could have had the Granada. I knew a man in Southend who knew a man. I'm telling you because I said I'd tell it straight. Then I thought about the Wade, six feet under, and about the kind of people who buy that kind of clock, and what they'd do to the kind of man I am. And I thought about the old boy at his bench saying *I should like to know why*. I put the pillowcase back and the blanket over it, and the lid on, and I tied his knot again as well as I could, which wasn't as well as he had.

I'm not a religious man. I don't claim that last thought was the one that did it.

So. I had him. I had Edmund Vane with the clock in his arms, and the place he'd put it, and from his wife the time of the boat and the name of the man who'd sent it. It was twenty to twelve and I'd done what Fenn was paying two hundred a day for. I could walk back to the house and find the solicitor and tell him, and at one o'clock by the hall, with the door open and the family gathered, the old man could ask his son why.

And I'd promised a woman in a green dress, twenty minutes earlier, that I'd come and tell her first.

I stood on the landing and smoked a cigarette over it, with the lamp at my feet. It ought to have been simple. One of them had hired me straight and the other one was lying to me about something and wore scent. I looked down the steps into the well, at the straw and the wet grey heap. I thought, it's a long way down for a man who's had a few. And then I thought about what she'd said. *One shove. Nobody'd ever know.*

I went out and left the gate the way I'd found it. A few inches open. That's the kind of thing I notice about myself afterwards. I was so careful not to disturb anything.

* * *

I didn't go back by the yard. I wanted to see the Hythe while I had the chance, and the ground between. So I pushed through the spinney to the far side and came out on the sea wall.

It's an earth bank with a path along the top, and that night there was nothing on the other side of it but noise. I climbed up, and I had to hold my hat on with both hands and lean. The water was right there. In the afternoon it had been a quarter of a mile off across the mud, and now it was slapping at the stonework eight or ten feet below my shoes, black, with the white coming off the tops of it and blowing in my face. You could taste it. The whole of that flat country I'd driven across was gone. It was the North Sea, all the way to the wall I was standing on, and the wall didn't seem a great deal to be standing on.

I looked for the mainland. There should have been the lights of Thorney, but the cook had said the whole village was out. There was nothing. No, there was one. It was a single yellow light, low down, a mile off perhaps, hard to say.

It was moving.

I watched it. I thought at first it was a boat, and my stomach went tight, because a boat meant Sears's man and he was two hours early. But it wasn't a boat's light. A boat's light swings and this one bobbed. It came on slowly and steadily in a dead straight line, and it took me a while to understand why the line was so straight, and then I did. It was coming along the Wade. Somebody was on that road, with a lantern, in the dark, with six feet of sea on top of it.

I'm a townsman. I stood on that wall and I couldn't make it mean anything. A man can't walk in six feet of water. A car can't drive in it. And still the light came on, a yard or two above where the road had to be, rising and dipping, patient. Once it stopped for a while, about where I judged the middle was, where the dip and the little bridge and the black box on legs were, and I thought, that's it, he's gone. Then it came on again, lower, and slower.

I never saw what carried it. The rain came down like a curtain between us and when it lifted the light had gone, behind the point of the island or out for good, I couldn't tell.

I'd seen a man that afternoon by the road with a big grey horse, lean, in a hat, who'd looked at my car and then at the house. I'd heard his name at dinner, and I'd seen what it did to a woman's face. I remembered all that standing on the wall. But I couldn't see what it had to do with me. I was there about a clock. Twenty years in the job, and I'd got it into my head that there was only the one story going on in that house that night. I thought I was in it, and everybody else was scenery.

It was five to twelve by the Tissot. I went back along the wall toward the house. I didn't get as far as the Hythe. I never have seen it by daylight.

* * *

I came in by the yard, and I was passing the dining-room windows when I saw them.

Somebody had put a lamp in there, on the sideboard. The curtains hadn't been pulled across that end, and I stood in the wet about ten feet off, in the dark, and looked into the room like a man at the pictures. The table had been cleared to the wood. On the sideboard, by the lamp, was a tray with decanters and glasses on it, and standing at the tray, with her back to the window, was a fair-haired woman in a green dress.

It was Celia Vane, I knew that, there was no mistaking the hair that time. She had a glass in her left hand, a balloon glass, a big one. With her right she was doing something to it that I couldn't see, because her shoulder was in the way. Her elbow was up. She put something down on the tray, a small thing, dark, and picked up a decanter and poured, and swirled the glass round, twice, the way you'd warm a brandy. She lifted it and looked through it at the lamp.

Then the door of the room opened and her husband came in.

He'd got a dry jacket on. His hair was still wet. He said something, and she turned round with the glass in her hand and that smile on, and held it out to him. I've seen a lot of wives hand a lot of husbands a drink. It was the nicest thing I'd seen either of them do. He took it. He said something else, and laughed, and put his free hand for a moment on the side of her neck, under the hair, and she let him. She tipped her head against it. You'd have said they were fond of one another. I think now that you'd have been right, and that was the hell of it.

He stood the glass down on the sideboard to get a cigarette out. And the nephew came in.

The nephew came in backwards, the way he did everything. He had a candle. He was talking over his shoulder to somebody in the passage, and he backed into the room, and turned round, saw them, and jumped. He started apologising. I could see his mouth going. He made for the tray, and he had another glass in his hand, a balloon like the first, with something in it; and he was trying to pour himself a drink, hold a candle, and explain something to Edmund Vane with gestures, all at once, and he put his glass down to do it. He put it down beside the other one.

Edmund Vane lit his cigarette and laughed at him and clapped him on the back, so that the candle went out. There was a bit of business with matches. The old vicar wandered in at the door, smiling, with his hand out, and the nephew, who was a well-brought-up boy, picked a glass off the sideboard and put it in the old man's hand. Then Edmund Vane picked up a glass, and drank some, and made a face, and drank some more.

I'm describing what I saw through a window in a gale, at ten feet, at midnight. I saw two balloon glasses go down side by side, and two get picked up again. I couldn't tell you which was which. I didn't try. I didn't know there was any reason to. I was looking at her. She'd stepped back from the tray when the nephew came blundering in, back against the panelling by the corner clock, and she was standing there with her hands behind her, and she was watching the glasses. Just the glasses. Her eyes went from the one to the other and her face was the face she'd had on the fourth stair.

And the small dark thing was gone off the tray. She had it behind her back.

I thought: aspirin. I swear to you I did. Or one of those powders women take. I'd watched a woman put something in a man's drink and I was standing in a flowerbed with the rain running off my hat, and I thought, she's given him something for his head.

I've been asked how I could have been a detective. That's fair.

* * *

I should have gone in then. If I'd gone in, and found Fenn, and said what I knew, the rest of it would have gone differently. I don't know that it would have gone better. I've stopped being sure that there was a better. But it would have been somebody else's night, and I'd have been out of it.

I didn't go in. What I did was this. I had it worked out that Edmund Vane would have to come back out of the house at about half past one, by my watch, to move his box from the ice house to the landing. I wanted to be there before him, somewhere I could see the track and the spinney both and not be seen, and somewhere dry, because I'd had about enough. I wanted a place to sit for an hour and a half with a view. And I'd noticed, coming round the end of the stable block, a door at the foot of the clock tower.

I went back across the gravel. The two up on the tower were only shapes. I didn't look at them long.

The door was a plank door with a thumb latch and it wasn't locked. Inside it smelled of leather and horse and mice. I turned the lamp up. It was the room where they'd kept the harness when there were horses to wear it: pegs round the walls, a couple of saddles on trees, going green, a bench, a heap of old rugs, a corn bin. In the far corner there was a wooden stair like a ladder going up through a hole in the ceiling, and from up the hole, very slow and heavy, came a sound like somebody cutting bread with a saw. *Tock*. And a long wait. *Tock*. The works of the tower clock, up over my head.

There was a little window on the side toward the trees. It would do. I'd be able to see his lamp when he came.

I remember what I did next quite clearly. I set my own lamp on the corn bin. I took my hat off and shook it. I got out the half-bottle I'd had in my raincoat pocket since Leigh, because it was the first chance I'd had, and I unscrewed the cap. I looked at my watch. It was ten past twelve.

I heard the floor behind me, one board, and I started to turn round.

I've been hit before. I was hit with a bottle in Stratford in 1971 and a length of scaffold pole in the Balls Pond Road. I know how it's done by people who do it for a living, and this wasn't that. It was all wrong. It was too hard, and too high, and it was swung flat instead of chopped, with the whole body behind it. That's how a kid does it who's never hit anybody and is so frightened that he shuts his eyes. I got an arm halfway up. I saw the lamp, and the wire cage on it, and the whisky going out of the neck of the bottle in a long brown rope and turning over in the air. I thought, that's a waste.

The stair in the corner tilted up and stood on its end. Something like a door slammed, a long way inside my head.

Then I was lying with my face on a horse rug that smelled of ammonia, and I couldn't get my eyes to open, and somebody was saying, a long way above me, in a high cracked whisper, a young voice, scared half out of its wits:

"You tell him. You tell Sears he'll get his money. You leave my father *alone*."

Feet went away from me across boards. A door banged. Up in the tower the clock cut another slice.

I thought, I'll get up in a minute. I thought I'd just lie there till it stopped going round.

That was at ten past twelve. I know it, because the glass on my watch got cracked right across when I went down, and I looked at it just before, and the hands were the first thing I saw when I could see anything again. I've had to do sums about that night ever since. Everybody who was there has. That one's mine. Thirty-five minutes.

## I. The Crossing

Dr Imrie says that I am to begin at the beginning and that I am not to explain. I have told him that I do not know where the beginning is. He says that in that case I am to begin with the first thing I saw, and so I shall begin with the water.

I came to Compline on the first day of October, in the afternoon, by the bus from Maldon. It set me down at the end of a village street with my one case and went away. I had been told that I should be met. No one was there. There was a church with a squat flint tower and a row of cottages with their doors shut, and beyond the last cottage the road ran up onto a green bank and seemed to end in the sky.

I walked up it. I am from Whitby, and I was brought up within the sound of the sea, and I had thought when I took the post that the sea at least would be no stranger to me. But our sea at home is a thing that stands up. It comes at the cliff and breaks, and you may hate it or love it, but you know where it is. From the top of that bank I looked for the sea and could not find it. There was a grey plain running out to a grey horizon, without a wave on it or a rock, and I could not tell what was land and what was not. A line of thin poles went away across it, getting smaller. There were birds crying. It was absolutely flat, and yet as I stood I had the plain sensation that I was looking down into something, as one looks down into a well.

Far out, at the end of the poles, there was a dark rise with trees on it, and among the trees a roof.

I have asked myself since whether I felt anything then. It would be easy to say that I did. I have read the kind of book in which a young woman comes to a house and knows at the first sight of its chimneys that she will suffer there, and I despised those books when I was a girl, because it seemed to me that a person with any sense would turn round. I can only say that I did not turn round. I had eleven pounds, and a reference, and nowhere else to go. And I will be truthful, since that is the whole purpose of this. What I felt, looking at that roof across the flats, was not dread. It was relief. I thought: it is a long way from anywhere, and there will be no one there who knows.

There was a notice at the top of the slope, and I read it through, to the end, as I read everything. It gave the hours when the road was closed. At the foot, in red, it said that all times were Greenwich Mean Time. I looked at my mother's watch. I worked out that I had the best part of three hours, and then I picked up my case and went down the slope onto the sea bed and began to walk.

They call it the Wade. It is nine hundred yards, and I counted my steps for the first part of it, from an old habit of the wards, where one counts everything. The surface was shingle and broken stone, and there was weed on it, still wet. Either side the mud lay shining, with small creeks winding through, and in the creeks the water was moving, though I could not have said which way. No sound came from the land behind me. I have never been in a place so wide that felt so much like a room.

Halfway over, the road dipped to cross a deeper channel on a bridge of planks. Beside the bridge stood a wooden box on four tall legs, black with tar, with a ladder against it and a small shut door. I stopped and looked at it. I knew what it must be for. I thought of someone climbing that ladder in the dark, with the water coming, and sitting in that box through the night to listen to it go by underneath.

I thought: someone has done that. I do not know why I was so sure. But I had been a nurse for five years. When one comes onto a ward at night one learns to tell, without being told, in which bed there has been a death. It is nothing one sees. The bed is made up fresh, and it has a kind of attention about it. That box on its legs had it.

I walked on rather faster. I did not count any more.

* * *

The house does not show itself until one is almost upon it. The road comes up off the flats between banks, and turns, and there is a sweep of gravel, and one sees it all at once and too close, as one sees a face that has come up beside one in a crowd.

It is a long house, and low, and it is not all of one age. The middle is very old, of narrow brick the colour of dried blood, with stone round the windows. A plain grey front has been put across this, like a mask, with tall sashes and a pillared door. At one end a later wing runs back, with pointed gables. There are a great many chimneys. The trees round it all lean one way, away from the sea, with their branches streaming, as though they had been caught in the act of running from it and fixed there.

Opposite the front door, across the gravel, is a range of stables with an arch through the middle, and above the arch a square tower with a clock. I looked at the clock, naturally, to see the time. It said ten minutes to three. My watch said ten to four.

I thought it had stopped. Then, while I looked, I saw the long hand move. And above the clock face, under a little tiled roof, I saw that there were two people standing, and looking down at me.

I have been told since that everyone who comes to Compline has that moment, and that the family think it rather a joke. They are figures of carved oak, as large as life, a man and a woman. They stand either side of a bell, and each has a hammer. The man strikes the quarters and the woman the hours. They have been there two hundred years and are called Jack and Jenny, and children are held up to wave to them.

I did not wave. I stood with my case on the gravel and looked at the woman. She is the taller. She wears a gown painted grey, with long sleeves, and her face is painted too, though the weather has taken most of it, so that there is only the suggestion of eyes. Her right arm is lifted, with the hammer, as high as her shoulder. She does not look at the bell. She looks out, over the yard, toward the house and the Wade beyond it, and I thought she had the look of a woman who has been waiting for someone longer than is reasonable, and who has made up her mind to it.

I do not know how long I should have stood there. A door banged, and a girl of sixteen or seventeen in an apron came running round the end of the house and said that I must be the nurse, and that they had been going to send Kit with the car, only nobody had told Kit. She took my case from me. Her name was Gail. She said that I was to come in by the kitchen, since the front was only opened for company, and that her auntie would give me my tea.

"Does it always say the wrong time?" I asked her. I meant the tower.

She looked up at it, and then at me, as though I had asked whether the grass was always green.

"That's the right time," she said. "That's Compline time. It's all the rest that's wrong."

* * *

I was given my tea in the kitchen by a Mrs Pask. She was the cook, and I came to respect her more than anyone else in that house, though I do not think she ever quite approved of me. She was a broad, short, silent woman with strong arms. She watched me eat a piece of bread and butter as though she were making up her mind how long I should last.

"You'll have been told about the old gentleman," she said.

I said that Mr Fenn had told me he was eighty-one, and that his memory was failing, and that he walked at night.

"He walks," said Mrs Pask. "He don't sleep above three hours, and then he's up. He'll be all over. The Shop, that's his workshop, down under. The long gallery. Out to the stable to wind the tower. Out on the wall. It's the wall we don't like. He's no notion of the cold, and he's no notion where his feet are, and there's a fifteen-foot drop to the saltings. You're to keep him off the wall."

"How am I to keep him off it?"

"I don't know," she said. "That's what you're for."

She told me the hours. I was to sit with him from ten at night until six in the morning. He had a sleeping draught at ten, one spoonful in water, from a bottle that I must keep on a tray and let no one else touch. In the daytime I might sleep until noon. After that I was to make myself useful with the papers.

"What papers are those?"

"Mrs Vane's. Her letters and such. There's a deal of them, in the morning-room desk and in boxes, and they've never been touched. Mr Vane wants them put in order. He says she'll be vexed to come home and find them in a muddle." Mrs Pask looked at me steadily. "She's dead. She's been dead twenty years this month. You're not to tell him so."

"Does he not know it?"

"Some days. Some days he knows it as well as you or me, and them's the bad days. Other days she's gone up to change, or she's over at the farm, or she's just this minute left the room. He'll ask you has she come in. You say, not yet, sir. That's what we all say. It does no harm, and it saves him having it to hear new."

I said nothing. I had my own opinion about that. I have been a nurse, and I have seen what is done to old people by those who lie to them out of kindness, and I had made a private rule that I would not do it. But I had been in the house for a quarter of an hour, and it was not the time.

"And you'll want to put your watch right," said Mrs Pask. "Back an hour. Mr Edmund'll tell you the same. You'll go mad else."

I took off my mother's watch and put it back an hour, there at the kitchen table. It was a small thing to do. I have thought about it a great deal. I think that it was the moment I came to belong to that house, and that I did it with my own fingers, because I was asked.

I should say that it is not only a matter of the clocks. One can alter a watch. But for the whole of the three weeks that followed I lived an hour behind the rest of the country, and I found that it did something to me that I had not foreseen. I had no wireless. I saw no newspaper until it was a day old. When I thought of my mother in Whitby sitting down to her tea, I had to remember that she had sat down to it an hour ago; and by degrees I stopped thinking of her. It was as though the house stood a little way back from the present, in a place that the present had already left. Everything that happened in the world had happened by the time it reached us. I do not know how to say it better than that. At Compline it was always slightly too late.

* * *

There are a hundred and eleven clocks in the house. I was told the number on my first evening by Mr Edmund Vane, who said that I should not try to count them, as it had driven a housemaid out of her mind in 1931.

I heard them before I saw them. Gail took me up by the back stairs to a small room on the first floor, next to the old gentleman's, and left me to unpack, and when she had shut the door I stood still and listened. I had been hearing it, I found, ever since I came into the kitchen, without attending to it, as one hears rain. It came through the walls and up through the floor. It was a ticking; but there were so many, at so many different paces, that they made together a kind of soft continuous rustle with a pulse in it. I thought of a great many people in a dark room, whispering.

And every quarter of an hour the house spoke. I learned to wait for it. One clock would begin, somewhere below, and then another on the stairs, and a third, in no order, each with its own voice. There were small sharp ones and deep slow ones and one, in the long gallery, that had a cracked note like a cough. No two struck together. It would take a full minute for the hour to pass through the house from one end to the other, like a rumour, and when the last of them had done there was always a moment's pause before the tower clock answered from across the yard. One could hear the big bell, and one knew that it was the grey woman, bringing down her arm.

I thought, that first night, that I should never sleep in it. As it happened I was not required to.

I met the family at dinner. I shall not say much of them here, since they will come in their places. There was a Mrs Edmund Vane, who was fair and handsome and tired, and who spoke to me kindly and forgot me the moment her head was turned. There was her husband. And at the head of the table there was the old gentleman, my patient, to whom I was presented as "Miss Quaile, who has come to help with Mother's papers."

He rose. He was very tall. He took my hand in both of his and bent over it and looked into my face through a magnifying glass that he wore on a cord, and I stood and was looked at.

"You've been on nights," he said.

"Yes, sir. For four years."

"It shows about the eyes. It is the same with watchmakers; we lose the use of daylight." He let go my hand. "You are a nurse, and they have told you that I'm to be watched. Don't trouble to deny it. They think I don't know. I know a great deal that I'm not supposed to, and then I forget it, which is very convenient for them." He smiled. He had a smile of great sweetness, like a child's. "I shall try not to be a nuisance. I should be obliged if you would not lie to me. Everyone else does."

"I shan't lie to you, Mr Vane."

"No," he said. "I don't believe you will. You have that sort of mouth." And then, turning to the empty place on his right, which I had not understood until that moment, he said in exactly the same tone: "Margot, this is the young person. She says she will not tell me lies. We shall see how long she keeps it up."

There was a chair there, drawn back, and a place laid. He waited, with his head a little bent, as one waits for a reply. Then he nodded and sat down.

I looked round the table. Mrs Vane was looking at her plate. Her husband was looking at me.

He was a big dark man in his fifties, heavy, with a handsome ruined face and a glass in his hand. I had been conscious of him ever since I came into the room, as one is conscious of a fire. He looked at me across the candles while his father spoke to the empty chair, and he lifted one eyebrow, very slightly. It was not mockery. It was the look that one gives to a new member of the staff on a difficult ward, over the head of a patient. It said: yes, it is as bad as you think, and you will get used to it, and I am sorry.

I looked down. I felt the colour come into my face, and I was angry with myself. I had come to that house to be nobody. It was not in my plans to be seen, and by him least of all.

* * *

I sat up that night for the first time in the room they call the Admiral's, where Mr Vane sleeps.

It is a large room on the first floor at the front, with three windows looking east across the gravel to the stables. There is a great bed with curtains. There were seven clocks in it then. I had given him his draught at ten, the one spoonful in water, and he had drunk it down obediently and thanked me, and by half past he was asleep on his back with his long hands folded on the sheet, like a figure on a tomb.

I sat in a low chair by the fire with a shaded lamp and a book, which I did not read. It was very quiet, apart from the seven clocks. The wind had fallen. I had left one curtain a little drawn back, as I always do, since I cannot bear to be shut in a room at night without some sight of the sky. Through the gap I could see the top of the tower with its two figures, black against the stars.

I should explain why I do not sleep at night. Dr Imrie knows; he has had it all from me, and it took him four months. I shall put it here in a few lines, since it belongs with the rest, and then I need not come back to it.

I was a staff nurse at a hospital in Scarborough, on a women's medical ward. I had been on nights for the best part of four years, by my own wish. In the March of last year I had charge of the ward, alone, with one auxiliary. There was a patient in the end bed, a Mrs Aske, who was seventy-six and had been admitted with her chest. She was not thought to be in danger. She was to be looked at every half hour. At three in the morning I sat down at the desk to write up the charts. I had been awake for twenty-two hours, as I had been to my father's that day, and I put my head on my arm for a moment.

It was twenty past four when I lifted it. I went down the ward at once. Mrs Aske was lying half out of the bed. She had tried to reach the bell, and it had fallen, and she had tried to reach it on the floor. She had been dead, they told me afterwards, about three-quarters of an hour. Her hand was still stretched out.

There was an inquiry, and I was not dismissed. They said that the staffing had been at fault and that I had an unblemished record. They were very fair. I resigned on the day the finding was read to me, and I have not worked in a hospital since.

I do not think that I shall ever be able to describe to anyone what that hand was like. But I can say what it did. Since that night I have not been able to sleep in the dark. I lie down, and I begin to go, and at the edge of it I see the ward and the end bed, and I am awake with my heart going like a hammer. I can sleep in daylight, with the curtains open and the sounds of people about. So I looked for night work, of the private kind, where there would be only one patient and I could sit by him and never close my eyes. I answered an advertisement in *The Lady*. That is how I came to be in the Admiral's room at Compline on the first night of October, with an old man asleep and seven clocks going.

I thought it would suit me very well. I had only to stay awake and watch, and I had no fear of failing at that. I knew I should never again do the other thing.

* * *

He woke at a quarter past one. He woke all at once, with no confusion, and sat up and put his feet to the floor.

"Mr Vane," I said. "It's the middle of the night."

"I know what it is." He was feeling for his slippers. "It's a quarter past one. I must go across. There is the tower to wind; it's a thirty-hour movement, and I let it go yesterday."

"It can be wound in the morning."

"It cannot. It'll be down by four, and she will lose the hour." He stood up. He looked about him, with a troubled face. "Where is my wife?"

I had known that it would come. I had not thought that it would come on the first night.

I stood up too. I put my book down on the chair. I was a good deal frightened. I had a rule and I had promised it to him at the dinner table, in front of his family, and I could see, very clearly, the easy thing. *She has not come in yet, sir.* It would have cost me five words, and he would have nodded, and I could have led him back to bed.

"Mr Vane," I said. "Do you want me to tell you the truth?"

He looked at me. In the lamplight his eyes were extraordinarily pale.

"You said that you would."

"Your wife is dead. I'm very sorry. She died twenty years ago. She was drowned."

I waited for it to break on him. I have told people that kind of thing before, in the small hours, in my uniform, and I have held them afterwards. I was ready for him to cry out. I was ready for him to fall, and I had moved near enough to catch him.

He did none of those things. He stood quite still by the bed, in his nightshirt, and I saw his face change. It did not crumple. It settled. It was like watching a man who has been looking everywhere for something find that he has had it in his hand.

"Yes," he said. "On the Wade. In the Odd Hour. I had put the clocks back before dinner, to save a scramble, and so she had the wrong time for the tide." He said it over to himself, like a lesson. "I knew that. Thank you. I knew it, and then I didn't. It gets away from me. It goes round a corner, and I'm left standing with the feeling that there's something I have forgotten to do." He put a hand on the bedpost. "They tell me she is upstairs. I go up, and she is not. Then I think she's angry with me, and is hiding. That is a great deal worse, you know. It is much worse than her being dead."

"I should think it must be."

"You'll tell me again? When I ask?"

"Every time."

"I shall very likely be rude."

"I've been shouted at before, Mr Vane."

"I daresay you have." He looked at me for a moment longer. Then he reached out, as naturally as a child, and patted my cheek. "You're a good girl. You have had some great trouble; I don't know what, and I shan't ask. It has made you truthful. It takes people that way or it takes them the other." He drew himself up. "And now I must wind the tower, because if I do not she'll lose her hour, and I've lost her enough."

I could not stop him. I did not think that I had the right. I put his dressing gown on him, and his overcoat over it, and found his shoes, and I lit a lantern and went with him.

We went down through the sleeping house, and out at the side door, and across the gravel under the stars. He walked slowly, with his hand on my arm. He told me as we went that the tower clock had been made by his great-great-grandfather in 1790, and that the Jacks had been carved by a man from Ipswich who did ships' figureheads. That was why Jenny looked out to sea, he said. She had been meant for the bow of a ship, and the ship had never been built.

He climbed the ladder-stair in the harness room by himself. I went up behind him with the lantern, to be under him if he fell. At the top there is a chamber, eight or nine feet square, with the great iron frame of the clock in the middle and a pendulum as long as a man swinging through a slot in the floor. It was bitterly cold. There are louvres in the walls and the wind comes straight through. A door on one side opens onto the leads, where the figures stand.

He wound it with a crank handle as long as his arm. It took him a quarter of an hour, with rests. I offered to do it and he would not let me. I stood and held the light.

When it was done he leaned on the frame, breathing. He looked at the door to the leads.

"Would you care to see her?" he said. "You'll not have been so near."

I did not want to. I should like that to be written down. I had no wish in the world to open that door. But he had it open before I could speak, and the night came in, and I went and stood on the sill.

She was a yard from me. I had only seen her from the ground. I had not understood how large she was. She is nearly six feet, and broad, and carved from a single trunk, and the grain of the oak runs up through her like sinew. They had painted her gown grey, long ago, and the paint has crazed all over into fine lines like the skin on old hands. Her face was turned away from me, outward. Her arm was up.

I held the lantern a little higher. And I had the most distinct impression that she had only just turned her head. It was nothing that I saw. It was what one feels on coming into a room where the talk has stopped.

"She strikes the hours," said Mr Vane behind me. "And he the quarters. So she has a great deal of time to stand, between. I have often thought that it was hard on her. I asked my father when I was a boy what she thought about, all that while. He said she was counting. He said that Jenny was counting up all the hours she had struck, and that when she got to the end of the sum she would come down."

"What did he mean by that?"

"I have no idea. He was a silent man, and it was a great many words for him. I've never forgotten it." He was looking past me, at the grey figure, with great affection. "My wife is painted in a gown of that colour. I chose it. She laughed at me; she said I wanted her to match the clock. She was quite right. I did."

The works behind us gave a whirr and a clank. I stepped back, quickly, into the chamber. A rod moved in the frame. Out on the leads, with a creak of old iron, the small figure on the far side of the bell lifted his hammer and struck, twice, and the note went out over the yard.

It was half past one. The woman did not move. It was not her turn.

I got him back to his bed by two, and he slept till seven. I sat in the low chair by the fire. I had drawn the curtain right across this time, over the gap.

I have said that I thought the work would suit me. I thought so still. But I had learned something on that first night which I had not known when I walked across the Wade with my case. I had believed that I was coming to a place where no one knew me, and where I might watch and never be looked at.

I had been in the house nine hours. I had been seen three times: by an old man with a glass, by his son across a table, and by a thing on a tower that was made of wood.

In the morning, when Gail brought me a cup of tea, I asked her what was in the room at the end of the long gallery. I had passed the door on my way down. It was the only one on that floor that was shut.

"That's the Grey Room," she said. "That was hers. That's kept locked."

"Who has the key?"

"Miss Imogen has one, but she's in Scotland. And there's one somewhere of Mr Vane's, only he's lost it." She put the tea down. "You don't want to go in there, miss. Nobody does. Auntie says it's just as she left it, with her frocks in the cupboard and her brushes on the table. Her shoes are still by the bed. She'd took them off, see, that night. She went out to the Wade in her stockings."

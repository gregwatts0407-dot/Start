## 4. A Quarter Past One

I came up out of it the way you come up out of a tube station, a step at a time, with the noise getting louder.

First there was the smell of the rug. Then there was the clock overhead, cutting its bread. Then there was my own head, which felt as if somebody had opened it with a tin-opener and left the lid up. I lay and looked at a thing in front of my face without knowing what it was. It was round, with a crack across it, and two little sticks on it, one long and one short. I worked at it. It was my watch, on my wrist, about three inches from my nose. The long stick was on the nine and the short one was just short of the one.

A quarter to one.

I said it aloud. It came out like a man talking through a blanket. I'd been there thirty-five minutes on a floor, and nobody had come to see if I was dead.

I got onto my hands and knees and was sick on the boards. Then I got hold of the corn bin and stood up by stages. The lamp had gone over when I did. It was lying on its side with the glass smashed and the paraffin run out of it into the dust, and why the whole place hadn't gone up with me in it, I don't know. Somebody up there was saving me for something. There was a faint grey square in the wall that was the window, and by that I found my hat. I found the half-bottle, too, by treading on it. There was an inch left in the corner and I drank it, and it stayed down, more or less.

Beside the door I put my hand on what he'd hit me with. He'd dropped it as he ran. It was a cricket bat. A good one, old, with the rubber gone from the handle and the face brown with years of linseed oil. I held it in the grey light and felt along the edge and there was something sticky there that was me.

A cricket bat. In my time I've been threatened with a sawn-off, a bayonet, and an Alsatian. Twenty years I'd got through, and I'd been laid out in a harness room by a music student, with a thing they give to schoolboys. *You leave my father alone.* He'd thought I was from Sears. He'd watched his grandfather stand up at that table and say there was a man in the house finding things out, and he had looked round for the hard face among the guests and found it, and gone and got his bat. I couldn't even be angry with him. I'd have liked a son who'd do that for me. I'd have liked one who knew where I lived.

I felt the back of my head. There was a lump there like half a tennis ball, split, and my collar was stuck to my neck. I could stand. I could see, more or less, though things had a second edge on them, a ghost about a quarter of an inch to the left. I've had concussion before and I knew the drill. You don't drive. You don't sign anything. You don't trust what you think for the next twelve hours.

I'd like you to remember I said that.

* * *

I opened the door. The wind had gone round. It wasn't in my face any more as I looked across at the house; it was coming from behind the stables now, colder, and the rain had thinned to a kind of flying wet. There was more light. The cloud had torn in places and what there was of a moon was getting through the holes, on and off, so that the gravel went from black to grey to black again while you looked.

I stood in the doorway holding on to the frame. I was trying to think what I'd been doing. The spinney. The box. A quarter to two, the boat. I had an hour yet. I'd have given the two hundred a day for a chair.

That was when I saw the dress.

Somebody was coming along the front of the stable block from the direction of the house. Quick, light steps on the gravel. No lamp. I pulled back into the door. The moon came through for about three seconds, and I saw a woman, bare-armed, hugging herself against the cold, half running. And I saw the colour. Even by that light you couldn't miss it. Green.

She went by ten yards from me and didn't see me, and went in under the arch in the middle of the block. I heard a door scrape somewhere in there, and a voice call something, low, a name, and the door shut.

I thought: Celia. I thought it without thinking, the way you put a name to a tune. She'd come out to the stables, without a coat, in the middle of the night. To meet somebody. To look for her husband. To look for me.

I was going to go after her. I let go of the door frame to do it, and my eyes went past the arch and across the gravel to the house. I wanted to see how many windows were lit.

There weren't many. The hall. A glow upstairs somewhere. And at the near end on the ground floor, the garden end, where the little room was with the desk and the sofa, there were French windows, and they had a lamp behind them. In front of the lamp, at the glass, looking out, stood a woman in a green dress.

She had her arms up, her hands flat on the panes on either side of her head, as if she were holding the windows shut against the wind. She was eighty yards away. The lamp was behind her. But I saw the dress, and I saw the colour of it where the light came through the skirt.

I looked at the arch. I looked at the window.

I've been over this more times than I can tell you. I put my watch up to my face in the moonlight because it was all I could think of to do. I thought, well, at least I'll know when I went mad. It said five to one. And I am telling you, on anything you want to name, that at five to one that night I saw the green dress go in under the stable arch, and I saw it in the morning-room window, and that the two things were not a minute apart. I'd been drinking, but by then I was sober. I'd been sick, and I'd been out cold on a floor for half an hour, and that takes it out of a man better than coffee. I knew what I saw. Work that out. I couldn't.

I told you at the beginning there were two green dresses in the house. I'd stood in the hall with a whisky at seven o'clock and watched the pair of them. I knew it as well as I knew my own name. And it didn't come into my head. Not then, and not that week. That's what a knock on the skull does. You don't lose what you know. You lose the string that ties one thing to another. I stood there and thought, she's in two places. Then I thought, no, she went through the arch and round by the back and in at a garden door. She'd have had to do eighty yards in ten seconds, through a wall. And then I thought the thing that stuck, and it stayed with me the rest of the night and for a long time after.

I thought: I can't trust anything that woman shows me.

So you see, I was right, in a way.

* * *

I leaned on the wall of the tower, out of the wind, and tried to get a cigarette going. It took three tries. My hands weren't taking orders. The quarter-jack went off above me while I was at it, one o'clock less a quarter by nobody's time but his, and I didn't look up.

I'll tell you how long I stood there, because it matters. I smoked about half the cigarette. Call it five minutes. The window in the house went dark; somebody had moved the lamp, or blown it out. I never saw the dress come out of the stable arch again. But at what I afterwards made to be five past one, or a minute either side, I heard someone running.

It wasn't on the gravel. It was behind me and to the left, down the track that went between the stable wall and the trees. It came fast, and it was somebody in the wrong shoes for it, slipping. I put my head round the corner of the tower.

She came up out of the dark of that track like something thrown. Both hands had hold of her skirt, up above her knees, and her head was down. The moon was out. I saw the fair hair blown across her face, I saw her face under it, and I saw the dress, which was green, but I wasn't going by the dress any more, I'd finished with dresses. It was Celia Vane. She passed so near me that I got the scent, even in that wind. Her mouth was open and she was making a noise as she ran, not crying. It was more like the noise a child makes when it's winded.

She had something in her right hand, held against the bunched-up skirt. It was a small dark bottle.

She didn't see me. She ran straight across the gravel, lurching, and round the garden end of the house, and was gone.

I looked down the track, where she'd come from. The trees at the bottom were moving about against the sky. There wasn't any light in among them. No lamp.

I dropped the cigarette. I knew then, the way you know when the telephone goes at four in the morning. I didn't know what yet, but I knew what kind.

* * *

It's sixty or seventy yards from the foot of the tower to that mound in the spinney. I don't know how long it took me. I had to stop twice and hold on to things. I'd no lamp of my own any longer, only the moon, on and off, and the Ronson in my raincoat pocket, and I went in under the trees with my arm up in front of my face and the wet branches hitting it.

The mound was a shape. The arch in it was a darker one. I put out my hand for the iron gate that I'd left standing a few inches open an hour and a half before.

It was shut.

I pushed it. It gave about a quarter of an inch and stopped dead, with a little clink, the way a gate stops when the tongue of a spring lock has dropped into its keep. I shook it. It was a good gate. Whoever had built that ice house had meant the ice to stay his.

"Vane," I said. Then louder. "Vane!"

Nothing. The wind was going over in the tops of the trees. Under that, from in front of me, from inside the mound, was a small steady sound that I couldn't place for a second, and then I did. It was water, dripping, a long way down.

The wooden door at the inner end of the passage was still wide open, as I'd left it. I could just see the paler oblong of it, six or seven feet in from the bars, and beyond that nothing, black. I got out the Ronson. I had to turn my back to the wind and hunch over it to get a flame, and then I turned round with my hand cupped and put my arm through the bars as far as the shoulder and held the lighter out in front of me, and looked along my arm.

You don't get much from a lighter. A yellow leaf about an inch high, jumping. It showed me the brick of the passage and the edge of the landing. It showed me there was no crate where I'd left the crate. And past the landing, where the floor fell away, it got down just far enough into the dark of that round room to touch the bottom of the steps. Twelve feet off, perhaps. Fourteen.

There was a man lying there.

He was on his back on the straw, with his legs up the last three steps, higher than his head, the way no man lies who's got any say in it. He had a white shirt-front on and a black jacket. One arm was thrown out wide, and the hand lay palm up in the wet where the ice had been running, and the fingers were curled a little, and they weren't moving. There was a ring on the small one. I saw it catch.

His face was toward me, tilted back. It was Edmund Vane. His eyes were open. They weren't looking at me, or at the light, or at anything. The flame was in them, two little yellow points, and they didn't blink at it. And the side of his head that was toward the steps, the left side, above the eye, was dark and wet and it had no business to be that shape. It went in where it should have gone out.

I held the flame there until the lighter burned my thumb. I said his name three or four more times. I rattled the gate like a monkey. He didn't move. His chest didn't move, not that I could see, under the shirt-front, by that light. Nothing in that place moved but the flame and the water dripping.

I pulled my arm back through the bars and looked at my watch. I didn't decide to. It's what's left when everything else has gone: you note the time. The glass was cracked across but the hands were plain enough in the lighter. And I'll set it down here just as I'd have set it down in the pocketbook, for the coroner, in the days when I had one.

By my watch it was a quarter past one, and Edmund Vane was lying at the bottom of the ice-house steps with his eyes open and the left side of his head the wrong shape. I have seen what dead looks like. It looks like that.

* * *

I've been asked, and I've asked myself, why I didn't get in to him.

I couldn't. That's the answer, and it's a true one, as far as it goes. It was an iron gate on a spring lock and I had a penknife. There were two keys to that gate in the world, I learned afterwards. One of them was in the pocket of the man at the bottom of the steps. The other was on a ring on a nail in the cook's kitchen, and I didn't know that. If I had known, I'm not sure I could have walked that far and back with my head the way it was. I got down on my knees and tried the lock with the blade and broke the blade. I got my shoulder to the bars and all that did was start my scalp bleeding again. I found a bit of branch and tried to lever the thing off its hinges, and the branch snapped and I went down on my face in the leaf mould. I lay there a minute. I think I might have gone out again for part of it.

But that isn't all of the answer. There's another part, and I've had it out with myself enough times to say it.

I didn't try as hard as I would have for a living man. I'd looked along my arm at him for the best part of a minute, and I'd made up my mind. I had been a police officer for twenty years, eleven of them in plain clothes. I'd been to the canal and the railway line and the bedsit with the gas on, and to Georgiou in his cold store with the frost in his eyebrows. You get so you know it from the door. You don't need to take a pulse. There's a way they lie. There's something about the eyes; they go flat, like a fish on a slab. I'd never been wrong. Not once, and some of those I'd have given a month's pay to be wrong about.

I knew he was dead. So I got up off the ground and started thinking like a copper, and stopped thinking like a man who's found another man hurt. There's a difference. One of them runs for a doctor. The other one starts asking who.

And I knew that too. I'd seen her run.

* * *

She hadn't lit the lamp again. I came round the garden end of the house, keeping a hand on the wall, and the French windows were dark, and I thought she'd gone. Then I saw one of them was open a few inches, moving in the wind. I pushed it and went in.

The fire had burned down to red. That was all the light there was, and it was enough. She was sitting on the floor in front of it. Not on the sofa. She was on the hearthrug, with her back against the sofa's front and her legs out, like a doll somebody had put down. She'd lost a shoe. The green skirt was dark to the knee with wet, and there were dead leaves stuck to it.

On the rug by her hand a small brown bottle stood upright, with a cork in it. It was a medicine bottle, ribbed. It had a label that I couldn't read from there.

She looked up at me. I don't know what I looked like. There was blood down the side of my face and mud down my front, and I'd come in from the garden at twenty past one in the morning without knocking. She didn't scream. She didn't even seem surprised.

"You've been there," she said.

"I've been there."

"Is he—" She stopped. She wet her lips. "I couldn't get in. I hadn't the key. I called him, and he didn't—I could see his hand. That's all I could see. I had a candle in a jar and it blew out. I could just see his hand, on the floor, in the water. With his ring on it."

"He's dead, Mrs Vane."

I said it flat. I wanted to watch it land.

I've done that a good many times as well, told a woman her husband was dead and watched her. Most of them, you learn nothing from it. They cry, or they don't. But there's a kind where you can see it isn't news. They've known for an hour, or a day, and what you see in their face when you say the words is something more like a door closing. Up till then, they could hope they'd got it wrong.

That was her. She shut her eyes. She breathed in very slowly through her nose and let it out, and a shudder went down the whole length of her, and then she was still.

"Yes," she said.

I went over. I got down, which hurt, and picked up the bottle and held it to the fire. The label was the chemist's in Maldon. Under the chemist's name somebody had typed: *Mr L. Vane. The Mixture. One 5ml spoonful in water at night. Not to be repeated.* And under that was a line in red capitals that said CHLORAL HYDRATE and something about keeping it away from children.

It was a little under half full.

"How much was in it?" I said.

"It was new on Thursday. The nurse keeps it on a tray in the old man's room, with his spoon. He has one spoonful, and it knocks him out for six hours, and he's eighty-one and weighs nothing." She was talking with her eyes still shut. "I thought, Edmund's twice the size of him. And he's got a head like teak, for drink, he's been pickling it for twenty years. One spoonful wouldn't touch him. I didn't know how much. I didn't know. I thought, he's got to go out like a light. He's got to be flat on his back on that sofa by half past twelve, and stay there till the tide's gone, or it's no use." She opened her eyes. "I put in half the bottle."

"In his brandy. In the dining room. At midnight."

"You saw."

"I saw you give it him. I thought it was aspirin."

That nearly made her laugh. It came up, and it turned into something else on the way, and she put her hand flat across her mouth to keep it in.

I sat down on the edge of the sofa. I had to. The room was going round, slowly, like a gramophone record, with her in the middle.

"You'd better tell me," I said. "Not the one you told me before. The other one."

* * *

She told me. She told it to the fire, mostly, in that trained low voice. She kept turning her ring.

"There were two things in his pocket on Wednesday," she said. "While he was in the bath. I said I went through his pockets. There was a passport. His. A new one, he'd had the photograph done in Colchester, he looked like a wanted man. And there was an envelope from a travel agent's with a ticket in it. Harwich to the Hook of Holland, the day boat, tomorrow morning. Sunday. One ticket, Harry. One passenger and no car."

"He was going with the clock."

"He was going with the boat. I'd worked it out by Thursday night, after that man rang. They weren't sending someone to collect a parcel. They were sending someone to collect Edmund. He'd hand it over, and they'd take him off with it at the slack, round to Harwich, I suppose, or wherever these people do things. They'd give him whatever it was worth less what he owed. There'd have been a lot left. You could live a long time in Holland on a million pounds less a hundred and eighty thousand. Or wherever he was going on to. Without me."

"You don't know that."

"I've been married to him for twenty-six years, I know what one ticket means. He wasn't going to tell me. He was going to stand in the hall at one o'clock with his father's door open and all of us there, and smile, and then go out for a breath of air. And I'd have woken up tomorrow in this house with Leo, and a hundred copies of clocks, and the debts, and Kit. And a note, if I was lucky. He's quite good at notes."

The fire shifted. A bit of ash fell through.

"So it wasn't Sears's people you were frightened of," I said.

"Oh, I'm frightened of them. I'm not a fool. But no. That was for you. I had to give you something to make you stay close to him, and I didn't think you'd do it for a wife who was being left. Men don't. They think it's funny." She looked at me then. "I didn't want him hurt. That part was true. You can believe that or not. I wanted him *stopped*. I thought, if he's asleep, he can't go. If he misses the tide, they can't wait for him; a boat can't lie at the Hythe ten minutes past the slack, not in this. They'd have to go without him and without the clock. And then in the morning he'd wake up, and it would be daylight, and Sunday, and he'd have a head on him, and I could say—"

"What?"

"I don't know. Something. I hadn't got that far. I'd have had another day. I've been getting through it a day at a time for years. You'd be surprised how long you can do that."

"And I was the insurance. If the dope didn't take, I'd be on his heels."

"Yes."

"You might have told me the truth and asked me to sit on his chest."

"Would you have?"

I thought about it. "No."

"No." She pushed the wet hair back off her face with her wrist. "It didn't take. Not the way I thought. He drank about half of it and pulled a face and said the brandy was corked. Then Toby got in the way, and the vicar wandered off with a glass, and Edmund was just—he was perfectly all right. He sat with us. He was a bit slow. He was yawning. At half past eleven he got up and said he was going to see to the generator, and he walked out of the room quite straight. I sat there. I thought, I'll give him five minutes. I couldn't follow him straight out, Imogen was watching me, she watches everything. And then I couldn't find a lamp, and I couldn't find you. I looked for you. Where were you?"

"Lying down."

"I went all through the downstairs. I stood at that window for I don't know how long, trying to see a light, trying to get up the nerve to go out. I'm a coward about the dark. I always have been. In the end I took a candle in a jam jar off the kitchen sill and went. I knew where he'd have put it. He's used the ice house before, he thinks nobody knows." She stopped, and put her hands together between her knees. "The gate was shut. I held the jar up to the bars. I saw his hand. I said, 'Edmund, don't be an idiot, get up.' I actually said that. And then the candle went out, and I was standing in the dark with him down there, and he didn't answer. I knew. You do know. It's not like someone asleep. It's like a room with nobody in it."

She'd said what I'd felt, the same as I'd have said it. Maybe that was what did it.

"He'd gone down those steps to fetch his box," Celia Vane said. "With half a bottle of that stuff in him and a bottle of Scotch, in the dark, on that knee. He'd have got to the top, and it would have come over him all at once, the way it does with Leo. Leo's legs go. One minute he's standing and the next he's just folded up. And there isn't a rail on those steps. There never has been." She picked up the brown bottle off the rug and looked at it. "I did that. Nobody pushed him, you needn't look for anybody. I put half of this in a glass and I handed it to him and I smiled, and he said, 'Thank you, old girl.'"

She set the bottle down.

"I killed him," she said. I believed her. I still do.

* * *

I'd better say why. I've had a long time to pick at it, and I know how it looks to you. A man who's been made a fool of once doesn't like to think it was twice.

I believed her because it fitted, and in my trade that's all belief is. I'd seen her put the stuff in the glass. I'd seen her run from the place with the bottle in her fist and that noise coming out of her. I'd seen him at the bottom of twelve brick steps with his feet higher than his head. And everything in between she'd just given me, without my asking, and it joined up. The single ticket. The lie she'd told me in that room at eleven, and the reason for it. I know when somebody's making it up as they go. They watch you. She hadn't once looked to see how I was taking it.

There were things that didn't fit. I knew that at the time. The gate, for one. He'd gone in to fetch his box, and the gate had been shut and locked behind him, and a man doesn't shut himself in a hole he means to come out of with his arms full. And the box. The box wasn't on the landing. I'd held the flame to where it had been and there was only wet brick. I thought he might have had it in his arms when he went, and that it was down there with him in the dark, past where the lighter reached. I've said my head was bad. I had those two things, the gate and the box, and I knew they were wrong, and I couldn't make them stay still long enough to look at. It was like trying to read a number plate from a moving car.

And there's this. I wanted to believe her. If she'd killed him the way she said, then it was a stupid, pitiful thing done by a frightened woman who had wanted to keep a man, and not what I'd thought when she first came and leaned on that door. I'd pegged her as the other kind. I'd thought she was working me. Now here she was on a hearthrug with one shoe, telling me she'd done it, when all she had to do was keep quiet and let me find him in the morning with the rest of them. The other kind don't tell you.

She was shivering. She had been all along; I hadn't noticed. The French window was still open behind me and the room was as cold as the yard.

I got up and shut it. I took my raincoat off, wet as it was, and put it round her. She let me. She took hold of my hand, as I did it, in both of hers, and held on to it.

"What happens now?" she said. "You were a policeman. Tell me what happens."

"There's no telephone."

"No. It went at eight."

"And nobody gets off this island before half past five."

"Twenty-five to six."

"Then at twenty-five to six somebody drives to Thorney and knocks up the constable, and by eight there's a detective inspector from Chelmsford standing in the hall with wet feet. He has the gate off. He finds your husband. He has a doctor to him, and they take blood. Then he finds out how much is gone from a bottle that was new on Thursday." I heard myself saying it. It was like listening to a man in the next room. "They'll call it manslaughter. They might try for more, with the passport and the ticket; a jury doesn't like a wife who knew she was being left. Either way, you'll be a long time finding out."

"Kit," she said. That was all. Just the boy's name. Her hands tightened.

"And there's a boat coming in twenty minutes."

She'd forgotten the boat. I saw her remember.

"Oh God. They'll come up to the house. If he doesn't come, and the box doesn't, they'll come looking. Won't they? They've got a million pounds waiting, they won't just—"

"No," I said. "They won't just."

I thought about Ronnie Sears's man walking up the sea wall to Compline House, and what he'd do when he found the family gathered in the hall round an old man and an open door. I thought about the boy with his bat. I thought about the old man, at one o'clock, waiting to ask his son why.

Then I told you what I'd do. I mean I told her. I'm not going to dress it up. I've done some things I'm not proud of and I did them for worse reasons.

"Listen to me," I said. "He fell. That's all. He was drunk, and he'd been drunk all day, twelve people watched him put it away at dinner. He went out to see to the generator and he missed his way in the dark, and went into the ice house, God knows why a drunk does anything, and he fell down the steps. It's an accident. It's nearly true. It only wants a couple of things seeing to."

"The bottle."

"You'll wash that glass he drank out of, if it's still on the sideboard, and you'll put this back on the nurse's tray where you got it, and top it up from the tap to where it was. It won't stand up to a chemist. It doesn't have to if nobody's given a reason to look. A man who's been on Scotch since noon and falls on his head, they don't go hunting for chloral. They've no call to."

"And the boat?"

"The boat wants a box. If the box goes to the boat, the boat goes away. Sears gets his clock and your husband's debt dies with him, and nobody comes up to the house. Then in the morning there's nothing in that ice house for the inspector to wonder about, except a man who slipped. No crate. No story." I looked at her. "I need to get through that gate. I need to get the box out from under him and along to the Hythe by a quarter to two, and shut the gate again behind me. Is there another key?"

She stared at me.

"On Mrs Pask's ring," she said. "In the kitchen. On a nail by the range. It's the one with a bit of red wool on it."

"Can you get it without being seen?"

"Yes."

"Then get it."

She didn't move straight away. She was still holding my hand. She said, "Why are you doing this?"

There were a lot of answers to that. I gave her the one I could stand to hear myself say.

"Because I know who's sending that boat. And I sat in a room once and watched some people fit up a kid for a thing he hadn't meant, and I said nothing, and it cost me a year of my life to put right. I'm not going to stand in a hall and watch them do it to a woman."

It was a good answer. Some of it was true, too. She got up on her knees on the rug, and my raincoat slid off her shoulders, and she put her cold hand on the side of my face, the side without the blood on it, and looked at me in the firelight for a long moment as if she were trying to see what I was. Then she kissed me.

I'd like to say it was a calculated thing and that I knew it. I'd like to say it was a real one and that I knew that. What I knew was that she tasted of brandy and salt, and that it was the first time in six years. And I knew that her husband was lying a hundred and fifty yards away with his eyes open. So did she. We both of us knew it, the whole time, and neither of us stopped.

Some clock in the corner of the room struck one, a thin little silver sound. Half past twelve, it said. It was wrong, like everything else in that house. It was half past one.

"The key," I said. "Red wool. I'll be at the corner of the stables."

She went. She left the bottle on the rug, and I picked it up and put it in my pocket to give back to her, and forgot it. I found it there in my raincoat three days later, in Leigh, when I was looking for a match.
